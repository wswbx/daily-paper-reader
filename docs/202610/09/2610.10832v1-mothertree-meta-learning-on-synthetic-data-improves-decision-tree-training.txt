ABSTRACT 

Conventional decision tree algorithms produce effective, transparent models that can be audited, communicated, and deployed independently of the training data, but require learning every new task from scratch. In contrast, tabular founda-tion models demonstrate that meta-learning from a synthetic prior distribution en-ables strong in-context prediction for previously unseen tasks, especially in small-sample regimes. However, this approach does not produce a standalone model that can be inspected in isolation. We introduce MotherTree, a tabular transformer that meta-learns decision tree induction: given a training set for a new task, it outputs a hard, axis-aligned decision tree, equivalent in form to classically trained trees, in a single forward pass. MotherTree is pre-trained on a synthetic prior us-ing stochastic gradient descent without requiring reference trees for supervision. On established benchmarks with controlled sample size, the approach is compet-itive with size-matched trees from common algorithms: recursive partitioning, gradient-based tree learning, globally optimal trees, and distillation from tabular foundation models. Notably, MotherTree consistently improves over from-scratch gradient-based learning and acts as a strong initializer: task-specific tuning of the generated tree outperforms the corresponding from-scratch learner on all bench-marks and sample sizes. These results show that meta-learning can provide effec-tive inductive biases for learning stand-alone, small decision tree classifiers. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Small decision trees are critical components of prediction and decision systems (Rudin et al., 2022). They are one of few model classes that can be directly inspected, audited, and communicated (Rudin, 2019). Still, tree models are usually trained one by one without transfer between learning tasks, fail-ing to benefit from similar tasks being solved before. Tabular foundation models (TFM) (Hollmann et al., 2023) suggest a different possibility by demonstrating the utility of meta-learning for tabular prediction. By pre-training on numerous data sets from a synthetic prior, TFMs have proven remark-ably efficient at learning new tasks from small samples (Hollmann et al., 2025). Inspired, we ask: How can decision tree induction benefit from synthetic-prior meta-learning? Current tabular foundation models like TabICL (QU et al., 2026) and TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025) exploit in-context learning (Brown et al., 2020), using Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017) to map directly from a labeled training set to test set predictions. After pre-training on tasks from a syn-thetic prior, in-context TFMs perform the equivalent of both training and prediction for unseen tasks in a single forward pass. This strategy has shown tremendous potential in benchmarks (Erickson et al., 2026), but generalization challenges remain and classical learners, including tree-based mod-els, are still competitive in small samples and preferable in large-sample regimes (Holzm¨ uller et al., 2024; Purucker et al., 2026). Moreover, in-context learning does not produce a reusable stand-alone model for a specific task, creating obstacles for interpretation and model sharing. Decision tree algorithms that use recursive partitioning (Breiman et al., 1984; Quinlan, 1993), greed-ily applying hand-crafted splitting rules, remain the default choice for many (scikit-learn, 2026), but do not produce optimal trees and often overfit in small samples. Optimal tree algorithms (Bertsimas & Dunn, 2017; Aglin et al., 2020) perform especially well in small-model scenarios but are highly 1arXiv:2610.10832v1 [cs.LG] 7 Oct 2026 Preprint. computationally intensive and difficult to scale. Gradient-based tree learning (Yang et al., 2018; Marton et al., 2024) fits all splits and leaf values jointly but has no guarantee of optimality, and has yet to demonstrate generalizable superiority over greedy methods. All three classes of algorithms could benefit from improving either their sample efficiency or the optimization itself. First attempts have been made to meta-learn tree fitting algorithms (Zhuang et al., 2024), but rely on access to gold-standard reference trees for supervision. Scalable and efficient meta-learning of decision trees remains a largely open problem. We make the following contributions toward solving it: • We formulate decision tree induction as an amortized meta-learning problem and introduce MotherTree, a transformer model that generates standalone hard axis-aligned decision tree classifiers in a single forward pass. MotherTree uses a tree-structured decoder, trainable without reference trees, enabling large-scale pre-training on data sets from a diverse syn-thetic prior distribution, and generalization to real-world tasks with different feature sets. • We evaluate MotherTree on 64 benchmark classification tasks from TabArena and OpenML CC-18, in controlled small-sample regimes, comparing against greedy, optimal, gradient-based, and distillation-based tree learners. MotherTree consistently outperforms the corre-sponding from-scratch learner, GradTree (Marton et al., 2024), e.g., exceeding its AUC by 0.069 on TabArena and 0.077 on OpenML CC-18 for B = 100 training samples (Figure 2). • By fine-tuning the generated trees on task-specific data (MotherTree-Tune), we create ad-ditional performance gains that grow with the size of the training set, making the mod-els competitive with all four baseline classes of tree learners. Synthetic experiments on learning noiseless parity functions suggest that the utility of MotherTree lies not only in improved sample-efficiency but in improved initialization for local optimization. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Our goal is to amortize learning of hard, axis-aligned decision trees. Hard trees use discrete, non-differentiable rules and deterministic routing, and soft trees use continuous, differentiable rules with fuzzy or probabilistic routing; see surveys by Li et al. (2022); Costa & Pedreira (2023). Oblique 

trees (Costa & Pedreira, 2023; Mao et al., 2025; Norouzi et al., 2015) use linear combinations of feature values rather than axis-aligned splits formed by a single feature and threshold. Common learning strategies for hard trees include greedy recursive partitioning (Breiman et al., 1984; Quin-lan, 1993) and global discrete optimization (Bertsimas & Dunn, 2017; Hu et al., 2019; Aglin et al., 2020). Both hard and soft trees may be parameterized as neural networks and trained using stochas-tic gradient descent (Tanno et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022; Mao et al., 2025). Soft trees use fully differentiable parameterizations (Pace et al., 2022; Irsoy et al., 2012; Tanno et al., 2019) but require post-hoc rounding to form hard, axis-aligned trees. Moreover, gradient-descent strategies are sensitive to initialization for both hard and soft trees. What’s more, all three classes of algorithms above fit trees from scratch, failing to benefit from solving such tasks before. The goal of meta-learning is to fit a base model that can quickly learn a new task from a small sam-ple of task-specific data (Schmidhuber, 1992; Ravi & Larochelle, 2017; Finn et al., 2017). Moth-erNet (M¨ uller et al., 2025) applied hypernetworks (Ha et al., 2016) to meta-learning for tabular prediction, training a transformer to take a small tabular data set as input and output the parame-ters of a task-specific multi-layer perceptron (MLP). Mahfoud et al. (2025) considered tree learning as “amortized structure inference”, but not in the sense of meta-learning from pretraining tasks. Knauer et al. (2025) use a large language model to output a decision tree based purely on metadata, not on tabular samples. Distillation (Frosst & Hinton, 2017) from a tabular foundation model can also meta-learn trees. Our closest related work is MetaTree (Zhuang et al., 2024), a Transformer for generating tree classifiers, pre-trained with supervision from ground-truth reference trees. MetaTree is limited by the need to construct high-quality ground-truth trees for each pre-training task. In con-trast, MotherTree learns directly from instance labels, which proved preferable in our experiments.

Encoding for standalone inference. Like tabular foundation models, e.g., Hollmann et al. (2025); QU et al. (2026), based on prior-fitted networks (PFN) (M¨ uller et al., 2022), MotherTree is pre-trained on tasks from a synthetic prior, but differs fundamentally in how the model is used for in-ference. In-context PFN models jointly encode both training and test sets when making predictions, allowing test set embeddings to attend to the training set. In contrast, the MotherTree generator 

Mϕ processes only the training set, acting as a hypernetwork that directly instantiates a task-specific decision tree. Consequently, there is no test-to-training-set attention . Once generated, the tree is a stand-alone model; test-time prediction requires only tree traversal, but no access to the training set or the tree generator. This distinction changes the role of the data set encoder. MotherTree requires the encoder to summarize the training set for model generation rather than to measure similarities between training and test samples. 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate MotherTree in two tabular benchmarks and a synthetic task designed to illustrate the strengths and weaknesses of tree learning algorithms. We pay close attention to the comparison with GradTree as it uses the same tree parameterization but does not exploit meta-learning. Section 4.1 showcases MotherTree’s meta-learning capabilities in the low-sample regime, especially in binary classification, and demonstrates that MotherTree provides a strong initialization for task-specific tuning (MotherTree-Tune). Ablations demonstrate the utility of our ancestral decoder. In Section 4.2, we study the strength of MotherTree as a tree optimizer in a synthetic task with known solution. 

Baselines. We compare MotherTree to a suite of single-tree learning algorithms, including (i) Greedy and globally optimal trees: CART decision tree implemented in scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), Tsallis (Balcan & Sharma, 2024) and DL85 (Aglin et al., 2020); (ii) Gradient-based trees: RADDT (Mao et al., 2025), GradTree (Marton et al., 2024), DNDT (Yang et al., 2018); (iii) Meta tree learner: MetaTree (Zhuang et al., 2024), LLM-Tree (Knauer et al., 2025); (iv) Models distilled from TabPFN2.6 (Hollmann et al., 2025): CART ←TabPFN and GradTree ←TabPFN . Details regarding the distillation methods are stated in Appendix A.4.2. We use existing PyTorch implemen-tions where available and re-implement other gradient-based baselines in PyTorch. Hyperparameters are tuned using grid search. Details on baselines can be found in Appendix A.4.3. Since we aim to compare the learning properties of different algorithms, rather than hyperparameter tuning, we fix the topology of all trees to have the same maximum depth, D = 5 .

Implementation Details. MotherTree is pre-trained in two stages. In the first stage, we utilize the synthetic prior from TabICL (QU et al., 2025) with 400 k training steps. For the second stage, the TabICLv2 (QU et al., 2026) prior is used for training 125 k steps. The training batch size is set to 128 and 64, respectively. The maximum learning rate is set to be 3 × 10 −5 with LinearLR scheduler and cosine annealing scheduler for each stage. Training was conducted on 4 × NVIDIA GH200 120GB. See Appendix A.3 for details and ablations for pre-training and hyperparameters. 

Evaluation protocol. We evaluate all methods under a varying training set budget B ∈{100 , 200 , 400 , 800 , 1000 }, where B defines the maximum number of training examples available to the learner in a given task. For each of five random seeds, we shuffle the samples and construct a train-test split subject to this budget. Details for sampling, preprocessing, and missing value han-dling are shown in Appendix A.4.4 & A.4.5. When the data set size is insufficient to allocate B

training examples while retaining a test set, we use a balanced train–test split instead. Performance (uncertainty) is measured using the ROC-AUC with average (standard dev.) computed over the five splits and the data sets within each benchmark. We report the average AUC ranks in critical differ-ence diagrams, for which staistical significance is assessed using the Friedman test followed by the Nemenyi test, following the protocol in TabArena (Erickson et al., 2026). To evaluate the tree struc-ture of gradient-based tree learners, we measure the number of uninformative paths—root-to-leaf paths on which any two splitting rules contradict each other. 4.1 EVALUATION ON TABULAR BENCHMARKS 

We evaluate all methods on 64 unique tabular classification data sets from two standard bench-marks: (i) 30 data sets from the small version of OpenML-CC18 (Bischl et al., 2017) ( 17 binary, 

13 multiclass) as used in MotherNet (M¨ uller et al., 2025), and (ii) 38 data sets from the TabArena benchmark (Erickson et al., 2026) ( 30 binary, 8 multiclass). The benchmarks overlap on 4 tasks; when aggregating over both, we keep one of each duplicated task. See Appendix A.4 for details. 

MotherTree learners are competitive across tasks and sample budgets. Figure 2 illustrates the performance of all methods for different training set budgets. For the smallest budget, B =100 , MotherTree and MotherTree-Tune achieve the best results on both TabArena and OpenML-CC18. This demonstrates that meta-learning can benefit tree fitting both directly (MotherTree) and as an initialization for subsequent optimization (MotherTree-Tune). CART is a consistently strong baseline (Figure 3), outperforming both optimal and gradient-based tree baselines at larger training set sizes. Only MotherTree-Tune remains competitive in this regime. GradTree is the strongest gradient-based baseline, and benefits substantially from distillation, but is consistently outperformed by MotherTree-Tune (we expand on this below). Meta-learning with MotherTree outperforms from-scratch gradient-based learning. A close comparison with GradTree reveals the utility of meta-learning: Base MotherTree optimizes the same parameterization as GradTree using a single forward pass through the meta-learned generator, rather than from-scratch stochastic gradient descent. From Figure 2, MotherTree outperforms GradTree up to a budget of B = 800 samples on both benchmarks, with the methods performing comparably on OpenML-CC18 at 1000 samples. Table 1 separates results for binary and multiclass classification tasks at B = 200 samples. In binary tasks, MotherTree improves the mean AUC in binary tasks over GradTree from 0.635 to 0.692 on TabArena and from 0.725 to 0.766 on OpenML-CC18-Small. MotherTree also outperform other gradient-based baselines at this budget, improving over RADDT by 0.065 (0.056) AUC and over DNDT by 0.097 (0.116) points on TabArena and (OpenML-CC18), respectively. For multiclass tasks, MotherTree improves over GradTree on OpenML-CC18 but underperforms on TabArena, indicating less consistent gains on this benchmark. RADDT is best among gradient-based methods at multiclass tasks with B = 200 , but returns an oblique (not axis-aligned) tree . The gap between MotherTree and GradTree decreases as the training data size grows, suggesting that the meta-learned prior is most beneficial when task-specific observations are scarce; fitting a tree from scratch using gradient descent becomes increasingly competitive with more data. 

Meta-learning and task-specific optimization are complementary. MotherTree-Tune continues to optimize the parameters generated by MotherTree using the same procedure and hyperparameters as GradTree. Notably, MotherTree-Tune consistently outperforms GradTree and GradTree ←TabPFN 

across training set budgets (Figures 2 & 3), benchmarks, and label types (Table 1), demonstrating the value of MotherTree as an initializer for gradient-based learning. Task-specific tuning is often preferable over base MotherTree: At B = 200 , on binary TabArena tasks, MotherTree-Tune de-creases AUC slightly from 0.692 to 0.680, but improves it from 0.766 to 0.772 on OpenML-CC18. The benefit is more pronounced for multiclass tasks, where tuning improves MotherTree from 0.698 to 0.757 on TabArena and from 0.764 to 0.804 on OpenML-CC18, outperforming the more flexible parameterization of RADDT. These results suggest that meta-learning and task-specific optimiza-tion are complementary: MotherTree provides a task-dependent initialization that i) consistently outperforms from-scratch optimization, and ii) can be improved further by continued tuning. 

Parameter meta-learning is an effective mechanism for transferring prior knowledge to trees. 

Finally, we compare different mechanisms for transferring prior knowledge to decision trees. These methods differ substantially in how the prior is represented and transferred. Our distillation base-lines transfer the predictive knowledge of TabPFN2.6 separately on each downstream task through teacher-generated labels. MetaTree meta-learns from pre-fit reference trees, thereby transferring an explicit prior over tree structures, whereas LLM-Tree constructs trees from the semantic information associated with table columns. In contrast, MotherTree meta-learns the tree-generation procedure from synthetic tasks and conditions the resulting structural prior directly on the observed context. Figure 3 reveals a clear dependence on the amount of task-specific data. At a training budget of B = 100 , MotherTree-Tune achieves the best average rank across the joint benchmark, while MotherTree also ranks among the strongest methods. In contrast, distillation provides limited ben-efit in this regime: both CART ←TabPFN and GradTree ←TabPFN rank below their meta-learned counterparts. As the context budget increases to B = 1000 , the gap narrows substantially, and CART ←TabPFN becomes the highest-ranked method. Real-world benchmarks do not reveal whether performance gains arise from recovering more ap-propriate tree structures, since the optimal splits are unknown. To test whether meta-learned tree induction improves feature selection , we construct synthetic binary classification tasks using par-ity (XOR) functions corrupted with individually-informative distractors. Every distractor is infor-mative about the label, whereas every parity bit is informative only through its joint interaction. Thus, greedy splitting is non-optimal by design. Given k bits b, a noiseless label is defined as 

y = Lki=1 bi, where bi is drawn uniformly at random. We then form the input x by appending m

distractor features to b, each of which is a noisy copy of the label: distractor j equals y with prob-ability q and is flipped with 1 − q probability. We compare tree learners to the “ceiling” achievable by a distractor-only model. For details about the Parity tasks, see Appendix A.8.1. 

Higher-order parity is challenging for both gradient-based and greedy tree optimization. 

Figure 5a first shows that the difficulty of the task is not caused solely by distractor features. For larger parity orders ( k = 5 ), gradient-based tree methods already exhibit a gap to the DL85 oracle and greedy splitting (CART) when no distractors are present ( m = 0 ). This reflects the difficulty of optimizing the differentiable tree parameterization to fit higher-order interactions, rather than an intrinsic limitation of the tree structure itself or sample inefficiency (since the label is noiseless). We therefore focus on the additional degradation caused by distractors, rather than interpreting the absolute gap to the oracle as feature-selection error. 

Meta-learned initialization improves robustness under a tight depth budget. The effect of distractors becomes pronounced once λ := m − (D − k) > 0. In this regime, the depth budget is exhausted, so using a distractor consumes a split that is required to fully represent the parity func-tion. Most tree learners deteriorate sharply as λ increases. In contrast, MotherTree-Tune degrades more gradually and remains substantially more robust across the difficult configurations, indicating that the meta-learned initialization helps task-specific optimization resist locally predictive but struc-turally sub-optimal features. When does amortized tree learning help? 

In our experiments, MotherTree provides the largest gains when task-specific training sets are limited in size; the advantage over fitting the same tree parameterization from scratch de-creases as more data become available. This is consistent with the role of meta-learning as a task-conditioned prior: it provides a stronger inductive bias when observations are scarce, whereas task-specific optimization becomes in-creasingly competitive with more data. Cur-rently, the gains are strongest on binary classi-fication tasks, while the weaker multiclass per-formance suggests that direct generation also inherits limitations of the underlying differen-tiable tree parameterization. Importantly, the structural statistics from Figure 6 show that this prior affects the tree itself: MotherTree uses a larger fraction of leaves and produces fewer contradictory root-to-leaf paths than GradTree under the same depth and parameterization, which shows that the improvement from meta-learning also leads to a better tree structure. 

Limitations. MotherTree currently generates complete binary trees with a fixed topology and depth. This simplifies amortized generation, since the number and correspondence of tree parame-ters are known in advance, but prevents the model from adapting tree size or topology to individual tasks. As a result, some generated trees may allocate capacity to unnecessary nodes, while more complex tasks may require a larger tree than the predefined structure allows. Extending MotherTree to generate variable-size or pruned tree structures is an important direction for future work. 9Preprint. Our study demonstrates the value of meta-learning induction of single-tree classifiers. However, tree 

ensembles universally achieve stronger predictive performance by combining predictions of multiple complementary trees. Future research extending amortized tree generation to ensembles must tackle questions about how to encourage diversity and coordinate dependency structures across generated trees, to minimize redundancy and improve coverage of the target task. A.1 ARCHITECTURE DETAILS 

Encoder We keep the same encoder from MotherNet (M¨ uller et al., 2025), where each row of the table is embedded through a linear layer and one-hot encoding for class is used before sending to the transformer blocks. Unlike TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025) and TabICL (QU et al., 2026), which rely on dual attention between context and features, MotherNet encoder operates exclusively on the context data and does not require test points to participate in attention. Concretely, given a context set with n samples and d features, the encoder first applies a linear projection to map each row and corresponding label into a d′-dimensional feature embedding. Ad-dition is used to combine feature and label information, followed by 12 inter-sample attention layers to capture the mutual information between rows. This yields a row-wise representation for every context sample. To aggregate the n row representations into a single data set embedding, the en-coder performs class-wise mean pooling: it averages the row representations belonging to the same class, and concatenates the resulting k class means into Eϕenc (D). The resulting Eϕenc (D) is fixed-dimensional, which the tree decoder subsequently maps to the parameters of an axis-aligned decision tree.

A.3 TRAINING DETAILS 

MotherTree is pre-trained on both TabICL (QU et al., 2025) and TabICLv2 prior (QU et al., 2026). The main reason is that, for TabICL prior, it provides diverse activation functions and tree-based SCMs to model hierarchical interactions between variables, which helps the model to grasp the basic complicated interaction between variables. However, due to the instability of some activa-tion functions like Exponential function , it sometimes introduces the overflow error, which makes the data set quality less effective. While in TabICLv2, a more sophisticated prior generation 13 Preprint. process is utilized. Random Cauchy graph creates richer connectivity of graph, but the genera-tion process is much more expensive than the previous one. For the second stage of training, we gen-erate 120 k files on the disk. For each file, it contains 4 groups of graph configuration. A.4.2 DISTILLATION BASELINE 

Following MotherNet (M¨ uller et al., 2025), we also incorporate distillation baselines for compari-son. Distillation provides a natural way to transfer knowledge from a powerful teacher model (e.g., TabPFN) to a lightweight student model. However, unlike the original distillation framework (Hin-ton et al., 2015), which relies on gradient descent with soft labels, the non-differentiability of CART prevents direct application of this approach. We therefore adopt a simplified strategy: hard labels sampled from the predicted probability of the teacher model are mixed with the original training data, and the student model is trained on this augmented data set. For consistency and simplicity, we apply this strategy to both CART and GradTree. Since both MotherTree and these distilled baselines leverage knowledge from PFN(prior fitted network), any performance gap between them can be attributed primarily to architectural differences rather than the source of knowledge. We sample the label for each training data according to the predictions from the teacher model. Then, append the sample with new label to the training set, which doubles the original size of the training set. As for the teacher model, we experiment with both TabPFN2.6 and TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026). We report results from TabPFN2.6 in the main paper, as we observe no significant perfor-mance difference between the two teachers, while TabPFN2.6 is substantially more time-efficient. In the original implementation of GradTree, categorical features are processed according to their cardinality. Features with fewer than 10 unique categories are transformed using a One-Hot En-coder , whereas categorical features with higher cardinality are transformed using a Leave-One-Out (LOO) Encoder . Based on our empirical experiments, however, we use the Leave-One-Out Encoder for all categorical features , regardless of their cardinality. This modification provides a unified representation for categorical variables while avoiding the potentially substantial increase in dimensionality introduced by one-hot encoding. Missing values are handled after categorical encod-ing using median imputation. Specifically, the median of each transformed feature is computed from the training data and used to replace remaining missing values. Following categorical encoding and missing-value imputation, we retain the Quantile Transformer used in the original GradTree im-plementation and apply it to all transformed features, including both the original numerical features and the LOO-encoded categorical features.
