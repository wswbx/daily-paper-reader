## ABSTRACT 

Symbolic regression (SR) seeks explicit equations that explain observed data, and recent methods increasingly employ large language models (LLMs) and agents for equation discovery. However, these systems typically rely on manually de-signed workflows and prompts. We argue that the SR agent harness should it-self be an optimization target: its design shapes search priors and how numeri-cal feedback guides subsequent equation refinement. We therefore propose Har-nessSR , a framework for jointly optimizing prompts and workflows to construct reusable SR agents. During both harness optimization and agent execution, Har-nessSR supports either PySR or an LLM-based equation generator through tool-specific interfaces, allowing the framework to optimize search guidance for dif-ferent equation-discovery mechanisms. HarnessSR first optimizes role prompts through equation-discovery feedback in basic workflows, then jointly searches prompt assignments and workflows with brief adaptation to align the two. Fi-nally, it refines role instructions through reflection on residual patterns, candidate equation structures, and search outcomes, improving how these signals guide sub-sequent equation search. On LSR-Synth in LLM-SRBench, HarnessSR achieves a mean ID Acc 0.01 of 61.33% across three backbones, a 45.0% relative improve-ment over the next-best baseline. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Symbolic regression (SR) seeks explicit mathematical relationships that explain observed data (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025b; Karniadakis et al., 2021; Krenn et al., 2022; Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020; Udrescu et al., 2020). The resulting equations can be inspected, interpreted, and manipulated, and related to conservation laws, dimensional constraints, and domain theories. SR thus goes beyond numerical prediction to turn observations into testable scientific hypotheses, supporting equation discovery across physics, biology, materials science, and other domains. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and agents have introduced a new paradigm for SR. These methods combine the scientific knowledge and reasoning capabilities of LLMs with numerical search, evaluation, and feedback to iteratively propose, test, and revise equation hypothe-ses (Xia et al., 2025; Shojaee et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2026). Central to these systems is the solver harness : the executable layer of role instructions, tool interfaces, and control logic that governs interactions between the LLM, data, and numerical tools. The harness governs how scientific knowledge informs candidate proposals and how numerical feedback guides decisions to retain, revise, or replace them. These decisions shape whether subsequent computation explores alternative functional forms, addresses structural errors, or repeats attempts, thereby affecting the quality of equations recovered within a given budget. However, recent SR solver largely focus on designing solver harnesses by hand, rather than treating the solver harness itself as an object of systematic optimization. Recent research shows that, even

Solver harness optimization extends beyond adding new SR roles. Within a fixed workflow, different instructions and few-shot demonstrations can shift the model’s output distribution. Conversely, with SR roles and prompts held fixed, changes in workflow topology—including invocation frequency and parallelism—can alter information flow, computa-tional allocation, and final outcomes. These choices influence how data analysis informs equation search, how fitting and residual feedback guide candidate revision, and how finite computation is allocated between exploration and refinement. The SR agent harness is therefore not merely an execution scaffold, but a design variable to be optimized for equation discovery. This motivates us to develop a method for automatically optimizing the harness for reusable SR agent, aiming to improve both the quality and efficiency of equation discovery. To this end, we introduce HarnessSR , a framework for optimizing reusable SR solver harnesses. We formulate harness design as the joint optimization of role instructions, demonstrations, and workflow organization to obtain an SR agent harness capable of discovering higher-quality equations. To address the strong coupling among these components and tailor their behavior to SR, we adopt a three-stage procedure. First, we optimize role prompts within basic workflows to establish strong starting points for joint search. Second, we jointly optimize workflows and prompt assignments, with brief adaptation to accommodate their interdependence. Third, we use a reflection model to diagnose failures in SR execution traces and refine role instructions, enabling the harness to translate residual patterns, candidate equation structures, and search outcomes into targeted search guidance. This guidance is applied through tool-specific interfaces that translate agent-generated search plans into parameters and inputs for two selectable base SR tools (we integrates PySR and an LLM-based equation generator as agent tools). These interfaces allow agents to steer the chosen tool during both harness optimization and deployment, while keeping its underlying search algorithm fixed. The top-performing harness is then frozen and reused across new SR tasks, with model weights and the underlying search algorithms held fixed. Across three backbones on LSR-Synth, HarnessSR achieves a mean ID Acc 0.01 of 61.33%, a 45.0% relative improvement over the next-best baseline. Our main contributions are: (1) We highlight that the solver agent harness can also be regarded as an optimization target for SR task. Its design shapes the LLM’s reasoning process, influencing the priors that guide equation search, how search feedback is interpreted and used, and ultimately the quality of the discovered equations. (2) We propose HarnessSR, an SR agent optimization frame-work that jointly optimizes prompts and workflows and uses reflection on SR execution traces to adapt harness behavior to equation discovery. (3) We demonstrate a 45.0% relative improvement in mean ID Acc 0.01 over the next-best baseline across three backbones on LSR-Synth. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Symbolic Regression. Classical SR methods include genetic programming, which evolves ex-pressions through crossover, and mutation (Koza, 1994; Stephens, 2024; Pawlak & Krawiec, 2018; Moraglio et al., 2012; de Franca & Aldeia, 2021). PySR combines evolutionary search with ex-pression simplification and constant optimization (Cranmer, 2023). Neural-guided methods such as DSR learn a distribution over expressions using a recurrent neural network and guide candidate generation through risk-seeking policy gradients (Petersen et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025a; Li et al.; Meidani et al., 2023; Kamienny et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; Shojaee et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025). LLM-based methods leverage the generative capacity, prior knowledge, and reasoning capabilities of LLMs to guide equation search. LLM-SR and SGA couple LLM-generated structural hypotheses with numerical parameter optimization (Shojaee et al., 2025a; Ma et al., 2024). Beyond candidate generation, LaSR introduces a learned concept library to guide evolutionary search (Grayeli et al., 2024), while RESTART uses residual feedback to guide an LLM in revising candidate equation structures (Li & Pan, 2026). Deliberate Evolution guides LLM-based equation generation with adaptive search operators, structural diagnostics, and reflective memory (Pang et al., 2026). Agentic methods extend this paradigm through tool use and feedback-driven interaction. SR-Scientist autonomously invokes data-analysis and equation-evaluation tools to refine hypothe-ses (Xia et al., 2025). KeplerAgent extracts physical properties, including symmetries, to configure numerical SR engines Yang et al. (2026). A-SR introduces hierarchical coordination to adapt role selection, memory use, and feedback routing during search (Zhao et al., 2026). 2Preprint Despite increasingly autonomous execution, the overarching harness in representative SR systems remains largely hand-designed. For example, SR-Scientist selects tools autonomously, but does so within predefined tool interfaces, instruction templates, and outer-loop control rules. Such auton-omy adapts the search within a given harness without optimizing the harness itself. Yet these design choices govern how hypotheses are proposed and how evidence guides subsequent search, mak-ing them integral to the equation-discovery strategy. Therefore, we focus on optimizing the solver harness under a limited budget to obtain stronger, reusable SR solvers. 

Self-Evolving Agents. Self-evolution and automated agent design extend optimization from in-dividual outputs to the procedures that produce them. MIPRO optimizes instructions and demon-strations using end-to-end task feedback (Opsahl-Ong et al., 2024). MASS optimizes prompts for individual building blocks, selects how these blocks are composed, and then refines their prompts within the selected workflow (Zhou et al., 2026), while Meta-Harness searches executable code sur-rounding a fixed language model using execution feedback (Lee et al., 2026). However, existing methods have devoted limited attention to harness optimization for automated scientific discovery. ## 3 METHOD 

Unlike prior SR methods that manually design a new solver, we optimize the solver harness itself. For an SR agent, HarnessSR jointly searches role prompts, activation, parallelism, and iteration. To coordinate these coupled components for equation discovery, we adopt a three-stage procedure. The resulting top-performing SR agent is reusable and better coordinates scientific reasoning with numerical search under limited compute. 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Given an SR task q, typically comprising scientific context, variable descriptions, and observed data, an SR agent S seeks an explicit equation explaining the observations. With the base language model

We aim to optimize SR agents by optimizing their harnesses without changing the LLM weights. We define H = ( R, w, F), where R specifies the typical roles in SR agent and their prompts:

The instruction Ir specifies the role’s objectives, reasoning requirements, and action principles. The demonstration set Er illustrates the role’s task through in-put–output examples and may be empty. Joint search with TPE. We use the TPE (Bergstra et al., 2011) to optimize (w, z ): the five work-flow parameters in w and up to four active prompt-package indices in z. We first evaluate the 2n

initial harnesses on D2 and initialize TPE with their configurations and scores. At each iteration, TPE divides evaluated configurations into high-scoring candidates and the remainder, models their parameter distributions, and proposes a new (w, z ) favored by the high-scoring distribution relative to the other. The resulting solver is evaluated on the same panel, and its score updates the search history. This loop can change both workflow structure and prompt assignment across the full valid space. Therefore, during TPE search, the prompts obtained in Stage 1 remain fixed and are only reassigned across different workflows. Invalid or previously evaluated configurations are excluded. 

Brief prompt adaptation for top candidates. TPE can only search for better prompt–workflow combinations within a fixed space and cannot generate new prompts. Because prompts and work-flows are tightly coupled, Stage 1 prompts may become suboptimal for the workflows found in Stage 2 and bias their evaluation. We therefore briefly refine the each role’s instruction Ir of promising candidates before allocating the larger Stage 3 optimization budget. We select the best K candidate agents with different workflows based on joint search and fix their workflows and demonstrations. For every role of each top candidate, we ask an LLM to propose a small set of instruction variants informed by role input, output, and execution feedback. TPE then searches for combinations of these variants, evaluating each combination through complete solver executions on a small subset of D2. Finally, we retain the best instruction combination for each top candidate, effectively fine-tuning its prompts. 3.5 STAGE 3: SR-G UIDED INSTRUCTION REFINEMENT 

Brief adaptation improves prompt–workflow compatibility but cannot fully address persistent fail-ures in equation search. Stage 3 therefore refines the K Stage 2 candidates using SR-specific execu-tion feedback. For each candidate, we fix the workflow w, demonstrations Er , and shared compo-nents F, and optimize only the role instructions Ir to better adapt them to SR tasks. 

SR diagnostic feedback. Each workflow maintains a separate candidate pool Cw, initialized with its adapted harness from Stage 2. At each iteration, we uniformly sample a parent Hw from the pool and execute it on D3, recording every role’s input and output, search plans, candidate equations, and observational residuals. An external diagnostic LLM examines these traces and reports identified problems, supporting evi-dence, and affected roles. It checks and constructs four types of diagnostic feedback: (i) evidence-to-action alignment, checking whether residual patterns and diagnoses lead to concrete search-plan changes; (ii) structural exploration, examining whether branches and rounds explore distinct equa-tion forms and improve candidate quality; (iii) constraint justification, identifying variable, operator, or structural restrictions unsupported by available evidence; and (iv) expression quality, examining fit, complexity, and numerical validity to inform subsequent planning. This step diagnoses behavior without rewriting instructions or accessing reference equations, ensur-ing that the diagnostics are not tailored to specific training problems.

The reflection model (Agrawal et al., 2026) receives the active roles’ current instructions and the diagnostic feedback, proposes evidence-supported changes, and produces revised instructions for every roles of candidate workflow. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Data. Following SR-Scientist (Xia et al., 2025), we evaluate on LSR-Synth, the synthetic subset of LLM-SRBench (Shojaee et al., 2025b), comprising 129 tasks across physics, chemistry, biology, and materials science. Harness optimization uses the 1,024 public synthetic training tasks released by SR-Scientist. The effect of training-set size is examined in Appendix I. Dataset details are provided in Appendix C.1. 

Models and baselines. We use Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct, and GPT-OSS-20B. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B supports the main results and ablations; Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct supports the comparison with model-weight post-training; and GPT-OSS-20B tests applica-bility to another model family with fewer total parameters. The non-LLM baselines are GPLearn, E2E, NeSymReS, DSR, uDSR, and PySR. The LLM-based baselines are LaSR, LLM-SR, and SR-Scientist; SR-Scientist+RL is used for the post-training comparison. Model and search settings are given in Appendices C and E; baseline settings are in Appendix D. 

Metrics. Following LLM-SRBench (Shojaee et al., 2025b) and SR-Scientist (Xia et al., 2025), we use accuracy-to-tolerance, Acc τ . For each task, we discard the 5% of test points with the largest relative errors and check whether all remaining errors are at most τ . We report the proportion of tasks meeting this criterion at τ = 0 .01 and 0.001 , together with mean solving time per task. Evaluation and timing details are provided in Appendices C.2 and C.3. We measure optimization effort by the 7Preprint total number of solver executions. Each execution is one run of an SR solver on one task and may include multiple LLM and SR-tool calls; repeated runs on the same task are counted separately. Symbolic-equivalence accuracy (SA) and supplementary OOD results are reported in Appendices B and F, respectively. 4.2 MAIN RESULTS 

Table 3 shows that HarnessSR’s mean ID Acc 0.01 and Acc 0.001 across the three backbones are 45.0% and 63.8% higher than those of the next-best baseline, SR-Scientist, respectively. Mean solving time is reduced by 58.0–85.7% relative to the fastest LLM-based baseline in each model group. Joint prompt–workflow optimization and SR-guided instruction refinement help translate solving feedback into more effective equation search. These gains extend across scientific domains. ## 5 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATION 

We introduced HarnessSR, a framework that treats the SR agent harness itself as an optimization tar-get. It jointly optimizes role instructions, demonstrations, and workflow organization while keeping model weights and underlying search algorithms fixed. Results on LSR-Synth support this approach as a way to construct stronger, reusable SR agents. A central implication for SR is that numerical feedback can inform both equation refinement within a task and the development of search strategies reused across tasks. Through harness optimization, evidence from training tasks shapes how agents propose hypotheses, interpret residuals, and organize exploration and refinement on new problems. 9Preprint Prompts and workflows therefore encode consequential choices about how equation discovery pro-ceeds. This perspective motivates studying solver organization as an optimization dimension in SR, alongside equation representations and search algorithms. However, the present formulation searches within a predefined workflow space. Whether more open-ended workflow structures can reveal additional reusable strategies for equation discovery remains an important question for future research.

## D COMPARISON METHODS 

The comparison methods span evolutionary search, neural-symbolic generation, LLM-assisted search, and autonomous tool interaction. We describe the mechanisms and experimental config-urations of the ten baselines below. 

GPLearn. GPLearn evolves expression trees with genetic programming, improving fit across gen-erations through selection, crossover, and mutation (Stephens, 2024). The gplearn Symbolic Regressor uses 500 individuals, 200 generations, and tournament size 20. Its functions are addi-tion, subtraction, multiplication, division, sine, cosine, square root, logarithm, absolute value, nega-tion, and reciprocal. It provides a conventional evolutionary baseline without a pretrained model or LLM inference. 

E2E. E2E uses a pretrained Transformer to predict equation structure and constants directly from observations, followed by numerical refinement (Kamienny et al., 2022). We use the public 

facebookresearch/symbolicregression implementation, at most 200 observed points per task, and refinement of 10 candidate expression trees. It tests whether pretrained end-to-end structure prediction can recover these new synthetic equations. 

NeSymReS. NeSymReS encodes observed points, generates symbolic expressions, and fits con-stants in candidate structures (Biggio et al., 2021). The public NeuralSymbolicRegression ThatScales implementation receives 500 points per task and uses beam width 32. It represents a neural generation approach with a distinct candidate search procedure from E2E. 

DSR. DSR samples expressions symbol by symbol with a recurrent neural network and uses risk-seeking policy gradients to increase the probability of high-scoring expressions (Petersen et al., 2021). The deep-symbolic-optimization implementation samples up to 100,000 expres-sions per task, with batch size 512 and learning rate 0.0005. Expression length ranges from 1 to 9; 15 Preprint operators are addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, sine, cosine, exponential, and logarithm. Its task-specific learned search provides a direct reference for uDSR. 

uDSR. uDSR adds genetic programming and a polynomial poly operator to neural-guided ex-pression search (Landajuela et al., 2022). Neural search retains DSR’s sample budget, batch size, and learning rate. The genetic population is 100, run for 20 generations, with crossover and mutation probabilities of 0.5, tournament size 5, and maximum mutation-tree depth 3. Its operator set adds 

poly to DSR’s operators. This comparison shows the effect of combining search mechanisms. 

PySR. PySR searches equations through multiple evolutionary populations and maintains a trade-off between fit and expression complexity (Cranmer, 2023). The independent baseline runs one backend solve per task with all input variables and operators: 125 iterations, 550 cycles per it-eration, 15 populations of size 33, maximum expression size 40, and randomization weight 0.1. Binary operators are addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and power; unary operators are exponential, logarithm, square root, sine, and cosine. Its final equation selection rule is given in Ap-pendix E.1. This baseline uses the same class of SR backend as HarnessSR, making the contribution of role guidance and workflow coordination visible. 

LaSR. LaSR maintains an LLM-proposed natural-language concept library within evolutionary equation search and uses those concepts to generate and modify equations (Grayeli et al., 2024). We use LibraryAugmentedSymbolicRegression.jl : 25 iterations, 550 cycles each, 10 populations of size 33, maximum expression size 30, and the concept library enabled. The LLM operation weights for mutation, crossover, and random generation are all 0.001. Operators and nesting constraints match the independent PySR baseline. The response limit is 4,096 tokens, with up to 1,000 model requests per task. 

LLM-SR. LLM-SR represents equations as programs, uses scientific context and strong exist-ing candidates to propose new programs, and advances the search through execution and constant fitting (Shojaee et al., 2025a). We use the baseline configuration released with LLM-SRBench (Sho-jaee et al., 2025b), allowing at most 1,000 model calls per task. Each prompt samples four indepen-dent candidates, with one call counted per candidate. The method’s candidate selector determines the final equation and selects from candidates available at each call checkpoint in Figure 3. 

SR-Scientist. SR-Scientist uses an LLM to call data-analysis and equation-evaluation tools, re-vises hypotheses from observed feedback, and carries a cache of previous strong equations across iterations (Xia et al., 2025). We use the public inference framework, with at most 40 iterations and 25 interaction rounds per iteration; the cache provides the top three equations to the next iteration. Temperature is 0.7, top p=1.0 , the response limit is 8,192 tokens, and the initial mean absolute percentage error target is 0.1%. The evaluator fits constants with BFGS. Its public candidate selec-tion rule determines the final equation. Iterations, interaction rounds, and the cache govern different steps; Figure 3 uses actual call counts to describe its inference demand.

## E SYMBOLIC REGRESSION BACKENDS AND CANDIDATE SELECTION 

With continued advances in the field, symbolic regression (SR) has attracted increasing atten-tion (Chen et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023; Meidani et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2023b; Kamienny et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2025; Shojaee et al., 2025a). Beyond numerical prediction, SR transforms observations into testable scientific hypotheses, enabling equation discov-ery across physics, biology, materials science, and other domains (Yu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2023a; Hu et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; 2024). Among the diverse families of SR methods, we select two representative approaches—a GP-based solver and an LLM-based solver—as the base SR tools in HarnessSR. By covering fundamentally different search paradigms, this choice demonstrates that HarnessSR can integrate heterogeneous SR algorithms as underlying tools rather than being restricted to a specific class of solvers. We compare three SR backends: PySR, LLM equation generation, and a hybrid of LLM and PySR. For each backend, we independently run all three stages of harness optimization to search for its role prompts and solver workflow. Each resulting harness is then frozen for evaluation. E.1 PYSR B ACKEND 

The main experiments use the PySR backend, with the same base settings as the independent PySR baseline in Appendix D. Roles can specify subsets of input variables and operators, structural con-straints, and initial expressions; the adapter maps this guidance into PySR search settings. The independent PySR baseline runs one backend solve with the full variable and operator sets. The selector computes candidate relative root mean square error on observed points with nonzero targets. Let e∗ be the lowest error. It first retains candidates with error no greater than e∗ +max(0 .05 e∗, 10 −4) and then chooses the expression with the fewest nodes. Thus, when fits are close, the simpler expression is preferred. HarnessSR and the independent PySR baseline use this same final selection rule. E.2 LLM E QUATION -G ENERATION BACKEND 

For an LLM equation generator, the adapter includes search guidance and structural hypotheses in the equation-generation prompt and turns expression guesses into initial program skeletons with free parameters. The generator then creates candidates, fits constants, and evaluates them. This back-end draws on LLM-SR’s equation-program generation and constant fitting (Shojaee et al., 2025a), and on SR-Scientist’s use of execution feedback to improve candidates (Xia et al., 2025). Its input includes scientific context, variable descriptions, a summary of observed data, and the roles’ struc-tural hypotheses, analysis, and repair directions. The equation generator and solver roles use the same base model. Its role prompts and solver workflow are obtained through independent harness optimization with the LLM equation-generation backend. One backend call consists of two rounds of generation and evaluation. Following LLM-SR’s sam-pling design, each round independently samples eight responses, and each response may provide up to ten candidate programs; one backend call therefore makes at most sixteen generation requests. Candidate programs are parsed, checked for numerical validity, and deduplicated by equation skele-ton. Free constants are fitted with Levenberg–Marquardt nonlinear least squares from up to 32 starting points. Fitted candidates are scored on observed data; stronger equations and their scores are retained as generation experience. The second round uses the first round’s fitting feedback to generate and evaluate further candidates. Equations and evaluations from both rounds are returned to the Harness for subsequent diagnosis, repair, and final equation selection. E.3 HYBRID BACKEND 

The hybrid configuration independently searches for its own role prompts and solver workflow. For a given problem, it executes complete solves with the LLM equation-generation backend and the PySR backend, pools the candidates from both routes, and selects one final equation. Both the LLM-only and hybrid configurations evaluate candidates using the 95th percentile of relative error on observed points, together with expression complexity and numerical validity; simpler expressions are preferred when errors are close.
