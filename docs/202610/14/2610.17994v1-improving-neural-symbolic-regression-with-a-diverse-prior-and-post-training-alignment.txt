## ABSTRACT 

Symbolic regression is the problem of finding an expression that fits a dataset. Solv-ing it requires searching a combinatorial space of candidate expressions. Neural symbolic regression can amortize that search by training a model once on synthetic data and then predicting expressions for a new dataset. But existing models still underperform genetic programming on real, noisy data. We argue that the deficit lies in the training rather than in the paradigm, and present RASR (Reinforced Amortized Symbolic Regression), a method with two key changes to the training recipe. First, we design a synthetic prior that narrows the gap with real-world data: inputs come from the prior of a tabular foundation model; constants are sampled bottom-up, conditioned on the data; targets carry heteroscedastic and structured noise; and everything is standardized. Second, we split training into two stages. Supervised pre-training maximizes the token-level likelihood of a reference expression. This is followed by reinforcement-learning post-training that aligns the model with its inference-time use by rewarding held-out fit and penalizing expression size. On the black-box track of SRBench 2.0, RASR achieves the best mean test R2 rank of all evaluated methods. Three RASR variants, trained with different size penalties, make up most of the accuracy-complexity Pareto front. On Strogatz, our method recovers more ground-truth expressions than any baseline, and on Feynman it is competitive with the best-performing methods. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Symbolic regression is the task of finding a closed-form mathematical expression that fits a regression dataset. The classical use case is scientific discovery, where the expression itself is the object of interest (Schmidt & Lipson, 2009). Another is general tabular prediction, where the data need not follow an exact law and symbolic regression can still yield models that are interpretable yet expressive. Searching over expressions is difficult, since the space is discrete and grows combinatorially with expression size, and the problem is NP-hard in general (Virgolin & Pissis, 2022). For decades this search has been driven predominantly by genetic programming (Koza, 1994), which is still widely regarded as the state of the art, with PySR (Cranmer, 2023) and Operon (Burlacu et al., 2020) among its strongest modern representatives. A more recent line of work is neural symbolic regression, where a transformer is trained over many synthetic examples and then predicts expressions for a new dataset directly (Biggio et al., 2021; Kamienny et al., 2022), amortizing a search that classical methods must rerun for every problem. We use the term in this narrow sense of amortized methods, excluding neural methods that train anew on each dataset (Section 2). A similar recipe has reshaped the neighboring field of tabular prediction, where tabular foundation models such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025), pre-trained once on a synthetic prior, now match or surpass classical approaches (Erickson et al., 2025). Neural symbolic regression, by contrast, still trails the best search-based systems, particularly on black-box problems (Cava et al., 2021; Aldeia et al., 2025). We argue that this gap is not inherent to the paradigm but stems from how these models are trained.

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Symbolic regression is classically approached with genetic programming, which evolves a population of candidate expression trees through fitness-based selection and tree-level mutation and crossover operators (Koza, 1994), with PySR (Cranmer, 2023) and Operon (Burlacu et al., 2020) as widely used implementations. Our method belongs to neural symbolic regression, which trains a sequence model once on large amounts of synthetic dataset-expression pairs (Biggio et al., 2021; Kamienny et al., 2022; Saegert & Köthe, 2026). Other neural approaches train anew on each dataset, such as EQL (Sahoo et al., 2018), which fits a network with symbolic activation functions, and deep symbolic regression (DSR) (Petersen et al., 2021), which learns a generative policy over expressions from a fit-based reward. 

Four design choices. Nearly all neural symbolic regression methods follow one recipe, that is, train once on synthetic dataset-expression pairs, then encode a given dataset and decode expressions for it. They differ on four recurring choices. The first is the synthetic prior. Expressions are sampled with the tree-counting strategy of Lample & Charton (2020), while the accompanying datasets are built from simple input distributions without target noise. The second is the training objective, usually a single stage of token-level cross-entropy, for which richer multimodal alternatives have been proposed (Meidani et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023), including Sym-Q (Tian et al., 2025). The third is the treatment of numerical constants, which are either left as placeholders for a downstream optimizer or predicted in high precision. The fourth is how test-time compute buys candidates, through sampling (Kamienny et al., 2022), beam search (Biggio et al., 2021), a latent-space search (Meidani et al., 2024) or TPSR’s Monte-Carlo tree search around a frozen decoder (Shojaee et al., 2023). 

Positioning of RASR. RASR departs from the usual answer on each of the four choices. The first two carry our main contributions, the prior of Section 3.1 and the post-training stage of Section 3.3. Reinforcement learning has been applied to symbolic regression before, but per dataset (DSR), as a test-time search around a frozen decoder (TPSR), or as an offline objective folded into pre-training itself (Sym-Q), so post-training a general pre-trained model against a task reward is new to the field. On the remaining two choices our departures are smaller. We emit rounded constants that act as an initialization region for a downstream optimizer, and we sample candidates independently at test time instead of running a guided search, since post-training has already shifted the policy toward strong candidates. Appendix A treats each choice in full. 

## 3 METHOD 

In symbolic regression we are given a dataset D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 with inputs xi ∈ Rd and targets 

yi ∈ R, and seek a closed-form expression f that both fits the data and stays compact. At the core of RASR is a transformer mapping D to a distribution over such expressions. We describe its four ingredients in turn: the synthetic prior the model is trained on (Section 3.1), the architecture (Section 3.2), the two-stage training procedure (Section 3.3), and the inference pipeline that refines and selects among the proposed expressions (Section 3.4). 3.1 SYNTHETIC PRIOR 

Each pre-training example is a pair (D, e ), where e is a ground-truth expression used during the dataset’s generation, and the task is to recover e from D. The quality of the prior these pairs are drawn from largely determines how well the model transfers to real data, and is therefore a central design component of this work. Sampling proceeds in the stages of Figure 2, and full details and hyperparameters are given in Appendix B. 

Skeleton sampling. Expressions are built from three binary and fifteen unary operators over variables 

x1, . . . , x 11 , all listed in Appendix B.1. We sample the number of distinct variables nv uniformly from {1, . . . , 11 }, then draw a binary tree from the unbiased tree-counting distribution of Lample & Charton (2020) and fill its leaves with variables. Following E2E (Kamienny et al., 2022), the unary operators are inserted afterwards at uniformly drawn positions, which keeps the number of binary and unary operators under independent control. We then insert constant placeholders wherever a constant would meaningfully change the function, omitting those a later standardization or a parent constant would make redundant, and simplify the result with SimpliPy (Saegert & Köthe, 2026). Input sampling. Previous work samples the input matrix X from simple fixed distributions. We instead draw it from a tabular foundation model prior, so that the features and their relationships resemble those of real tabular data. Such priors sample a random structural causal model, a directed acyclic graph over variables with a random function at each node, and read a subset of the resulting variables off as columns (Hollmann et al., 2023). Because all columns arise from the same coupled mechanism, they can be nonlinearly related, correlated and redundant, as they are in real data. We use the prior of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026), drawing a pool of twelve standardized numerical columns: the first nv become the expression’s variables, one is held out as a noise node shaping input-correlated noise, and a random number of the rest are appended to D as correlated distractor features absent from f , so the model must learn to ignore irrelevant inputs. The number of observations n is sampled uniformly from 8 to 1024 . We use the prior essentially as published, with targeted modifications that make its samples better suited as continuous inputs (Appendix B.2). 

Bottom-up constant sampling. Filling the placeholders independently from a fixed distribution has two problems. It produces degenerate functions, for example arguments that saturate exp or fall outside the domain of log , and it does not ensure the expression is one a user would consider a reasonable solution given the dataset, as characteristic shapes may be missed entirely or one term may dominate another by orders of magnitude. We therefore sample constants conditioned on the data in a single bottom-up pass over the tree: each subtree is evaluated on X, and when the evaluation reaches an affine wrapper c0t + c1, the constants are drawn conditioned on the empirical distribution of the 4Preprint child values t, so that the operator receives inputs in a sensible regime by construction. Positive-domain operators keep their argument’s minimum above zero, and the terms of a sum are scaled jointly so that none vanishes or dominates (Appendix B.3). 

Noise and standardization. Real data is rarely clean, and a model trained only on clean targets may fail to generalize. We inject noise into most datasets while leaving 40% untouched, at a strength drawn per dataset from an exponential distribution in units of the clean target’s standard deviation, so most datasets are only mildly disturbed with a long tail of stronger corruption. Two forms are used equally often. The first is Gaussian, with a per-row standard deviation that blends a constant with a profile derived from the auxiliary noise node. Since that node’s relation to the observed features is inherited from the SCM prior, the noise spans homoscedastic through input-correlated heteroscedastic while staying zero-mean at each row, so the ground truth remains the conditional mean E[y | x] and hence the optimal predictor. The second form instead mimics unobserved confounders: the noise node is passed through a random activation and centered over the dataset, which adds structured deviations rather than white noise. Since it is zero-mean only marginally, and not conditionally on x, the ground-truth expression is here an expressible fit rather than the exact conditional mean (Appendix B.4). Finally, since real targets arrive at arbitrary scales, we standardize the target as 

ˆy = ( y − μy )/σ y and rewrite the expression to describe ˆy rather than y.

Tokenization and constant strategy. Expressions are serialized in prefix notation, where operators and variables map to single tokens, while numeric constants are emitted as digit tokens, a mantissa rounded to three significant figures with a single-digit exponent. We treat these values only as an initialization for the refinement of Section 3.4, so the model learns an approximate region of constant space rather than an exact value. 3.2 ARCHITECTURE 

The model is an encoder-decoder transformer that maps an input table to symbolic expressions. The encoder turns the table, whose first column holds the target, into one latent per row, and the decoder generates an expression as a prefix token sequence while cross-attending to those latents, so sampling it repeatedly yields a set of candidates. We deliberately keep the backbone close to a standard encoder-decoder transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) and apply only targeted changes. Every scalar cell is embedded through the raw IEEE-754 bits of its float32 value (Biggio et al., 2021), giving the model direct access to magnitude and sign, and the encoder alternates attention over the two axes of the table, following TabPFNv2 (Hollmann et al., 2025). Since sample-axis attention treats the rows as an unordered set, the encoder is permutation-equivariant over samples and accepts a variable number of rows, and each row’s target-column representation becomes its latent. The number of rows varies by two orders of magnitude across datasets, so along that axis we replace the usual fixed attention scale with the length-robust QASSMax (Qu et al., 2026). Further changes are gated attention (Qiu et al., 2025), and QK-norm (Henry et al., 2020) and pre-normalization with RMSNorm (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019) for training stability. Our main model has 94 M parameters, alongside a 24 M variant for ablations. Appendix C gives the full description and a diagram. 3.3 TRAINING 

The model is trained in the two stages of Figure 3, which serve distinct purposes. Pre-training is where scale happens: a cheap, massively parallel supervised objective over the synthetic pairs of Section 3.1, whose role is to train on as much data as the budget allows and leave a strong initialization. Post-training then aligns that initialization with how the model is actually used, replacing the surrogate objective with the quantities we measure at inference, and teacher forcing with the model’s own autoregressive samples. 

Pre-training. Given the encoded dataset, the decoder is trained to predict the ground-truth prefix expression one token at a time under a token-level cross-entropy objective, with each step conditioned on the true prefix rather than on the model’s own output (teacher forcing). Pairs are generated offline and streamed, so each is seen at most once. The 94 M model sees roughly 62 M datasets over 242 ksteps (Appendix D).

Data and variants. Post-training needs no ground-truth expression, since the reward depends only on the dataset and the predictions. We target both the black-box setting, where the data is not guaranteed to come from a closed-form expression, and the classical setting, where it is. Post-training therefore uses an equal mixture of freshly sampled datasets from our pre-training prior and datasets drawn 6Preprint directly from the modified TabICLv2 prior, whose underlying function need not be expressible in our primitive set. We sample 128 generations per dataset at temperature 1.0 and disable the KL penalty entirely. The weight λ has no single correct setting, since how much fit a user will give up for a smaller expression depends on the application, so we treat it as a property of the trained model and post-train three variants of the 94 M model from the same checkpoint, at λ ∈ { 0.05 , 0.15 , 0.5}

with λ = 0 .05 as the default. Applying the penalty during post-training rather than at selection time shapes the sampling distribution itself, so a strongly penalized variant searches within a compact region of expression space instead of filtering a pool of large candidates later. 3.4 INFERENCE 

At test time we encode D once and autoregressively sample K candidates, discarding those that are invalid, too long, or reference more variables than the dataset provides. Each remaining unique candi-date’s constants are refined by nonlinear least squares, using the Levenberg–Marquardt (Levenberg, 1944; Marquardt, 1963) solver in scipy (Virtanen et al., 2019) seeded from the emitted values with several Gaussian-perturbed restarts. Candidates are scored on fit, measured by R2, and size C(e),and exposed in two ways: whenever a user can make the trade-off themselves we return the Pareto front, the candidates not beaten on both axes at once, and when a single expression must be returned without a human in the loop, as in a benchmark evaluation, we select the candidate maximizing 

S(e) = R2(e) − γ C(e), (2) where the parsimony weight γ ≥ 0 states how much additional fit a candidate must deliver to justify one further node. Appendix F gives more details. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate RASR on SRBench 2.0 (Aldeia et al., 2025), the recent successor to the widely used SRBench suite (Cava et al., 2021), and on the Feynman and Strogatz tracks of the original SRBench. This section gives an overview of the main results. Additional results and the ablations are in the appendix, summarized in Section 4.4. 4.1 SETUP 

Benchmarks and protocol. The black-box track of SRBench 2.0 is our primary point of evaluation. Its twelve regression datasets come from the PMLB collection (Olson et al., 2017), mostly real-world problems without a known closed-form target, curated to be harder and more representative than in the original SRBench suite. The other tracks instead pair every problem with a known expression. We follow the respective evaluation protocols. RASR samples K = 1024 candidates per run, from a single configuration left unchanged across all datasets and tracks. The only quantity we tune is the scalar parsimony weight γ ∈ { 0, 0.01 , 0.1} of Equation (2), selected by cross-validation on the training split, which acts at selection time on an already generated candidate pool rather than steering the search. Since RASR was trained on at most 1024 rows and 11 features, we cap its input at 2048 

rows, twice the training maximum, and at 11 features, which reduces four of the twelve black-box datasets and subsamples the Feynman problems. Appendix G describes the datasets of all four tracks and Appendix H the remaining protocol details. 

Metrics, baselines and variants. We record each run’s test R2 and expression size, counted as the number of nodes after conversion to SymPy (Meurer et al., 2017), and aggregate a method’s runs on a dataset by the median. On the black-box track we summarize the per-dataset medians by two complementary track averages, the mean test R2 rank, which only depends on the ordering of methods, and the mean improvability (Erickson et al., 2025), (err i − err ⋆i )/err i with err i a method’s test RMSE on dataset i and err ⋆i the lowest of a fixed pool, which retains the magnitude of the gaps (Appendix H). As baselines we take the full set of methods evaluated by the benchmark that defines each track, covering genetic-programming systems, non-evolutionary search and neural methods. To all four tracks we add two pre-trained models that neither benchmark includes, Flash-ANSR (Saegert & Köthe, 2026) and SNIP (Meidani et al., 2024), run under the same protocol. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

Limitations. Several limitations remain in evaluation scope, method and measurement. Twelve black-box datasets leave track averages uncertain. The protocol fixes one expression per run while RASR returns a front, and the reported costs place GPU inference next to single-core CPU search. Constants at nearly every operator argument overparameterize refinement, and the bound of 2048 

rows and 11 features costs accuracy on four black-box datasets. Finally, expression size, our only interpretability measure, cannot judge what an expression means to a domain expert. 

Discussion. We see three implications. First, the prior is a primary design axis: at 24 M parameters, a model trained on an earlier-style prior ends at about twice our improvability (Appendix N). We release ours as a modular basis for further work. Second, post-training specializes a base model. Needing no ground-truth expression, it could equally adapt to real data of a target domain or to other expression preferences. Third, our results are unlikely to mark the limit of the approach: gains from 

24 M to 94 M parameters (Appendix M) are in line with early evidence of scaling laws (Otte et al., 2025), and larger training datasets may lift our bound on rows and features. Our results suggest that neural symbolic regression’s deficit against search-based methods on real, noisy data stems from training rather than from the paradigm. With a synthetic prior built to resemble real data and reinforcement-learning post-training, RASR attains the best mean test R2 rank on the black-box track of SRBench 2.0, with all three variants on the accuracy-complexity Pareto front, and the highest solution rate on Strogatz. 9Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

## APPENDIX 

## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

This section expands Section 2 on neural symbolic regression, the family our method belongs to, and closes with the reinforcement-learning line our post-training stage borrows from. It is organized around the same recurring design choices, and states our own position on each in place. 

Synthetic training data. Every pre-trained method stands on a synthetic data generator. For sampling the expressions, methods rely on the sampling strategy of Lample & Charton (2020), which draws expression trees uniformly given their operator counts. NeSymReS (Biggio et al., 2021) and Flash-ANSR (Saegert & Köthe, 2026) additionally simplify the sampled expressions before training on them. The generation of the accompanying datasets has received far less attention. Inputs are typically sampled i.i.d. from simple fixed distributions, a uniform one in NeSymReS and a mixture of Gaussians in E2E (Kamienny et al., 2022). Constants are sampled from fixed intervals chosen independently of the data, with rejection sampling cleaning up the frequent degenerate outcomes such as saturated exponentials and out-of-domain logarithms. The resulting tables bear little resemblance to real data, which is often noisy and contains correlated and redundant features. Since a pre-trained model can only be as good as its training data, we regard this as a large untapped source of improvement in the recipe. We therefore treat the data prior as a central design component rather than a fixed preliminary, and construct inputs, constants, and targets accordingly (Section 3.1). 

Pre-training objectives. The standard recipe minimizes plain token-level cross-entropy against a single reference expression, but a recent line of work argues for richer, multimodal objectives. SNIP (Meidani et al., 2024) uses a contrastive loss that aligns numeric-data embeddings with embeddings of the corresponding expression, and the model of Li et al. (2023) combines such a term with the usual cross-entropy objective. Sym-Q (Tian et al., 2025) frames decoding as sequential decision making, training with a contrastive loss plus an offline reinforcement-learning objective. We deliberately stay with plain cross-entropy, since these objectives mainly buy searchability, a role filled in our pipeline by the post-training stage of Section 3.3. 

Numerical constants. Existing methods split into two camps. Skeleton-based methods, among them NeSymReS, Flash-ANSR, SNIP, Sym-Q, and the model of Li et al. (2023), predict only placeholder tokens and delegate all numeric values to a downstream optimizer. This keeps the task purely symbolic, but it leaves the model no way to express a prior over plausible constants, which could help avoid poor local minima and overfitting on noisy data. End-to-end methods, namely E2E and SymFormer (Vastl et al., 2024), instead aim to predict constants directly in high precision, with an optional refinement step taking the predicted values as initialization. This places the full burden of numeric precision on a discrete autoregressive decoder, a task at which transformers are notoriously weak. A single mispredicted digit can leave the refinement stage starting far from any good solution. We take a middle position, emitting rounded constants (Section 3.1) that serve only as an initialization region for the refinement of Section 3.4, so the model steers the constant search without being trusted for precision. 

Test-time compute and selection. Although not always framed as such, the methods above all spend test-time compute on the same three ingredients: generating multiple candidate expressions, fitting or refining each candidate’s constants, and selecting a final one by training fit and complexity. Some run these once in sequence, others interleave generation and scoring. They differ in how many candidates they generate and in how they generate them. Flash-ANSR reports that recovery keeps improving as more expressions are sampled, whereas NeSymReS and E2E settle for a small fixed number. On the generation mechanism, NeSymReS, E2E, SymFormer, and Flash-ANSR use beam search or temperature sampling, SNIP searches the latent space its pre-training constructed, and TPSR (Shojaee et al., 2023) wraps a Monte-Carlo tree search around a pre-trained decoder, using fit-based rollout rewards to steer decoding. 15 Preprint We sample plainly with a small candidate budget (Section 3.4), because post-training has already shifted the policy toward strong candidates, and because an extensive search against a criterion computed on the observed data invites overfitting when that data is small or noisy. 

Deep symbolic regression. Reinforcement learning first entered symbolic regression as a per-dataset search. Deep symbolic regression (DSR) (Petersen et al., 2021) trains a policy network from scratch on each problem, optimizing a risk-seeking policy gradient that rewards the best sample in a batch rather than the average one, which reflects that only the single best expression is ultimately returned. We build on the same idea, but apply it a single time, as a post-training stage on our general pre-trained model, so that the search cost is amortized across all future datasets instead of being repaid on each one. Our objective keeps the risk-seeking motivation of DSR and realizes it within a group-relative policy optimization scheme (Appendix E), which matches the best-of-n metric that our sampling-based inference actually targets.

Table B.1 lists the structural ranges of the skeleton sampler and the categorical operator weights, which are normalized separately within the binary and unary sets. Given the sampled number of binary operators, the binary tree is drawn uniformly over all trees of that size with the counting procedure of Lample & Charton (2020). B.2 INPUT SAMPLING AND TAB ICL V2 M ODIFICATIONS 

Prior neural symbolic regression methods draw X from simple fixed distributions, such as a uniform distribution (Biggio et al., 2021) or a mixture of Gaussians (Kamienny et al., 2022). We instead use the TabICLv2 regression prior with numerical features only, from which we draw a pool of twelve columns, each standardized to zero mean and unit variance. The first nv columns become the variables and the last one the noise node (Appendix B.4). The number of distractor columns is drawn from a half-normal with scale 1.5, rounded, and capped by the number of remaining columns. After the target has been computed, all observed feature columns, variables and distractors alike, are randomly permuted and the variables of the expression renamed accordingly, so that a column’s position carries no information about whether the expression uses it. We use the TabICLv2 prior essentially as published, with the targeted changes summarized in Table B.2, all of which serve to make its columns suitable as the continuous support of a symbolic target. First, we bias the mixture of feature-generating function types towards smooth ones, giving equal weight to multilayer-perceptron, linear, quadratic, Gaussian-process, and product functions while down-weighting the piecewise-constant tree and discretization functions to a single component each. Second, for the same reason, we remove the discontinuous, step-like activations from the prior’s activation pool. Third, where discrete functions do occur, we raise their minimum granularity, so that the resulting columns still have enough distinct levels to act as continuous regressors. Finally, we add a per-column diversity filter that rejects a sampled dataset if any feature column has fewer than eight distinct value clusters or a single value cluster covering more than a third of the rows. Together these changes steer the prior away from degenerate, low-cardinality inputs while leaving the bulk of it untouched. B.3 BOTTOM -U P CONSTANT SAMPLING

## C ARCHITECTURE 

This section expands Section 3.2 and specifies every component of the model. Figure C.1 gives an overview, and Table C.1 the two configurations we train. 

Table input and cell encoding. The input is a table of shape (n, d +1) whose first column holds the target and whose remaining columns hold the d features. Within a batch, tables are padded to a common shape and a boolean mask records the valid entries. Every scalar cell is encoded through its raw IEEE-754 representation: the 32 bits of the float32 value are mapped to a {− 1, +1 }32 

vector, which a linear layer projects to the model dimension (Biggio et al., 2021). This gives the model direct access to the magnitude and sign of every value. A standard sinusoidal positional embedding (Vaswani et al., 2017) over the column index is added so that the encoder can distinguish the target column from the features and the features from one another. 

Alternating axial-attention encoder. Following the encoder design of TabPFNv2 (Hollmann et al., 2025), each block applies attention along the feature axis, letting every cell attend to the other columns in its row, and along the sample axis, letting every cell attend to the same column in other rows, followed by a single position-wise feed-forward network. This factorization avoids full attention over all n(d+1) cells while keeping the model expressive. Sample-axis attention treats the rows as an unordered set, so the encoder is permutation-equivariant over samples and accepts a variable number of rows n. The column positional embedding breaks this symmetry along the feature axis. After the final block, the representation at the target column of each row is taken as that row’s summary, yielding the n per-sample latents the decoder attends to. 

Decoder. The decoder is a stack of standard transformer decoder blocks. Each block first cross-attends to the n per-sample latents emitted by the encoder, then applies causal self-attention with rotary position embeddings (RoPE) (Su et al., 2021) and finally a position-wise feed-forward network. Token embeddings use a learned table with additive sinusoidal absolute positions, and the embedding matrix is tied to the output projection (Press & Wolf, 2017). 20 Preprint

## F INFERENCE DETAILS 

This section adds the details of the inference pipeline of Section 3.4. When a candidate has more free constants than data points, which can happen for very small datasets, the least-squares fit is underdetermined and lm (MINPACK’s lmdif ) errors, so we fall back to the trust-region solver trf 

rather than discard the candidate. Refinement runs independently per candidate and in parallel, and the post-training reward reuses it, fitting on a training split before scoring on held-out rows. Restarts perturb the seed by additive zero-mean Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of 0.5. Candidates are sampled at temperature 1.0 with top-p 0.95 , unchanged in every experiment we report. In general, and especially in our heavily parameterized setting, constant refinement can be prone to overfitting. Starting in a predicted constant region reduces that risk without removing it.

## H EVALUATION PROTOCOL DETAILS 

Runs, tuning and compute. We follow the evaluation protocol of the respective benchmark. Every experiment is repeated over a fixed set of distinct seeds, where each seed determines both the 75 /25 

train/test split and any stochasticity in the method. These are the 30 seeds of SRBench 2.0 on its black-box and first-principles tracks and the 10 seeds of the original SRBench on Feynman and Strogatz. Within a run, hyperparameters are selected by 3-fold cross-validation on the training split. The selected configuration is then refit on the full training split and evaluated once on the held-out test split. For RASR the only tuned hyperparameter is the parsimony weight γ of Equation (2). We also follow the benchmark’s compute constraint of a single CPU core, here one core of an Intel Xeon Platinum 8562Y+, with the neural methods additionally using one L40S GPU for inference. 

Metrics. The mean test R2 rank depends only on the ordering of methods, so every dataset contributes equally regardless of how large the gaps on it are. Mean improvability (Erickson et al., 2025) is the complementary view, computed from the RMSE as (err i − err ⋆i )/err i per dataset, with 

err ⋆i the lowest error over a fixed pool, so 0% marks the pool-best method and the magnitude of the gaps is retained. On the first-principles track we score methods by their per-problem accuracy-complexity Pareto front rather than by a track-level aggregate, and clip test R2 at 0 as the benchmark does. The clipping matters for Hubble and Tully-Fisher, where the accepted expression’s own raw test R2 is negative. 

Input size handling. Before fitting we bring each dataset into a range the model can handle. Rows are capped at 2048 , double the maximum of 1024 the model was trained on, subsampling for fitting while still evaluating on the full test split. When subsampling is necessary, the rows left over form a validation split on which the selection criterion of Equation (2) is computed, replacing training 

R2. Features are capped at 11 , the maximum the model accepts: we first drop near-constant features 24 Preprint with a variance threshold and, if more than 11 remain, keep those most correlated with the target in absolute Pearson correlation. The row cap applies to two black-box datasets and the feature cap to two further ones, so four of the twelve are reduced in total. On Feynman, whose problems all come with 100 ,000 rows, the row cap applies throughout, while Strogatz and the first-principles track stay within both bounds. Flash-ANSR is subject to the same handling, with the feature cap raised to the 

17 features it supports; SNIP reduces oversized datasets internally. 

Aggregation. We deliberately avoid the max-over-seeds aggregation the benchmark uses for some of its plots, which reports an optimistic best case and rewards high-variance methods and easier splits rather than typical behavior. We likewise avoid summarizing results through the area under the performance profile curve that the benchmark reports over both seeds and datasets: a performance profile is informative precisely because it shows the whole distribution of outcomes across thresholds, and collapsing it to a single scalar discards what the plot was meant to convey. Appendix O nonetheless reports the black-box track under the benchmark’s own post-processing, so that our runs can be read next to its published numbers. Improvability is defined relative to a pool of compared methods, and we use two. The Pareto plots against the baselines use all baselines together with the three RASR variants they show. The ablations and the remaining studies of our model share a single pool of all baselines together with every RASR variant appearing in any of them, fixed once rather than recomputed per study, so that their values are directly comparable. 

Baseline configurations. For the two pre-trained models not part of SRBench 2.0 we stay as close to the published default configuration as a comparable compute budget allows. For Flash-ANSR we use the 120 M-parameter model and sample K = 16 ,384 candidates, which places it in the upper region of the low test-time-compute regime its authors propose, and tune the parsimony weight over 

γ ∈ { 0, 0.05 , 0.1}, which includes their default of 0.05 . For SNIP we keep the default configuration but limit its latent-space search to a population of 15 over at most 10 iterations, again for a comparable budget, and run no hyperparameter search, following the authors’ own SRBench submission. 

## I HANDLING FAILED AND INVALID RUNS 

Not every run returns a usable expression. A run can terminate with an error, exhaust its time budget without converging, or return an expression that evaluates to NaN on the test set. In the extreme, every seed of a method can fail on a dataset, as happens for PySR on USCrime. The benchmark prescribes no consistent handling for these cases. We impute a failed run conservatively, setting its test R2 to 0 and its expression size to the median size over the method’s successful runs on that dataset. Where a method fails on every seed, no such median exists, so the dataset enters the R2

aggregation with a value of 0 and is left out of the expression-size aggregation. This is imperfect, but the most neutral rule we found, and it keeps a method that silently fails from being scored as if it had been absent.
