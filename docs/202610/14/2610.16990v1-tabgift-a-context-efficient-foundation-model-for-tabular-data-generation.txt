ABSTRACT 

Tabular data generation aims to synthesize realistic samples from limited obser-vations while faithfully capturing the underlying data distribution. Tabular foun-dation models (TFMs), pretrained on diverse tabular tasks and adapted through in-context learning, have demonstrated strong predictive capabilities, suggesting the potential of extending this paradigm to tabular data generation. However, the strong predictive performance of TFMs often relies on sufficiently large context sets, whereas tabular generation is typically needed precisely when target data are scarce. Extending the foundation-model paradigm to generation therefore re-quires reliable distribution inference from only a limited context. To this end, we propose T AB GIFT , a context-efficient tabular foundation model enabling both efficient distribution modeling and effective generation from only a few context samples. T AB GIFT maps heterogeneous tables into a shared latent space with a pretrained tabular encoder, where an in-context diffusion model learns to denoise latent samples conditioned on a small set of clean examples. This design transfers generative priors across datasets and enables distribution inference from limited context, while a lightweight dataset-specific MLP decoder maps generated repre-sentations back to the original data space. Experiments on 7 classification and 11 regression datasets show that T AB GIFT consistently outperforms representative baselines in synthetic data quality, with particularly strong gains under limited-context settings, while achieving substantially faster adaptation and generation. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Tabular data are ubiquitous in real-world applications such as finance (Zheng et al., 2020; Clements et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023), healthcare (Gong et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021), and scientific discovery (Du et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2023). However, high-quality tabular data are often limited by privacy constraints, restricted accessibility, and costly collection or anno-tation. Tabular data generation (Mehrabi et al., 2021; Dastin, 2022) therefore provides an important means to alleviate data scarcity by synthesizing realistic samples that preserve the underlying data distribution while reducing reliance on direct access to sensitive or limited real-world data. Existing tabular generation methods are mainly non-parametric (Chawla et al., 2002; He et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2018) or parametric (Xu et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). The former depend on observed samples for resampling or interpolation, while the latter require dataset-specific training and tuning. These limitations motivate tabular foundation models, which aim to amortize learning across datasets and adapt to new tables with minimal target-specific optimization. Tabular foundation models (Hollmann et al., 2025; J¨ ager et al., 2026; Grinsztajn et al., 2026; 2025) provide a promising solution to these limitations. By pretraining on diverse tabular tasks and adapt-ing through in-context learning, they can achieve strong predictive performance on unseen datasets without dataset-specific retraining or hyperparameter tuning. Their ability to rapidly transfer across datasets naturally suggests a promising direction for tabular generation, leading to a direct question: 

can tabular foundation models also be extended to tabular data generation?

However, extending TFMs from prediction to generation introduces a distinct challenge in context efficiency. Unlike prediction, which only requires task-specific outputs conditioned on observed fea-tures, generation must infer the joint distribution of all variables from the provided context, making reliable generation more demanding under limited observations. Yet relying on large context sets is fundamentally at odds with the data-scarce settings (Wang et al., 2026b; Park & Ko, 2026; Lin et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026) where synthetic data are most valuable. Meanwhile, accurately capturing dependencies within the context often requires fine-grained interactions among features and sam-ples, whose computational cost grows rapidly with context length and table dimensionality (dpg, 2025). Therefore, an effective generative TFM must not only infer target distributions from a small context, but also model that context efficiently. To address these challenges, we propose T AB GIFT , a context-efficient foundation model for tabular data generation. TAB GIFT builds a shared latent space to unify heterogeneous tables, performs in-context generation in this space to infer target distributions from limited observations, and uses lightweight dataset-specific decoding for efficient adaptation. Together, these designs enable high-quality generation from short contexts with low computational overhead. The generation pipeline is illustrated in Fig 1. Specifically, T AB GIFT first employs a pretrained tabular encoder to map rows from heterogeneous tables into a shared latent space, enabling a unified generative model across datasets with different schemas. In this space, we develop an in-context diffusion model that conditions noisy latent samples on a small set of clean context examples and performs denoising through efficient row-level interactions. This formulation allows the diffusion model to reuse generative knowledge across datasets while adapting the generated distribution to the provided context. Finally, a lightweight dataset-specific MLP decoder maps the generated latent rep-resentations back to the data space, requiring only minimal target-specific adaptation. Experiments on 7 classification and 11 regression datasets show that T AB GIFT consistently outperforms repre-sentative baselines in synthetic data quality, with particularly strong gains under limited-context settings and substantially improved generation efficiency. Our contributions are threefold: 2Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 • We highlight the importance of context efficiency in foundation models for tabular generation, showing that long-context dependence fundamentally conflicts with the data-scarce settings where synthetic data generation is most valuable. • We propose T AB GIFT , a context-efficient tabular foundation model that combines transferable distributional priors, efficient row-level modeling, and lightweight dataset-specific decoding for fast generation from limited context. • We conduct extensive experiments across 18 classification and regression datasets, demonstrating that T AB GIFT consistently improves synthetic data quality under limited-context settings while achieving substantially higher generation efficiency. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 TABULAR GENERATION MODELS 

Early work on tabular data generation primarily focused on improving downstream predictors rather than learning a high-fidelity data generator. Representative methods such as SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002) and ADASYN (He et al., 2008) address class imbalance by synthesizing minority samples, but they do not aim to match the full joint distribution of the original table. A subsequent line of work instead fits expressive generative models to observed samples. These methods can be broadly grouped into two categories. The first adapts image-inspired generators to tables, including GAN-based models such as ADSGAN (Yoon et al., 2020) and CTGAN (Xu et al., 2019), VAE-based approaches such as TVAE (Xu et al., 2019), and diffusion-based generators such as TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., 2023), TabDiff (Shi et al., 2024), and TabSyn (Zhang et al., 2024). The second develops table-specific families: Bayesian networks (Ankan & Textor, 2024) factorize the joint distribution along a directed acyclic graph, normalizing flows (Durkan et al., 2019) provide tractable likelihoods and efficient sampling, and tree-based alternatives such as Adversarial Random Forests (Watson et al., 2023) offer non-parametric density estimation and capture feature interactions. While these approaches can model complex distributions, they are typically trained per dataset and often degrade when training data are scarce. 2.2 TABULAR FOUNDATION MODELS 

Tabular foundation models have evolved rapidly from the Prior-Fitted Network (PFN) paradigm in-troduced by TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Hollmann et al., 2025; J¨ ager et al., 2026; Grinsztajn et al., 2026), which pretrains on synthetic tasks and performs prediction on unseen datasets through in-context learning. Building on this framework, TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) further improves scalability and representation learning by introducing column-aware model-ing and a multi-stage pretraining strategy, making foundation models applicable to larger and more heterogeneous tables. More recently, the field has expanded considerably, with models such as TabFM (Kong & Das, 2026), EXAONE (Eo et al., 2026), Mitra-2 (Tao et al., 2026), LimiX (lim, 2026), Xiaomi-TabLDM (Wang et al., 2026a), and newer versions of TabPFN exploring different directions in model scale, task coverage, architectural design, and pretraining strategies. Together, these developments have substantially advanced the capability and generality of tabular foundation models. Compared with predictive modeling, tabular foundation models for data generation remain rela-tively underexplored. TabPFGen (Ma et al., 2023) and TabEBM (Margeloiu et al., 2024) combine TabPFN with energy-based modeling (Grathwohl et al., 2020) to achieve foundation model gener-ation ability. TabFORGE (Jiang et al., 2026) leverages pretrained causality-aware tabular represen-tations for generative modeling, while DiffICL (Han et al., 2026) formulates tabular synthesis as an in-context learning problem to infer target distributions from limited examples. Despite these ad-vances, existing methods still rely on relatively long contexts or costly interaction modeling, leaving context-efficient and computationally efficient generation insufficiently explored. 3Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 

3 METHOD 

3.1 OVERVIEW 

In this section, we introduce the architecture of T AB GIFT and how to use T AB GIFT to generate new samples. T AB GIFT can be viewed as a latent diffusion model (LDM) (Lin et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; cor, 2025) consisting of three components: (1) an encoder obtained from the general tabular prediction model SwiftPFN (Liu & Ye, 2026), which leverages its pretrained generalization ability on tables to map heterogeneous tabular data into a unified latent space; (2) a score-based diffusion model (Zhang et al., 2024) trained in the latent space via in-context learning, where latent embed-dings of real samples serve as context to help the denoising process for noisy embeddings, enabling sampling from random noise; and (3) a dataset-specific MLP decoder that maps the generated latent embeddings from the latent space back to the concrete data space. 3.2 SETUP 

We consider a tabular dataset X={xi}Ni=1 , where N is the number of samples, xi∈X 1×· · ·×X F is the feature vector with F features. Features may be numerical or categorical. Given a training set Xtrain , tabular data generation aims to learn a model pθ that produces a syn-thetic dataset ˆXsyn approximating the underlying distribution of Xtrain , while preserving marginal distributions and inter-feature dependencies and avoiding copying of training samples. 3.3 SWIFT PFN E NCODER 

We use SwiftPFN as a general-purpose encoder to handle heterogeneous datasets, which is a founda-tion tabular prediction model. Using only sample-attention, SwiftPFN achieves performance com-parable to Limix (lim, 2026) and TabPFNv2 (Hollmann et al., 2025) (which use sample-attention and feature-attention). In specific, SwiftPFN first maps both the training samples ( Xtrain and ytrain )and the test samples ( Xtest only) to be predicted into the same dimension ( d = 192 ). It then uses a 12-layer Transformer architecture with a causal mask (training samples can attend to each other, while test samples can only attend to training samples) to enable interactions between the training and test samples. This allows the embedding of a test sample to learn label information, and finally a prediction head outputs the prediction result. 4 TRAINING DETAILS 

4.1 TRAINING DATA 

Following TabPFN, a general-purpose pretrained model for tabular data, we first train our model on large-scale synthetic data. Specifically, we use the open-source TabICL data generator to produce 5,000 batches of datasets. Each batch contains 512 datasets generated from different SCMs (Pearl, 2009; Poinsot et al., 2024), where each dataset has between 128 and 2,048 samples and between 2 and 50 features. We reuse these 5,000 batches 10 times and randomly shuffle the columns, yielding 50,000 usable training batches in total. For the second stage, we train on 72 real-world datasets from OpenML-CC18 (Bischl et al., 2019) and OpenML-CTR23 (Fischer et al., 2023), sampling rows and columns within the same ranges as the synthetic data. In each round, we sample one subset per dataset and group all 72 subsets into a single batch. Repeating this 1,000 times yields 1,000 batches, and reusing each batch 10 times gives 10,000 training batches in total. 4.2 TRAINING PROCESS 

We first train the model on the aforementioned 50,000 synthetic training batches and then on 10,000 real-data training batches. For each dataset, we uniformly sample a context ratio between 0.5 and 0.8, and use the remaining portion for noising and reconstruction. The entire training process is conducted on a machine equipped with an Intel Xeon Platinum 8336C CPU and 4 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. It takes about 8 days in total, with the first 7 days using synthetic data and the last day using real data. The loss curve is shown in the figure 3. We use a cosine learning rate schedule with linear warmup. The initial learning rate is set to 2 × 10 −4 with a warmup proportion of 0.02, gradient clipping of 1.0. 

5 EXPERIMENT 

This section presents our experimental setup and results. We conduct experiments on 18 real-world datasets, consisting of 7 classification tasks and 11 regression tasks. The generated data are evaluated in terms of statistical fidelity, privacy preservation, and downstream model performance. We will introduce our experimental setup and results step by step. 6Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 5.1 DATASETS 

We use the tabular benchmark TALENT (Ye et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024), which contains 300 high-quality tabular datasets. We employ a subset provided by them, Tiny Talent, which consists of 45 datasets. After removing datasets with more than 50 features, we obtain 7 classification tasks and 11 regression tasks. Detailed information about the datasets is provided in Appendix A. To systematically evaluate various generative algorithms under different data budgets, we subsample from each dataset to construct training sets of sizes 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and 512. These subsets are then used to train the generative models. For each training size, we repeat the experiment 5 times using 5 different random seeds. We save the generated data produced by each method under each training size for subsequent evaluation and comparison. This protocol allows us to assess how generation quality varies with the amount of available training data. For baselines that require standardized data, including T AB GIFT , we preprocess each dataset by applying Z-score normalization to continuous features and ordinal encoding to categorical features. This ensures that all methods operate on comparable input representations. 5.2 BASELINE GENERATORS AND DOWNSTREAM PREDICTORS 

We compare T AB GIFT with existing tabular data generation methods, including: (i) the classical data augmentation method SMOTE (Chawla et al., 2002); (ii) the VAE-based method TVAE (Xu et al., 2019); (iii) the GAN-based method CTGAN (Xu et al., 2019); (iv) the diffusion-based method TabDDPM (Kotelnikov et al., 2023); (v) the tree-based method ARF (Watson et al., 2023); (vi) a Bayesian network (BN)-based method (Ankan & Textor, 2024); (vii) the normalizing flow method NFlow (Durkan et al., 2019); and (viii) TabEBM (Margeloiu et al., 2024). For some of the methods that require hyperparameter tuning, we performed 20 optimization trials. Implementation details can be found in the Appendix B. We select 6 representative downstream predictors: XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), Random Forest (Breiman, 2001), TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025), multi-layer perceptrons (Rumelhart et al., 1986), k-nearest neighbors (Cover & Hart, 1967), and Linear Regression or Logistic Re-gression (Cox, 1958). For some downstream predictors, we perform hyperparameter tuning, while synthetic data is used to train the models. 5.3 EVALUATION 

5.3.1 DOWNSTREAM MODEL EFFICACY 

In the actual evaluation, downstream models are trained either using only the real training data or using synthetic data generated by different generation methods on top of the training data. We denote the performance of models trained on real data as Dreal , and the performance of models trained on synthetic data as Dsyn . We adopt a relative performance metric for evaluation. Specifically, the performance achieved using real data is normalized to 1. For classification tasks, we use the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) as the evaluation metric, where higher values indicate better performance. The relative performance is thus computed as Dsyn /Dreal . For regression tasks, we instead use the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), where lower values indicate better performance. Accordingly, the relative performance is defined as Dreal /Dsyn . The detailed results are shown in Tab 1. TAB GIFT consistently outperforms dataset-specific generative methods in the limited-context set-ting. Dataset-specific generative models are prone to memorizing training samples: with only a handful of examples, the empirical distribution is easily memorized, and the model struggles to dis-tinguish generalizable structure from sample-specific patterns. In contrast, during ICL pretraining, the context is constantly changing and memorizing the context cannot reduce the pretraining loss. This removes the memorization incentive and forces the model to infer transferable distributional priors, enabling high-quality generation from limited context. As the number of training samples increases, the hypothesis spaces of dataset-specific models expand with the data, and their empirical fitting ability gradually strengthens. In tab 2, we generate 1,024 synthetic samples under different training sizes and compare the runtime of all methods on the Adult dataset. SMOTE is by far the fastest ( ≈0.015s on average), since it only performs interpolation and requires no model training. TabEBM is also training-free, but it relies on multiple calls to TabPFN; its inference time alone ( ≈28s) already exceeds the total time of T AB GIFT (≈10.5s). Among methods that require training, T AB GIFT is only slower than ARF (≈5.6s) and BN ( ≈7.7s), while being substantially faster than TVAE ( ≈25.9s), CTGAN ( ≈55.8s), TabDDPM ( ≈135.5s), and NFlow ( ≈31.4s). The runtime of T AB GIFT is dominated by training the dataset-specific MLP decoder ( ≈8.2s), and inference accounts for only about 20% of the total time (≈2.3s). This confirms that T AB GIFT achieves a favorable efficiency–quality trade-off, especially in the small-data regime. 

6 LIMITATIONS 

SwiftPFN is pretrained for prediction, so its embeddings are not necessarily optimal for representing a sample in generation, and a dataset-specific MLP decoder is still required. We have explored alternatives such as a universal decoder and jointly training the encoder and decoder as a general tabular VAE, but both yielded inferior results. More systematic efforts are needed to design an encoder–decoder pair that is both transferable and generation-oriented. Pretraining on synthetic data alone leads to suboptimal generation quality, and adding real data improves performance, suggesting that the synthetic prior is insufficiently diverse or realistic. Future work could improve the quality of synthetic pretraining data, scale up real-data pretraining, and increase per-dataset size and feature dimensionality. 

7 CONCLUSION 

TAB GIFT is an efficient foundation model for tabular data generation, designed to address the key limitations of existing generative methods in small-data regimes, including overfitting, poor privacy protection, and high computational cost. Its core idea is to shift the burden of distribution learn-ing from the target dataset to foundation-model pretraining: by pretraining on large-scale hetero-geneous tabular data, T AB GIFT acquires transferable structural and distributional priors, enabling 9Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 high-quality synthetic data generation from only a few context samples. T AB GIFT has three com-ponents: a frozen SwiftPFN encoder mapping heterogeneous samples to a 192-d latent space; an 18-layer Transformer denoiser with a sample-wise attention mask that enables context-guided de-noising while keeping query denoising independent; and a lightweight dataset-specific MLP decoder mapping latents back to the data space. Training proceeds in two stages: large-scale pretraining on synthetic data (50,000 batches), followed by fine-tuning on 72 real-world datasets (10,000 batches). Experiments on 7 classification and 11 regression tasks show that T AB GIFT consistently achieves the best or near-best generation quality in the limited-context regime (size 16–64), while being sub-stantially more efficient than most baselines. 10 Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

A.2 TALENT 

TALENT (Liu et al., 2024) is a comprehensive benchmark and toolbox for tabular data learning, providing a large collection of high-quality tabular datasets together with standardized pipelines for data preprocessing, model training, and evaluation.
