# ABSTRACT 

Tabular foundation models adapt to new datasets through in-context learning (ICL), and, like most transformers, they tie computational depth to the number of independently parameterized layers. We ask whether the ICL stage of such a model needs a distinct set of parameters for every layer, or only depth. We introduce Tab-Loop, which replaces the 12 untied ICL blocks of TabICLv2 with 4 tied blocks applied k times and changes nothing else: the encoders, decoder, synthetic prior and training recipe are shared with an untied baseline trained for the same compute. At matched pretraining compute (18.2 EFLOP) and matched inference compute (12 block applications), Tab-Loop matches the baseline with 62% fewer parameters (10.4M vs. 27.6M): held-out log loss is 0.4583 vs. 0.4588, mean accuracy on 200 TALENT datasets is 84.10% vs. 83.92%, and Elo on 38 TabArena datasets is 1409 vs. 1396. A 4-block untied control with Tab-Loop’s parameter count, which sees 32% more training tables for the same compute, trails both (83.46%; Elo 1343). Because weights are shared, depth becomes a choice made at inference. Accuracy rises with the number of loops through the trained range and holds beyond it, and continued training with deeper loops extends the useful range, reaching the baseline’s stage-2 scores at 4–5 × its inference compute. At this scale, the ICL stage of a tabular foundation model needs depth more than it needs distinct parameters. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Foundation models have changed how machine learning is practised, replacing task-specific training with a single pretrained model that adapts to new tasks at inference. After language and vision, this shift has reached tabular data, the modality behind applications such as credit scoring, fraud detection, and demand forecasting. Tabular data long resisted deep learning, and gradient-boosted trees remained the method to beat (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; McElfresh et al., 2023). Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023; 2025), TabICL (Qu et al., 2025; 2026), Mitra (Zhang et al., 2025b), TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025), LimiX (Zhang et al., 2025a), TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026) and EXAONE Tabular (Eo et al., 2026) have changed this picture. Pretrained on synthetic or real tables, they predict labels for a new table in a single forward pass by conditioning on its labelled rows, without per-dataset training or tuning, and on small and medium-sized tables they now match or outperform tuned tree ensembles (Hollmann et al., 2025; Erickson et al., 2025). However, because adaptation happens at inference, every cost of these models is paid at every prediction, and this cost grows with model size and context length (Kong et al., 2026). Moreover, these models inherit a design choice from standard transformers: every layer has its own parameters, so depth and parameter count are the same resource. Recent work suggests that they need not be (see Section 2). Tabular foundation models refine their representations iteratively across layers, and much of their depth is redundant (Balef et al., 2026). This raises a crucial unanswered question: does the ICL stage of a tabular foundation model need a distinct set of parameters for every layer, or only depth? Answering it requires a controlled comparison, in which the looped and untied models share everything except the ICL stage and, 

critically , are compared at equal compute, both in training and at inference. To address this, we introduce Tab-Loop, which builds on TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) and replaces its ICL stage with a recurrent one. Rather than proposing a new architecture family, we use Tab-Loop as a controlled probe of where the capacity of a tabular foundation model lives. Our contributions are as follows. 

A controlled test of recurrence in tabular foundation model. Tab-Loop, loops 4 tied blocks in place of the 12 untied ICL blocks of TabICLv2, with parameter-free input re-injection and training over sampled loop counts, and leaves the encoders, decoder, prior and curriculum unchanged. We compare it with the untied baseline at matched, measured training compute, and with a 4-block untied control that has Tab-Loop’s parameters and spends the same compute on 32% more training tables. Unlike earlier proof-of-concept studies, this design separates weight sharing from depth, and depth from additional data (Sections 3 and 4). 

The ICL stage relies on depth more than on distinct parameters. At equal training and inference compute, Tab-Loop performs comparably to the baseline with 62% fewer parameters, reaching 84.10% against 83.92% mean accuracy on 200 TALENT datasets and an Elo of 1409 against 1396 on TabArena. The control trails both despite its additional data. Moreover, read at a single loop, Tab-Loop is weaker than the control, which shows that its computation is distributed across loops rather than stacked on a complete single pass (Section 5.1). 

Inference-time depth scaling. Without retraining, TabArena Elo rises from 1288 at one loop to 1424 at four and 1427 at five, and TALENT accuracy peaks at 84.18% at four loops, the only depth with a significant gain over the baseline. Continued training with deeper loops moves the useful range outward. On TabArena-Lite, Elo climbs from 1298 at three loops to 1512 at twelve and to about 1548 at 24 to 32 loops, while TALENT accuracy reaches 84.42% at sixteen loops, matching the stage-2 baseline at 4 to 5 times its inference compute (Sections 5.2 and 5.3). 

# 2 WHY RECURRENT DEPTH FOR TABULAR IN-C ONTEXT LEARNING ?

Recurrent depth lets a tabular foundation model apply its in-context learner as many times as a table requires, while storing the weights of that learner only once. Tabular foundation models have instead 2Preprint followed the recipe of language modelling, which is to scale: TabPFN-3 is roughly five times larger than TabPFN-2.5 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026), and published models now span more than two orders of magnitude (Table 1). Because these models adapt at inference, this growth is paid at every prediction. Compared with adding untied layers, recurrent depth has several advantages. • Tabular in-context learning is iterative and draws on the context rather than on stored knowledge. Gradient boosting refines a prediction by fitting each new learner to the residuals of the last (Friedman, 2001), and transformers learn in context in a similar way, with successive layers implementing steps of an iterative learner (von Oswald et al., 2023; M¨ uller et al., 2024). Parameters, in contrast, are where a model stores knowledge (Morris et al., 2026), whereas a tabular foundation model answers from the labelled rows in front of it. Looped transformers already match deeper untied models on in-context learning tasks with a fraction of their parameters (Yang et al., 2024; Saunshi et al., 2025). • Existing tabular foundation models are redundant in depth. Their layers refine rep-resentations progressively, and much of their depth can be removed (Balef et al., 2026; Koshil et al., 2026). However, removing layers reduces parameters and depth together, so redundancy alone cannot say which of the two the model needs. Sharing weights reduces parameters while preserving depth, and is therefore the intervention that separates them. • Recurrent depth decouples memory from computation. A tied model stores its blocks once, so Tab-Loop applies 12 blocks with 62% fewer parameters than the untied baseline. Tying alone does not reduce computation, since a shared block costs as much as an untied one, but it makes computation the only cost that grows with depth. • Depth becomes a choice at inference. An untied stack cannot be shortened after training, because its decoder only ever reads the final layer. A recurrent model with one decoder trained across depths can be read after any number of loops, and recurrent networks are known to solve harder problems by iterating longer (Schwarzschild et al., 2021; Geiping et al., 2025). Computation can then follow the difficulty of the dataset rather than the size of the model. Recurrence also has known failure modes: a shared block must perform every stage of a computation that an untied stack spreads across specialized layers (Blayney et al., 2026; Bae et al., 2025a), its state can drift with depth (Geiping et al., 2025; Movahedi et al., 2026), and information about the input can wash out unless it is re-injected (Yang et al., 2024). Tab-Loop addresses these by looping a group of four blocks rather than a single block, by re-adding the encoded input before every block application, and by confining recurrence to the ICL stage (Section 3). # 4 EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN 

Three models share the prior, encoders, decoder, optimizer and schedule, and each receives 18.2 EFLOP of stage-1 training (Table 2). They differ only in the ICL stage. Tab-Loop against the baseline holds depth and compute fixed and varies tying, so it measures parameter efficiency. Tab-Loop against the control holds parameters and training compute fixed and varies depth, so it measures whether compute is better spent on depth or on more training data. 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on three suites, with all models applied zero-shot using TabICLv2’s default preprocessing (Appendix B.4). (i) Held-out prior : 1,024 synthetic tables drawn with a seed disjoint from training, cached once and reused for every checkpoint, so comparisons are paired. We report query log loss. (ii) TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025): 38 classification datasets and all 594 splits, with Elo against the public leaderboard and 95% bootstrap intervals. Intermediate checkpoints and stage-2 models are evaluated with TabArena-Lite (one split per dataset), whose Elo is not comparable with full TabArena. (iii) TALENT (Liu et al., 2024): 200 classification datasets with official splits. We report mean test accuracy.

With one seed, we read this as equivalence, not superiority. The two models also trade places at intermediate budgets: at 7.9 EFLOP the baseline leads on both real-data suites, and at 3.6 EFLOP Tab-Loop leads on TabArena-Lite (Appendix Figure 7). 

Depth beats data. The control has Tab-Loop’s parameter count, applied once. Its steps are 24% cheaper, so at equal compute it trains on 32% more tables. It still trails Tab-Loop at every evaluation, including the end of training. On held-out tables it ends at 0.4681. On TALENT its accuracy is 83.46%, against 84.10% for Tab-Loop (better on 130 of 200 datasets) and 83.92% for the baseline. Its full TabArena Elo is 1343, against 1409 and 1396. At this scale, compute spent re-applying shared weights is worth more than compute spent on more data. 

The computation is spread across loops. Tab-Loop’s weights are not a good 4-block model refined by extra passes. Read at one loop, a depth never used in training, they are worse than the control throughout training (Figure 3, pink against green) and at the end: held-out loss 0.4979 against 0.4681, TALENT accuracy 82.78% against 83.46%, and TabArena Elo 1285 against 1343. Taken together, the two comparisons indicate that the ICL stage needs depth, not a distinct set of parameters for every layer. 5.2 DEPTH IS A DIAL WITHIN THE TRAINED RANGE 

Because the blocks are shared, the number of loops is free at inference. Figure 4 varies k for the final stage-1 checkpoint. Performance rises with depth on both benchmarks. One loop, effectively a 4-block model, falls well below the baseline (Elo 1288; accuracy 82.78%), and two loops come close (Elo 1370; 83.83%). Four loops, at a third more inference compute than the baseline, give the best results in the trained range (Elo 1424; 84.18%); this is the only depth whose TALENT gain over the baseline is significant. Beyond the trained range, Elo is 1427 at k = 5 and TALENT accuracy holds at 84.15% for k = 5 and k = 6 . Extra loops thus help up to about one loop beyond training and are neutral after that, without collapsing. The useful range of the dial is set by the depths seen in training. 5.3 TRAINING WITH DEEPER LOOPS EXTENDS THE DIAL

# 6 RELATED WORK 

Tabular foundation models. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023) showed that a transformer pretrained on synthetic tables can classify real tables without parameter updates. TabPFN v2 (Hollmann et al., 2025), TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) and TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) improved the prior, the architecture and the training curriculum. Mitra (Zhang et al., 2025b) mixes synthetic priors, TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025) trains on real data, and TabPFN-2.5 (Prior Labs, 2025), TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026), LimiX (Zhang et al., 2025a), EXAONE Tabular (Eo et al., 2026) and TabSwift (Liu & Ye, 2026) extend the family in scale, scope and efficiency. Attention along both rows and columns, which these models share, traces back to non-parametric transformers (Kossen et al., 2021). These models use untied layers. We change only that choice and hold the rest of TabICLv2 fixed. We evaluate on TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025) and TALENT (Liu et al., 2024). 

Weight sharing across depth. Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) and ALBERT (Lan et al., 2020) share parameters across layers. Looped transformers can emulate programs and iterative learning algorithms (Giannou et al., 2023), and they match standard transformers on in-context learn-ing tasks with fewer parameters, with input injection as a key component (Yang et al., 2024). Saunshi et al. (2025) show that reasoning benefits from depth without a proportional increase in distinct pa-rameters, looped transformers generalize to longer inputs than they were trained on (Fan et al., 2025), and recurrent networks extrapolate from easy to hard problems by iterating longer (Schwarzschild et al., 2021). Looped language models have since been pretrained at scale (Zhu et al., 2025). Relaxed Recursive Transformers (Bae et al., 2025a) and Gated Recurrent Transformers (Hegazy et al., 2026) study the trade-off between sharing and specialization in language models. 8Preprint 

Recurrent depth at test time. Geiping et al. (2025) scale test-time compute in language models through recurrent depth and describe convergent, orbiting and drifting latent trajectories. Fixed-point formulations stabilize deep loops (Movahedi et al., 2026), and mechanistic analyses show that a single looped block can carry out different stages of computation across iterations (Blayney et al., 2026). These dynamics motivate our question of how far beyond the trained depths a looped model remains useful. 

Adaptive computation. Adaptive Computation Time (Graves, 2016) and PonderNet (Banino et al., 2021) learn when to halt, and Mixture-of-Recursions (Bae et al., 2025b) learns a recursion depth per token. For tabular in-context learning, early stopping of the forward pass has been studied for untied models (K¨ uken et al., 2025). Tab-Loop makes depth a choice at inference; selecting it adaptively for each table is left to future work. 

Recurrence for tabular ICL. Balef et al. (2026) show that the layers of tabular foundation models are largely redundant and give a small-scale proof of concept for layer reuse, and task-aware pruning removes many of their blocks (Koshil et al., 2026). Iterative computation also appears in state-space tabular models (Thielmann et al., 2024; Koch et al., 2025) and in tiny recursive networks (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). Unlike these, Tab-Loop changes only the ICL stage of an established tabular foundation model and compares it against both an untied baseline and a parameter-matched control at equal compute, so that differences can be attributed to tying and depth. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

At the scale of modern tabular foundation models, four tied blocks applied three times match twelve distinct blocks at equal training and inference compute while using 62% fewer parameters, and a parameter-matched model that spends the same compute on more data instead of more depth falls behind. The ICL stage of a tabular foundation model therefore needs depth more than it needs distinct parameters. Tying also turns depth into a choice made at inference: within the trained range, additional loops give modest and reliable gains, and training with deeper loops extends that range. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

Before every block application i > 0, the cached stage input X0 is added to the current state, so the block input is Si + X0. The injection is applied uniformly, including between blocks within a loop, and has no learned parameters or scaling. We compared it with two alternatives in a small-scale study (Figure 6): • No injection , where blocks receive only the current state Si. Re-adding X0 lowers validation log loss by 0.0011 nats at 2 loops and by 0.0059 nats at 12 loops, so its benefit grows with depth. • Learned linear carry , Si ← W [Si; X0] with W ∈ Rd×2d initialized to [I, I ]. It is slightly worse than additive injection at the trained depths. Left unconstrained, the carry becomes expansive: the largest singular value of its state block grows to 2.2, and the state changes by more than its own norm at every loop. Its loss diverges beyond the trained range, reaching 1.14 nats at k = 32 , against about 0.40 with additive injection. # B TRAINING AND EVALUATION DETAILS 

B.1 PRETRAINING DATA 

All models are pretrained on synthetic tables drawn from the TabICLv2 prior (Qu et al., 2026), which mixes structural-causal-model, tree-based and nearest-neighbour generators with randomized feature counts, class counts and context sizes. No real-world data is used in pretraining. Each table is split into labelled context rows and unlabelled query rows, and the loss in Eq. 4 is computed on query rows only. A fixed held-out slice of 1,024 tables (16 batches of 64) is drawn from the same prior with a seed disjoint from training. It is cached once and reused for every checkpoint of every model, so per-checkpoint comparisons are paired. B.2 OPTIMIZATION 

Table 6 lists the training settings. They are shared by the untied baseline, the control and Tab-Loop; only the number of stage-1 steps differs for the control (660K instead of 500K), to equalize total pretraining compute at 18.2 EFLOP. B.3 LOOP -C OUNT SAMPLING AND GRADIENT TRUNCATION 

Stage 1. The loop count is sampled once per micro-batch, k ∼ U{ 2, 3, 4}, and shared by all tables in the micro-batch so that it can be processed as a single unrolled graph. Gradients flow through every loop. The expected number of block applications is 4 · E[k] = 12 , equal to the baseline, so per-step cost matches. 

Stage 2. The loop count is sampled as k = min {2+ z, 32 } with z ∼ Poisson(10) , giving a mean of about 12 loops. One additional loop is run after the supervised depth to compute the latent-consistency term in Eq. 6, with weight λ = 1 . Backpropagation is truncated to the final min {8, k + 1 } loops: the earlier loops run under torch.no grad , their final state is detached, and the differentiable tail runs from that state.
