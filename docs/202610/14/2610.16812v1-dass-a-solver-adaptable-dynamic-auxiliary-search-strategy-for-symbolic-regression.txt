Abstract 

Symbolic regression (SR) aims to discover interpretable mathematical expressions from data and plays a key role in scientific discovery. However, existing methods face a common bottleneck: the enormous search space and severe combinatorial explosion make it difficult to achieve a favorable trade-off between accuracy and algorithmic efficiency. We argue that, in addition to designing increasingly complex solver-specific SR strategies, an equally promising direction is to equip them with a shareable auxiliary strategy that can provide reliable search guidance. To this end, we propose DASS 3, a solver-adaptable Dynamic Auxiliary Search Strategy that improves SR by constructing and refining a high-potential auxiliary search subspace. DASS represents the auxiliary search space as a functional subspace spanned by a set of high-potential basis function terms. It estimates term utility through multi-environment evaluation, filters unstable guidance, and updates term potential scores with a Gibbs posterior, thereby calibrating the subspace to provide more reliable guidance for downstream solvers. The high-quality solutions produced by the solver are used to update the set of basis terms. This forms a virtuous closed-loop optimization process: the subspace guides the downstream solver, and the solver feedback reshapes the subspace for subsequent exploration. DASS can be easily integrated with various types of SR solvers. Extensive experiments on LLM-SRBench show that DASS significantly improves the accuracy of original solvers with a manageable increase in runtime. 

1 Introduction 

Symbolic Regression (SR) is a fundamental problem in scientific discovery and interpretable mod-eling [Chen et al., 2022, Xu et al., 2023, Meidani et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2025a, Li et al., 2023b, Kamienny et al., 2022, Li et al., 2024, Xia et al., 2025, Shojaee et al., 2025a]. Unlike conventional regression, which assumes a predefined functional form, SR seeks to discover the underlying math-ematical expression directly from data. Given N observed samples {xi, y i}Ni=1 , SR aims to find a function f : x 7 → y that explains the observed patterns. This makes SR especially important in science and engineering, where interpretability is often as crucial as predictive accuracy [Yu et al., 2025a, Li et al., 2023a, Hu et al., 2024, Guo et al., 2025, Hu et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2025, 2024]. In recent years, many methods have advanced SR. However, they share a common bottleneck: the expression space is enormous and highly compositional, while effective search signals are extremely sparse [Li et al., 2026]. Instead of letting an SR solver search the full expression space without guidance, DASS provides a solver-adaptable auxiliary search subspace spanned by high-potential basis terms. The subspace is initialized from LLM prior knowledge, calibrated by multi-environment evaluation, and reshaped using feedback from high-quality solver results. In this way, DASS guides diverse SR solvers toward promising regions while continuously refining the guided subspace. 2022] regard the SR problem as a direct prediction problem from data to expressions, allowing efficient inference. Yet, when faced with such a vast combinatorial space, this one-shot prediction paradigm often struggles to ensure expression accuracy and structural reliability, as it lacks explicit structural exploration and iterative refinement. In contrast, search-based methods can often produce higher-quality results. However, whether based on Genetic Programming [Cranmer, 2023, Stephens, 2024, Zhang et al., 2025a], Reinforcement Learning [Petersen et al., 2021], Monte Carlo Tree Search [Sun et al., 2023], or Large Language Models [Shojaee et al., 2025a, Du et al., 2024], these solvers fundamentally rely on repeated trial-and-error and backtracking in a massive combinatorial space, and thus often suffer from slow search, high randomness, and low exploration efficiency. We argue that, beyond designing increasingly complex search strategies for individual solvers, an equally important direction is to develop a shareable auxiliary strategy that can guide the search across different solvers. Such a subspace should satisfy three key requirements: (1) reliable , meaning that it highlights high-potential regions that remain worth exploring across different stages and conditions rather than reflecting unstable biases induced by incidental patterns; (2) dynamic , meaning that it can be continuously updated by search feedback rather than providing static one-shot guidance; and (3) solver-adaptable , meaning that it can support various SR solvers rather than being tied to a specific SR method. As illustrated in Fig. 1, with such a subspace, SR is no longer unguided trial-and-error over the full expression space; instead, search is steered toward high-probability regions, and optimization is lifted from improving isolated candidate expressions to continuously reshaping that region. The key point is how to represent such an auxiliary subspace. Complete equations are too costly and inflexible as basic units, while atomic operators or symbols are too fine-grained to carry meaningful structural semantics. In this paper, we model the auxiliary subspace as an expression subspace spanned by a set of high-potential basis function terms. This term-level representation turns optimizing auxiliary subspace into the continuous updating of basis terms, enabling efficient representation and reshaping of high-potential search regions at a much lower cost. Building on this insight, we propose DASS, a solver-adaptable Dynamic Auxiliary Search Strategy. DASS dynamically maintains and refines a reliable, shareable auxiliary search subspace that steers the solver toward more promising regions. Specifically, DASS initializes the basis terms and their potential scores from prior knowledge, then samples expressions from the current subspace. To identify reliable search guidance, DASS evaluates these sampled expressions across multiple environments and estimates the utility of each basis term for effective search, while stability filtering suppresses terms that are only effective at particular stages or under particular environments. Based on these utility and stability signals, DASS updates term potential scores via a Gibbs posterior, thereby calibrating the subspace toward high-potential regions for prioritized exploration. High-quality solutions returned by the solver are then used to update the basis term set and its score distribution for subsequent rounds. This forms a virtuous closed loop: the subspace guides search, and search feedbacks in turn reshape the subspace. As a solver-adaptable front-end module, DASS can be 2naturally integrated with a wide range of SR solvers. Extensive experiments on LLM-SRBench show that, under the same search budget, DASS nearly doubles average accuracy, with a 97.84% relative gain and only a 24.14% runtime increase . Conversely, to achieve comparable or better accuracy, DASS-augmented solvers can be 1.5 × to 8 × faster than their original versions. Our contributions come in three areas: (1) We introduce a novel direction for SR: developing reliable, updatable, and shareable auxiliary search guidance for diverse solvers rather than focusing solely on solver-specific design. (2) We propose DASS, a solver-adaptable framework that models auxiliary subspace as a term-spanned search space and serves as a front-end module for different SR solvers. (3) We develop a closed-loop optimization mechanism that turns sparse solver feedback into stable term-level guidance through multi-environment evaluation, stability-aware filtering, Gibbs posterior updating, and feedback-driven subspace refinement. 

2 Related Work 

Most classic SR methods rely on search and optimization algorithms, such as genetic programming (GP) and its variants [Cranmer, 2023, Stephens, 2024, Xu et al., 2020, Virgolin et al., 2021]. These methods evolve candidate equations iteratively through mutation, crossover, and selection. In addition, SPL [Sun et al., 2023], DSR [Petersen et al., 2021], and other methods utilize methods such as MCTS and RL to optimize search. However, such search-based methods require repeated exploration and backtracking in a vast discrete combinatorial space, often leading to slow search. To improve inference efficiency, another line of work tries to directly learn the mapping from numerical observations to symbolic expressions, giving rise to end-to-end neural SR [Biggio et al., 2021, Kamienny et al., 2022, Li et al., 2022]. For example, E2ESR [Kamienny et al., 2022] directly predicts complete expressions together with their constants. By shifting most of the search cost to pretraining, these methods can generate expressions rapidly during inference. However, such one-shot prediction paradigm lacks explicit exploration and correction, making it difficult to ensure reliable structures and accurate predictions. To balance accuracy and search efficiency, several methods combine Transformer-based pre-trained models with optimization and search algorithms, aiming to leverage both learned priors and search feedback [Meidani et al., 2023, Shojaee et al., 2023]. GenSR [Li et al., 2026] constructs a smooth latent space and uses CMA-ES algorithm to search expression. Although these methods are not purely end-to-end prediction, they still lack explicit structural guidance at the search stage. Recently, with the development of LLMs, many works try to exploit their knowledge and program-generation capabilities for SR [Xia et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025b, Ma et al., 2024]. LASR [Grayeli et al., 2024] abstracts and evolves concepts from high-quality expressions using LLMs, constructing a concept library to guide search. LLM-SR [Shojaee et al., 2025a] represents equations as programs and uses LLMs to generate equation skeletons. Actually, these methods replace traditional search processes such as crossover and mutation with language-model-driven generation. Coupled with the inherent hallucination issues of LLMs, they still require repeated exploration in an extremely large expression space and involve a large number of iterative LLM calls. Overall, existing SR methods have made significant progress in searcher design, pretrained modeling, and the use of LLMs capabilities. While they exhibit different strengths, they share a fundamental bottleneck: the expression space is highly combinatorial and extremely large, whereas effective search feedback is sparse. Thus, we argue that instead of only improving a specific type of solver, it is more desirable to provide different solvers with a reliable, dynamic, and shareable auxiliary search strategy. In this way, search is no longer an unguided trial-and-error process over the full space, but is transformed into a continual process of guiding and reshaping high-potential regions. Thus, different solvers can achieve higher efficiency while retaining their advantages.

3.2 Prior-Driven Subspace Initialization 

At the initial stage, DASS leverages the domain-specific priors of LLMs to initialize the auxiliary subspace. Specifically, we prompt the LLM to generate commonly used function terms in the target scientific domain. The prompt includes three components: (1) role specification, which places the model in the context of classical scientific literature in the target domain; (2) task instruction, which provides the task background, variables’ description, and structural constraints of candidate basis terms to be generated; and (3) output-format constraints, which define the required format to ensure that the generated results can be easily parsed and aggregated. To estimate the initial potential scores, we repeatedly query the LLM with the same prompt under memory-free sampling, where each generation is independent of previous outputs. Terms that appear frequently across independent generations are considered more likely under the target domain background and are assigned higher potential scores. We retain the top-n unique terms by frequency to form the initial basis set B(0) , and define the initial potential score of term si as its normalized generation frequency: ω(0)

If we regard basis terms as directions of the subspace, subspace calibration aims to estimate which directions have a higher search potential and whether such potential remains stable across different environments. We update the potential score of each term in three steps: (1) multi-environment evaluation, i.e., the utilities of basis terms are evaluated under diverse environments; (2) stability filtering, i.e., suppressing terms with unstable utility across different environments; and (3) Gibbs posterior update, i.e., we update the potential scores via Gibbs posterior. Terms with high potential scores are used to guide the downstream solver. At t-th round of DASS, let B(t) and ω(t) denote the current basis set and its potential scores, respectively. Since the current auxiliary subspace is induced by the terms in B(t), we can sample m

expressions from the auxiliary subspace by randomly combining basis terms under a maximum-length constraint: Q(t) = {f1, · · · , f m|fi ∼ span( B(t))}.These sampled expressions are then evaluated to estimate the potential scores of the terms in B(t),thereby obtaining the score distribution over different directions of the subspace.

3.4 Solver-Adaptable Search Guidance 

After calibration, DASS obtains a set of basis terms with updated potential scores, which define the current auxiliary search subspace. To guide downstream symbolic regression solvers, DASS samples high-potential terms according to these scores and converts them into solver-specific search guidance direction. For LLM-based solvers, these terms are injected into prompts as structural hints; for evolutionary solvers, they bias mutation and crossover toward promising symbolic components; and for MCTS-based solvers, they are incorporated into the grammar library to guide tree expansion. In all cases, DASS acts as a front-end module rather than a replacement for the solver itself. Additional implementation details are deferred to the Appendix C. 

3.5 Feedback-Driven Subspace Reshaping

4 Experiment 

4.1 Experimental Setup Dataset. Since our method and the main baselines involve LLMs, we evaluate DASS on the LLM-SRBench [Shojaee et al., 2025b]. As noted in Xia et al. [2025], we also find that LSR-Transform dataset of LLM-SRBench may still suffer from memorization issues. Thus, we choose LSR-Synth dataset of LLM-SRBench to evaluate all methods. LSR-Synth dataset constructs symbolic regression problems by combining known functional terms with synthetically generated novel terms, thereby avoiding evaluation contamination from equations that may have been memorized by LLMs during large-scale pretraining. LSR-Synth contains 129 symbolic regression tasks spanning four scientific domains: Physics ( 44 ), Materials Science ( 25 ), Chemistry ( 36 ), and Biology ( 24 ). Each task provides a training set, an in-domain (ID) test set, and an out-of-domain (OOD) test set.

Base solvers. DASS is not designed as a standalone SR solver, but rather as a plug-in module that assists existing solvers. The position of DASS makes our main experiments substantially different from prior SR work. Under the same settings, comparing solver A with solver A+DASS most directly isolates the gain introduced by DASS, which motivates the paired comparisons in Tab. 1. In contrast, comparing solver A+DASS with solver B, conflates the effects of both the base solver and DASS. Similarly, comparing solver A+DASS with solver B+DASS still includes the inherent performance gap between solvers A and B. A direct comparison between DASS and solver A is not meaningful because DASS cannot perform symbolic search independently. In the main text, we integrate DASS into five representative SR solvers covering three search paradigms: LLM-based solvers, including LLM-SR [Shojaee et al., 2025a] and LaSR [Grayeli et al., 2024]; GP-based solvers, including PySR [Cranmer, 2023] and gplearn [Stephens, 2024]; and an MCTS-based solver, SPL [Sun et al., 2023]. For the two LLM-based methods, we evaluate them and their DASS-augmented variants on both a small LLM, Qwen3.5-9B [Qwen Team, 2026], and a larger LLM, Gemma-4-31B. For each solver, the baseline and its DASS-augmented variant use exactly the same core search hyperparameters, so that the comparison isolates the contribution of the DASS module itself. Moreover, the Appendix D provides implementation details and experimental results for integrating DASS with latent-space-based method (GenSR [Li et al., 2026]) and policy-based method (DSR [Petersen et al., 2021]). These two methods do not expose an explicit interface for injecting symbolic priors or search biases. 4.3 Low-Budget Base Solvers with DASS vs. High-Budget Base Solvers 

Table 1 shows that DASS consistently improves the accuracy of the corresponding base solvers under a fixed search budget. In practice, however, search budget is often a primary concern [Yu et al., 2025b]: LLM calls incur non-negligible cost, and non-LLM solvers can also require long runtimes 8on individual problems. Therefore, a more meaningful comparison is whether a DASS-augmented solver with low-budget can match a DASS-free base solver with high-budget. We answer this question using three representative solvers from distinct search paradigms: LLMSR, PySR, and SPL. Each solver uses low and high budgets, with all other hyperparameters fixed; details are in Figure 3. Averaged over the four scientific domains, Figure 3 shows a consistent trend: low-budget DASS matches or exceeds the accuracy of the corresponding high-budget base solver while requiring substantially less search time. For LLMSR and PySR, DASS reaches comparable accuracy using only about 12 .42% and 67 .87% of the high-budget search time, respectively. For SPL, it uses about 49 .53% of the time and further improves accuracy by 18 .17% . This result indicates that by shifting the solver away from undirected search over the full expression space and into a continuously improving high-potential subspace, DASS extracts more useful signal per unit of search budget. 5 Conclusion: Limitations & Future Work 

We introduced DASS, a solver-adaptable dynamic auxiliary search strategy for SR. DASS maintains a continuously updated auxiliary search subspace that guides the search toward high-potential regions of the expression space. Nevertheless, DASS has limitations. Although DASS is designed to be solver-adaptable, its integration still depends on whether the downstream solver provides an interface for receiving guidance signals. For example, genetic algorithms can incorporate guidance signals during crossover and mutation; LLM-based methods can include them in prompts; and MCTS methods explicitly maintain a grammar library for priors. For solvers without explicit prior-injection or bias-control mechanisms, additional design may be needed before DASS can be applied effectively. For example, DASS can still be coupled to latent-space-based and policy-based methods by intervening at the latent-search level or at the policy level, which are discussed in Appendix D. More broadly, we hope this work highlights a complementary direction for symbolic regression: beyond building stronger standalone solvers, it is also valuable to study shared, reliable, and updatable search guidance across solver families. We hope based on our DASS, future work can explore more universal guidance signals, richer subspace representations, and extensions of this closed-loop paradigm to broader scientific discovery settings.

Symbolic-regression benchmarks have evolved from physics-inspired equation discovery [Udrescu and Tegmark, 2020, La Cava et al., 2021] to more recent LLM-driven scientific equation discov-ery [Shojaee et al., 2025b]. Each task in LSR-Synth [Shojaee et al., 2025b] is defined by a synthetic ground-truth expression together with its corresponding set of input variables. The expression-generation pipeline proceeds as follows: GPT-4o [Hurst et al., 2024] first generates a set of candidate synthetic terms for a given scientific question; these candidates are then sequentially filtered by a numerical solver to verify solvability, by GPT-4o to assess novelty, and finally by domain experts, who cross-validate the retained expressions against their data visualizations. Each task contains 4,000 training samples, 500 in-distribution (ID) test samples, and 500 out-of-distribution (OOD) test samples. Gaussian noise, whose variance is scaled according to the magnitude of the target variable, is added to the outputs. The ID test samples are drawn independently from the same variable ranges as the training set. In contrast, the OOD test samples extend the range of at least one variable (typically time or a control variable) beyond the training interval, primarily via upper-end extrapolation, in order to evaluate the stability of the fitted expression under variable extrapolation. All tasks in LSR-Synth provide both ID and OOD test sets, and Appendix K.1 reports OOD performance separately. 

B Base Solvers, LLM Backbones, and Compute Resources 

B.1 Base Solver Configurations LLM-SR LLM-SR [Shojaee et al., 2025a] represents an equation as a Python program with constant placeholders: the LLM iteratively proposes program skeletons, while a numerical optimizer fits the constants for each skeleton [Broyden, 1970]. High-scoring skeletons are then added to an experience pool composed of multiple sampling islands [Romera-Paredes et al., 2024, Novikov et al., 2025], which is used to guide subsequent proposals. In our configuration, LLM-SR generates at most 100 candidate programs per task, a 10 × reduction from the 1000 used in the original paper; this budget is kept identical between the base solver and its DASS-augmented variant. As shown in Section 4.3, the base solver augmented with DASS at this 100-candidate budget approaches the original 1000-candidate accuracy while spending significantly less search time. 

LaSR LaSR [Grayeli et al., 2024] augments the multi-population GP framework of PySR with an LLM-maintained natural-language concept library. In each generation, the LLM replaces mutation, crossover, or randomization operators with a small probability, using the current concepts as prompts. Elite expressions are abstracted into concepts and further evolved by the LLM to form the next set of concepts [Holt et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2025a]. We use 10 populations with 33 individuals per population, 20 outer iterations, and 550 GP cycles per outer iteration. The LLM replacement weights for crossover, mutation, and randomization are all set to 0.02 .

PySR PySR [Cranmer, 2023] is a multi-population island-GP framework [Koza, 1994] that com-bines an annealing-style acceptance criterion with an expression-complexity penalty in the Pareto-front spirit of modern SR [Cranmer et al., 2020, Virgolin et al., 2021], with constants fitted by BFGS [Broyden, 1970]. PySR is a widely adopted baseline in recent SR benchmarks [La Cava et al., 2021, Shojaee et al., 2025b], with parallel C++ alternatives such as Operon [Burlacu et al., 2020]. In the main table, we use 15 populations with 33 individuals per population, 65 outer iterations, 550 GP cycles per outer iteration, and a maximum expression length of 30. The binary operator set is 

{+, −, ×, ÷, ∧} , and the unary operator set is {exp , log , √·, sin , cos , | · |} . As shown in Section 4.3, PySR augmented with DASS at 65 outer iterations approaches the accuracy of the base solver run at 130 iterations at substantially lower search time. 

GPlearn GPlearn [Stephens, 2024] is a scikit-learn-style single-population GP implementation that evolves expressions over a fixed population using tournament selection. We use a population of 500 individuals and a tournament size of 20, and run the evolution for 100 generations. The function set follows PySR’s setting. 14 SPL SPL [Sun et al., 2023] formulates symbolic regression as an MCTS [Browne et al., 2012] decision process over a context-free grammar. Each select–expand–simulate–backup cycle samples a grammar-derivation path, after which the resulting expression is fitted with constants using Powell optimization and assigned a reward; the reward is then backpropagated to update the UCB [Auer et al., 2002] estimates. SPL belongs to the broader family of neural-guided and search-based SR methods, including DSR [Petersen et al., 2021], neural-guided GP seeding [Mundhenk et al., 2021], and the unified deep SR framework [Landajuela et al., 2022]. After several search episodes, a transplant operation is performed: substructures are extracted from the current elite expressions and introduced as new grammar rules. We run 10 transplant operations for each task, with each transplant consisting of 1000 episodes. The maximum expression length is set to 20, the UCB decay coefficient to η = 0 .999 , and the initial module capacity to 20 . As shown in Section 4.3, SPL augmented with DASS at 10 transplants achieves both higher accuracy and lower search time than the base solver run at 20 transplants. 

B.2 LLM Backbones 

We use two open-weight LLMs as backbones. Qwen3.5-9B [Qwen Team, 2026] is a 9B-parameter post-trained model from the Qwen3.5 family, released as open weights in March 2026. Qwen3.5-9B uses a dense language backbone with a hybrid architecture that interleaves Gated DeltaNet layers and gated attention layers, together with a vision encoder. In our experiments, we use only its text-generation capability. On reasoning-oriented benchmarks such as MMLU-Pro and GPQA Diamond, Qwen3.5-9B matches or exceeds the previous-generation Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 [Qwen Team, 2025] model despite its smaller parameter count. Gemma-4-31B 4 is Google DeepMind’s 30.7B-parameter dense instruction-tuned Gemma 4 model, with a 256K-token context window and multilingual support. Both models natively support configurable thinking modes. To align the inference paradigm with the non-reasoning LLMs used in the original LLM-SR and LaSR settings and to speed up inference, we explicitly disable thinking for both backbones. Both models are served through vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023], with the sampling temperature set to τ = 0 .8 and the maximum response length set to 8192 tokens.

All experiments are conducted on a single server equipped with 6 × NVIDIA H800 PCIe GPUs (80 GB each), dual AMD EPYC 9654 96-core CPUs (192 physical cores, 384 threads in total). LLM-based solvers (LLM-SR, LaSR) use the GPUs for vLLM-based inference, while non-LLM solvers (PySR, GPlearn, SPL) run on the CPU. C DASS Integration with Each Base Solver 

C.1 LLM-SR + DASS 

The search procedure of LLM-SR is fully preserved when combined with DASS: equation-skeleton proposal, constant fitting, candidate scoring, and cross-round search-state management all follow the original LLM-SR implementation. DASS is incorporated as an inserted module during prompt construction. These terms are presented alongside the reference equations originally used by LLM-SR, serving as structural guidance for the language model. Unlike LLM-SR, which directly generates code, LaSR guides GP search using a natural-language concept library maintained by an LLM. Elite expressions extracted by GP are abstracted into concepts, which are then fed back in prompt form to the mutation and crossover operators in the next generation. The integration point of DASS with LaSR therefore lies at the input side of the concept library. At the DASS stage of round t, the top-k% terms are selected from B(t) according to ω(t) and converted into natural-language concepts through a lightweight translation layer. These concepts are used as the initial concepts for the LaSR call in that round and are incorporated into the GP process together with the concepts evolved by LaSR itself. The internal GP algorithm of LaSR, including the population size, hall of fame, and mutation/crossover operators, as well as its concept-evolution mechanism, follows the original settings. 

C.3 PySR + DASS 

PySR implements GP search in Julia and natively supports two mechanisms: guesses, which inject external expressions into the population, and warm start, which makes the next search continue from the population, complexity archive, and hall of fame produced by the previous search rather than restarting from scratch. Building on warm start, we divide the search for each task into several consecutive search calls, each referred to as a chunk. The entire process uses the same regressor instance. Before each chunk begins, DASS supplies PySR with two kinds of guesses: the top-k%

terms from the current (B(t), ω(t)), and additive combinations of these terms, with combinations constrained to satisfy PySR’s expression-complexity limits. Once inserted into the population, these guesses in PySR become ordinary GP individuals and can subsequently be selected, mutated, and crossed over under the same rules as other expressions; within that chunk, the search proceeds under PySR’s default procedure. 

C.4 GPlearn + DASS 

GPlearn is a GP implementation with a scikit-learn-style interface. In each round of DASS, the top-k% terms from the current (B(t), ω(t)), together with additive combinations of these terms constrained to satisfy GPlearn’s program-depth limits, are compiled into GPlearn’s internal program objects and used to replace the same number of individuals in the current population, thereby keeping the population size unchanged. When a new generation starts, GPlearn’s tournament selection draws a fixed-size random subset from the entire population and picks the highest-fitness individual as the parent. The injected individuals and the original individuals participate in this selection process under the same rules. Across multiple injections, GPlearn continues training on the same regressor instance, with the generation counter accumulated and the population, mutation and crossover operators, parsimony coefficient, and other settings all retained. 

C.5 SPL + DASS 

SPL constructs expression trees over a context-free grammar (CFG) [McKay et al., 2010] using MCTS. Each action in the search corresponds to selecting a grammar production rule, which expands the current stack-top nonterminal into a more specific substructure. Once a complete expression is obtained, its constant placeholders are numerically fitted, and the node estimates are updated through UCB backpropagation. The injection point of DASS in SPL is the production-rule set. At the DASS stage of round t, the top-k% terms in B(t), ranked by ω(t), are each encapsulated as a new production rule. These rules are added to the grammar alongside SPL’s base production rules and the rules generated by transplantation, thereby introducing effective signals from DASS into the search process. The MCTS selection, expansion, simulation, and backpropagation loop, the UCB scoring form, the transplant triggering mechanism, and the maintenance of high-reward module rules all follow the original SPL settings.

D.1 Coupling DASS with latent-space-based SR solver (e.g., GenSR) 

GenSR [Li et al., 2026] belongs to the broader family of neural SR approaches that operate in representations, including equation-learner networks [Sahoo et al., 2018] and end-to-end transformer-based SR [Kamienny et al., 2022, Meidani et al., 2023, d’Ascoli et al., 2023]. I Term-Level Utility and Interaction-Aware Attribution 

We compare our term-utility estimator in the main text with two alternatives. The counting variant adds one unit of utility whenever a term occurs in an elite expression. The marginal variant replaces the fitness numerator in the standard utility estimator with 

∆sF (f ) = max {0, F (f ) − F (f \ s)} , (6) where F (f ) = Fitness( f ) and f \ s denotes the expression after removing term s. The coefficients of the reduced expression are refitted before its fitness is evaluated. This variant measures the effect of removing a term in the context of the remaining terms, while retaining the expression-length normalization and the subsequent DASS update procedure. It captures some context dependence, but is not a complete decomposition of higher-order interactions. Marginal attribution yields a further 1.33 -point gain over the original estimator, but increases time from 245 .3 to 364 .1 seconds, approximately 48 .4% . The original estimator offers a favorable observed accuracy–time trade-off, while marginal attribution is a useful alternative when additional computation is available.

k, and the number of DASS rounds per task T . Sections L.1–L.4 conduct ablation studies on these four hyperparameters. The base solvers are LLM-SR, PySR, and SPL, and the evaluation metric is the average Acc 0.01 over the four domains on the LSR-Synth ID test set. L.5 Prompt for Initializing the Basis Term Set B

We instantiate a unified prompt template for each domain (Fig. 13) and use GPT-4o to initialize the basis term set B, resulting in the initial set B(0) . GPT-4o is used to remain consistent with the LLM employed by LLM-SRBench [Shojaee et al., 2025b] during its synthesis stage. The template contains five main domain-related placeholder fields: the domain, textual descriptions of the output and input variables, and the output and input symbols. These fields are filled according to the description of each scientific domain. The {count} field is set to 10, so that each call asks the model to return 10 candidate terms. Under memory-free sampling, the same prompt is independently queried 20 times (with sampling temperature drawn independently from U (0 .7, 1.1) per call), yielding 200 candidate records for each domain. Before computing frequencies, we normalize the names of all free coefficients in each record by replacing them with a shared placeholder. Records that differ only in the naming of coefficient symbols are therefore merged into the same unique term; for example, 

α sin( x) and β sin( x) are treated as equivalent after merging. Let c(si) denote the frequency of the merged term si. We select the top-n unique terms ranked by frequency to form the initial basis set 

B(0) for the corresponding domain, where n is the upper bound on the basis-set size. The normalized frequency is used as the initial potential score, ω(0)
