Abstract 

The hallmark of Deep Research agents lies in compositional reasoning, the capacity to aggregate distributed, heterogeneous informa-tion into coherent logical insights. However, current agentic systems are often retrieval-heavy but reasoning-light, where success is predominantly determined by simple entity-seeking rather than the multi-step aggregation of scattered evidence. To address this, we pro-pose a data synthesis pipeline WebAggrega-tor , designed to shift the agentic paradigm from retrieval-centric to compositional aggre-gation. Our approach first employs Proactive Explorer to collect interconnected knowledge, then Compositional Logic Proposer to weave knowledge into complex questions using over 12 composition guidelines derived from a rigor-ous deconstruction of the Deep Research prob-lem setting. By leveraging 10K verifiable QA pairs grounded on 50K websites, we curate a high-quality SFT dataset via rejection sampling. Fine-tuning on this corpus fundamentally trans-forms agent behavior, fostering deliberate com-position reasoning and reduced tool redundancy. The resulting WebAggregator-32B surpasses GPT-4.1 and matches Claude-3.7-Sonnet on GAIA, WebWalkerQA, and XBench. To ad-dress the lack of benchmarks that emphasize both reasoning and retrieval, we introduce the WebAggregatorQA testbed, which reveals that even with perfect retrieval, top-tier models still underperformed. These results demonstrate that compositional reasoning, not retrieval, is the true performance ceiling for next-generation research agents.

2 WebAggregator 

We aim to automatically synthesize scalable, chal-lenging QA pairs grounded in real web resources, requiring minimal human involvement . Unlike fact-lookup puzzles (Wei et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b), these tasks demand both complex retrieval and com-positional reasoning. We propose an automatically verifiable pipeline, WebAggregator (Figure 3), that frames synthesis as an agentic task: 1 starting from anchor URLs, Proactive Explorer harvests inter-connected information from diversified sources, which is formulated by Compositional Logic Pro-poser into reasoning-intensive QAs, which then undergo rigorous automated quality control. 

2.1 Anchor URL Collection 

By selecting seed URLs across multiple domains, we can effectively regulate the dataset’s domain distribution, thereby enabling effective distribution balance. We sampled 5,000 topic-diverse queries from QA and scientific datasets (Yang et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2019; Trivedi et al., 2022) and retrieved URLs via Google Search, resulting in over 160,000 anchor links from 11+ domains (Figure 4).

2.2 WebAggregator: From Exploration to Composition 

In this section, we introduce our approach to synthe-sizing target QA pairs by framing task synthesis as a specialized form of web-agent tasks . The agent is given a task prompt consisting of two components: Proactive Explorer and Compositional Logic Pro-posal, enabling it to complete the task construction in an end-to-end manner. 

2.2.1 Proactive Explorer Proactive Explorer aims to collect diverse infor-mation as the foundation for task construction by exploration on the real web. During this phase (Fig-ure 3), the agent is prompted to start from a single anchor URL and employ various tools to navigate across web pages to discover any unknown but rel-evant information. Interactions include navigating heterogeneous content types such as text, files, and images, as well as dynamic element interactions, which are still challenging for even state-of-the-art vision language models (Zhang et al., 2025). To control task difficulty and ensure the comprehen-siveness of the knowledge scope, a minimum num-ber of web page visits is enforced (Appendix B.3). This unconstrained exploration proves highly ef-fective at sourcing diverse information. Analysis of 5,296 exploration trajectories (Figure 4) reveals sig-nificant interaction diversity. Every trajectory lever-ages at least three foundational tools: Search, Visit, and ScreenShot. Crucially, a majority of trajecto-ries employ a wider toolkit: 48.36% use 4 distinct tools, and 28.55% use 5, while 13.41% engage with 6 or more. 2.2.2 Compositional Logic Proposer Compositional Logic Proposer transforms raw, un-structured information into sophisticated reasoning challenges. To systematically generate diverse and challenging reasoning chains, we developed a tax-onomy of logical operations inspired by prior work on multi-hop and compositional QA (Sen et al., 2022; Talmor et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2025c; Fang et al., 2024; Krishna et al., 2025). As shown in Figure 3, this taxonomy is organized into four high-level categories: Element, Set, Scientific Analysis, and Temporal Reasoning, which are further refined into 12 granular subtypes. Element and Set op-erations are the basis of compositional reasoning behaviors among knowledge snippets, such as math-ematical calculation among elements and Filtering 

among sets. While Scientific Analysis and Tempo-ral Reasoning are advanced applications of them, involving computation-intensive and data science tasks, to reflect real but challenging user cases. Crucially, these 12 subtypes act as high-level conceptual guides within the prompt, not as rigid templates. They instruct the Proposer to instanti-ate these patterns into concrete, context-specific questions. For example, Element->mathematical calculation guide can be instantiated as addition, subtraction, or ratio calculation, depending on the available data. This flexible, guided approach en-sures the generation of novel and diverse reasoning challenges (examples provided in Table 4). 

2.2.3 Quality Control QA Alignment Checking We implement a two-stage verification process to ensure data integrity. First, the Proposer employs a self-refinement tool with a checklist to revise questions (Appendix B.5). Second, another agent reviews the data by solv-ing it, verifying reference URLs to guarantee strict alignment between questions, answers, and sources (Appendix B.4). This rigorous post-hoc filtering removes 11.72% of the initial samples. 

Diversity Constraint We actively balance the dataset across domains and compositional types. 2

First, we normalize the distribution of anchor URL domains annotated by GPT-4.1 (Figure 4). Second, we dynamically adjust prompts to upweight under-represented composition operations (e.g., calculat-ing average) identified via preliminary analysis. As shown in Figure 8, this strategy successfully spawns diverse low-level operations tailored to high-level guidance, such as intersection for Set tasks and table processing for Scientific Analysis. 

2.2.4 Trajectory Sampling 

After the task synthesis, we collect the trajectory that completes these tasks. We utilize the agent based on GPT-4.1 with SmolAgents and collect the plain text trajectories. To ensure the quality of the collected trajectories, we conduct a further rejection sampling and finally collected 6,184 trajectories for the foundation model training. (1) Correctness : We retain those trajectories with correct predictions ac-cording to the reference answers. (2) Format : Data with format errors (e.g., undefined tool name or pa-rameters) is filtered out. (3) Exception Handling :Anomalies in observations (e.g., page failures) are kept to improve the model’s generalization, since similar situations would occur in the real web.

2.3 WebAggregatorQA testset for Compositional Reasoning Evaluation 

Existing benchmarks (Wu et al., 2025b; Wei et al., 2025) primarily assess information-seeking, often neglecting compositional reasoning. To address this critical gap, we introduce the test set of We-bAggregatorQA, which is meticulously annotated to evaluate both retrieval and reasoning capabilities. 

Annotation Details We uniformly split 200 tasks as seeds from WebAggregatorQA across different domains to ensure high task diversity. We aim to ensure every question is unambiguous, with the reference answer correct, other candidates are veri-fiably incorrect. First, human annotators solve the QA pairs, then review the QA and references to eliminate ambiguities by adding clear constraints. To further enhance sample reliability and reduce bias from the solver’s perspective, this process is repeated twice: tasks are solved, ambiguities identi-fied, and revisions made by annotators. In the final cross-validation stage, each question was answered by at least two annotators, yielding 155 consistently aligned samples. The other 4 samples were aban-doned by annotators due to difficulty, but their ref-erences and questions were verified to ensure data quality and thus were retained. More details are shown in Appendix B.2. This yielded 159 samples, including those in text and multimodal, categorized by difficulty into Level 1 (24), Level 2 (99), and Level 3 (36). 3.2 Main Results Effects of WebAggregatorQA Training Set 

The experiment results are shown in Table 1 and Table 2. In the zero-shot setting, the closed-sourced models surpass Qwen models on benchmarks. How-ever, after tuning on WebAggregatorQA, Qwen models exhibit clear and steady improvements on all of the benchmarks and approach the performance of these strong baselines. Specifically, the WebAg-gregator based on Qwen2.5-32B and Qwen3-32B surpasses most of the strong baselines, including GPT-4.1 and WebShaper. The pass@3 performance of WebAggregator-32B achieves 69.9% on GAIA-text. These observations prove the quality of We-bAggregatorQA and the effectiveness of our data construction paradigm. 

Difficulty of WebAggregatorQA Test Set The complex aggregation needs in WebAggregatorQA pose a new challenge for current agent systems. GPT-4.1-powered SmolAgents attain 43.7% accu-racy on GAIA-text but drop to 25.8% on WebAg-gregatorQA. Claude-3.7-sonnet shows a similar de-cline. Furthermore, the performance gap between Claude and GPT-4.1 is smaller on WebAggrega-torQA than on GAIA-text. This suggests that for the harder questions in WebAggregatorQA, neither model can solve them effectively, which leads to the reduced gap. These results and our following analysis (Section 4) highlight the substantial gap between current agent capabilities and the de-mands of compositional reasoning needed for multi-hop web tasks. 

Data Efficiency and Small Foundations The construction of WebAggregatorQA also enables highly efficient model training. By fine-tuning Qwen3-8B on small subsets of 500 and 1,200 trajec-tories, we achieve 36.9% and 38.83% accuracy on GAIA-text, respectively, demonstrating that even limited high-quality data from WebAggregatorQA can yield significant performance gains. This effi-ciency translates to superior performance in smaller foundation models; for instance, WebAggregator-8B achieves parity with GPT-4.1 on GAIA-text and significantly outperforms previous baselines on WebWalkerQA and XBench (Table 2). Despite these gains, a performance gap persists between 8B and 32B models on the more complex WebAggre-gatorQA tasks, suggesting that while small foun-dations are increasingly viable and cost-effective, breaking through the performance bottleneck of small foundations on hard tasks remains a vital di-rection.

4.4 Screening of Failure Modes 

To elucidate the inherent challenges of WebAggre-gatorQA, we conduct a qualitative analysis of fail-ure trajectories. We manually examine 50 repre-sentative cases from the models listed in Table 3 where all references were retrieved, comprising: 28 instances from WebAggregator-32B, 6 each from Claude and GPT-4.1, and 10 additional failure cases. We reveal a taxonomy of three failure categories: 

Information Seeking Failures These errors pre-clude access to valid evidence. A primary issue is Source Location Failure , where agents misled by initial uncertainty or sub-optimal search queries, fail to locate gold references and settle for incom-plete secondary sources. 

Agent Foundation Capability Failures Opera-tional errors also derail tasks. Common issues in-clude Invalid Tool Calls (e.g., malformed queries) and Exceeding Max Context , often resulting from inefficient planning or error-correction loops. 

Compositional Reasoning Failures This category covers errors during the composition of retrieved information. (1) Faulty Composition : agents mis-interpret evidence or apply flawed reasoning, such as making unsupported assumptions, despite ac-cessing the correct sources (Figure D.1). (2) Error Accumulation : minor inaccuracies (e.g., prema-ture rounding) compound across steps to yield a significantly incorrect result (Figure D.3). (3) Inef-fective Refinement : a critical failure where agents fail to self-correct, proceeding down the erroneous logical path while being unable to revise previous mistakes (Figure D.2). The above failures are rarely isolated. As shown in Figure D.2, an initial Faulty Composition can propagate errors downstream, exacerbated by In-effective Refinement . This cascade consumes the context window ( Exceeding Max Steps ), increasing 24493 the probability of hallucinations or timeouts. 

5 Related Work 

Resources for Deep Research Agents Robust in-formation retrieval and precise question answering form the bedrock of deep research agents. Conse-quently, developing multi-hop datasets for training agent foundation models (Tongyi, 2025; Qiao et al., 2025) necessitates sophisticated tool-use and rea-soning, making manual construction challenging. Existing datasets like HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2022) lack the complexity of authen-tic web interactions. While some works synthesize request-action pairs (Xu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; He et al., 2024), they often lack goal-oriented depth. Recent methods construct tasks from logical flows over knowledge snippets (Wu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025a; Shi et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2025), such as using KGs from offline pages (Shi et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025a) or entity expansion (Xia et al., 2025; Tao et al., 2025). However, these remain lim-ited by static content and often neglect knowledge composition (Figure 3), where complexity stems more from reasoning than entity tracing. 

Benchmarking Deep Research Agents Most existing benchmarks prioritize information-seeking and reference filtering (Wu et al., 2025b; Wei et al., 2025), while few (Li et al., 2025c) assess compo-sitional reasoning. Krishna et al. (2025) evaluates aggregation but is confined to Wikipedia, and Wong et al. (2025) focuses on long sequences of simple ac-tions. GAIA (Mialon et al., 2023), though most rel-evant, is becoming saturated by recent agents (Fang et al., 2025b; Qiu et al., 2025). This underscores the need for challenging benchmarks that jointly evaluate information-seeking and complex reason-ing. 

Training and Optimization for Research Agents 

Recent advancements focus on sophisticated train-ing and test-time optimization. Besides standard rejection-sampling based SFT, which is adopted in most agent post-training works (Tongyi, 2025; Fang et al., 2025b; Team et al., 2025), there are more advanced techniques leveraging reinforce-ment learning and test-time self-evolving. In ad-dition to simply applying the training QA pairs as RL input queries, self-synthesized queries can be useful for providing process-level supervision sig-nals for better credit assignment (Fang et al., 2025b; Feng et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026; Fu et al., 2026). More specifi-cally, execution errors during tool interaction can also be converted into corrective supervision sig-nals within the RL loop to improve multi-turn tool robustness (Zhang et al., 2026). Regarding test-time self-evolving (Fang et al., 2025a), synthesized data could help building agent failure taxonomy and corresponding rubrics for better evaluating the generation of actions (Wan et al., 2026). 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce WebAggregator, a frame-work designed to bridge the gap between informa-tion seeking and compositional reasoning in Deep Research agents. Through the construction of We-bAggregatorQA and the WebAggregator models, we demonstrate that verifiable, reasoning-intensive training data significantly boosts agent performance across diverse benchmarks. Crucially, our analy-sis reveals a fundamental bottleneck: accurate re-trieval does not guarantee task success , as current models struggle to integrate distributed information. By mitigating these deficits through targeted fine-tuning, WebAggregator paves the way for future research to transcend retrieval-centric paradigms and move closer to autonomous scientific discovery. 

Limitations 

To ensure a fair and controlled comparison, all evaluation protocols and experimental details are strictly aligned with established methodologies (Wu et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025b; Tao et al., 2025), precluding any interference from prior knowledge. Following these standards, we evalu-ated WebAggregator across several well-recognized benchmarks (Mialon et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025) to demonstrate its generalization capabilities. Furthermore, we conducted a rigor-ous data contamination analysis on WebAggrega-torQA; as detailed in Appendix C, the results con-firm zero overlap with existing benchmarks. Given our automated pipeline, we implemented strict post-construction filtering and utilized a rejection sam-pling procedure during training to eliminate mis-aligned QA pairs, thereby guaranteeing high data quality. While the data construction of WebAggre-gator requires environmental interaction and execu-tion time, it remains highly cost-effective compared to human annotation, which often demands several hours of expert labor per task. B.1 More Explanations of Quality Control QA Alignment-based Filtering To improve data quality, we implemented a two-stage refinement process for task construction. The first stage uses a self-refinement tool with a checklist (Appendix B.5) to quickly verify and revise questions during cre-ation. Items meeting all criteria are accepted and outputted; those that don’t are revised based on feed-back until they comply. The second stage involves a data checking agent that thoroughly reviews all reference URLs to ensure alignment of question, an-swer, and sources (Appendix B.4). About 11.72% of the data were filtered out for failing to meet these standards. 

Diversity Constraint We ensure dataset diver-sity by balancing domain and aggregation opera-tion distributions. First, we annotated anchor URL domains with GPT-4.1 and sampled data to achieve a more balanced domain distribution, shown in Figure 4. Second, we analyzed compositional rea-soning types using GPT-4.1 to identify high- and low-level operations (e.g., math subtraction ). Al-though not perfectly reliable without solving the questions, common operations like calculating av-erage GDP can be accurately detected. We then ad-justed prompts to emphasize rare aggregation types, increasing their sample frequency. Data Leakage Avoidance During proactive web exploration, agents may download and parse exist-ing datasets. To prevent data contamination, we created a website keyword blacklist. Pages match-ing the blacklist or containing identified datasets were excluded from retrieval and subsequent model evaluation to ensure the fairness of the evaluation. 

B.2 Testset Annotation B.2.1 Data Collection and Verification 

The construction of the test set includes the seed tasks collection, several rounds of revision, and cross-validation procedures. Three human annota-tors are involved, all of them with at least a bache-lor’s degree. Each sample requires an average of 3 hours of annotation work, and the whole procedure lasts for more than 4 weeks of part-time work (4 hours a day). 

Seed Tasks Collection A single human annota-tor, even if highly specialized in one domain, faces inherent limitations in generating diverse and com-prehensive samples across multiple fields. To ad-dress this, we engage multiple annotators to revise 200 topic-diverse tasks, uniformly selected from WebAggregatorQA based on their domain labels. These carefully revised seed examples help ensure that the test set attains the desired diversity.

While this ambiguity may be tolera-ble for training, it is unacceptable for testing. All claims must be explicitly stated, and if multi-ple sources of evidence exist, additional constraints should be provided in the question to avoid ambi-guity.
