## DeepSynth-Eval: Objectively Evaluating Information Consolidation in Deep Survey Writing 

Hongzhi Zhang †*, Yuanze Hu ‡*, Tinghai Zhang †*, Jia Fu †*, Tao Wang †*,Junwei Jing †, Zhaoxin Fan ‡, Wei Bi †, Ruiming Tang † , Han Li †, Guorui Zhou †, Kun Gai †

Abstract 

The evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) towards autonomous agents has cat-alyzed progress in Deep Research. While re-trieval capabilities are well-benchmarked, the post-retrieval synthesis stage—where agents must digest massive amounts of context and consolidate fragmented evidence into coherent, long-form reports—remains under-evaluated due to the subjectivity of open-ended writing. To bridge this gap, we introduce DeepSynth-Eval, a benchmark designed to objectively evaluate information consolidation capabili-ties. We leverage high-quality survey papers as gold standards, reverse-engineering research requests and constructing "Oracle Contexts" from their bibliographies to isolate synthesis from retrieval noise. We propose a fine-grained evaluation protocol using General Checklists (for factual coverage) and Constraint Check-lists (for structural organization), transform-ing subjective judgment into verifiable metrics. Experiments across 96 tasks reveal that syn-thesizing information from hundreds of refer-ences remains a significant challenge. Our re-sults demonstrate that agentic “plan-then-write” workflows significantly outperform single-turn generation, effectively reducing hallucinations and improving adherence to complex structural constraints. 

1 Introduction 

The evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) marks a paradigm shift from fundamental token completion to sophisti-cated task completion via tool augmentation(Yao et al., 2023). This transition has notably accel-erated progress in Deep Research(OpenAI, 2025; Google, 2024; Shao et al., 2024), where agents are tasked with not only retrieving information but also

While Information Retrieval (IR) has robust benchmarks such as GAIA(Mialon et al., 2023), HLE(Phan et al., 2025) and others(Wei et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025), the latter stage—Post-Retrieval Synthesis—remains under-evaluated. In this stage, models must digest massive amounts of retrieved contexts, consolidate fragmented evidence, and or-ganize them into a coherent narrative. Evaluating this capability is challenging. Unlike short-form QA tasks like GAIA and HLE where answers can be checked against ground-truth facts, deep survey reports are long, lack a unique reference, and vary widely in content selection, structure, and writing style. Consequently, current practices rely heavily on subjective human judgments or LLM-as-a-judge scores(Zheng et al., 2023), which are costly, un-stable, and prone to reward hacking(Wang et al., 2024a). Furthermore, end-to-end evaluations of-ten conflate retrieval noise with synthesis failures, obscuring the model’s true ability to consolidate information. To objectively evaluate this information consol-idation capability, we propose to assess deep syn-thesis performance via survey papers. High-quality survey papers can be viewed as expert-written re-search reports that serve as “gold standards” of syn-thesis. For each survey, we first “reverse-engineer” a corresponding research request and construct the input context from its bibliography. We then manu-ally extract a set of checklists that specify what a good report must cover under this request, includ-ing representative methods, key design choices, im-portant datasets, and other domain-specific points. These checklists transform open-ended report eval-uation into verifying whether the model’s output accurately covers the required items, enabling a more objective measurement of completeness and factual correctness. Conse-quently, expecting a model to spontaneously repli-cate the exact structure of a reference survey is unreasonable. Given a model-generated report, our pipeline automatically aligns the content with checklist items to compute coverage, precision, and structural constraint satisfaction scores. Compared to directly judging unstructured free-form text, this key-point verification reduces the knowledge burden on judge models, supports fine-grained and discriminative scoring, and makes the evaluation more robust and repro-ducible—providing a reliable foundation for training and improving deep synthesis systems. Our main contributions are: • Objective Checklist Metric. We address the challenge of evaluating long-form gener-ation by introducing a checklist-based met-ric derived from gold-standard surveys. This design isolates post-retrieval synthesis from retrieval noise, ensuring reproducibility and preventing ground-truth leakage. • Evaluation and Open Source. We estab-lish a rigorous baseline by benchmarking end-to-end and agentic workflows. We find that while agentic methods dominate, synthesizing context from over 100 references remains a 33806 formidable bottleneck. We open-source the entire framework to accelerate research in this direction. 

2 Related Work 

The paradigm of LLMs is shifting from closed-ended tasks—exemplified by benchmarks like MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023), and Humanity’s Last Exam (Wang et al., 2024b)—to complex Deep Research . Unlike these static evaluations, Deep Research requires in-tegrating reading, searching, and writing. Existing works fall into three categories. 

Long-Context Understanding Serving as the cognitive foundation for deep research, Long-Context Understanding has garnered significant at-tention; however, current evaluations are often con-fined to information extraction rather than logical restructuring. Existing benchmarks, such as Long-Bench (Bai et al., 2024), InfiniteBench (Zhang et al., 2024), and L-Eval (An et al., 2023), pri-marily assess the model’s capacity for information retention within extended context windows. How-ever, the task formulations in these benchmarks are largely restricted to “Needle-in-a-Haystack” sce-narios or local extractive QA. In such settings, mod-els can typically succeed by locating and reciting specific segments within the context, without the necessity for global association or cross-document reasoning to synthesize unstructured evidence dis-persed across numerous documents. 

Agentic Retrieval and Fact-Finding Transition-ing from passive information processing to ac-tive information acquisition, Deep Search bench-marks (e.g., GAIA (Mialon et al., 2023), BrowseC-omp (Wei et al., 2025)) focus on evaluating an agent’s ability to utilize tools for solving complex problems. However, these evaluations typically involve low information throughput , where retriev-ing a few key pages suffices. Consequently, they fall short of measuring the comprehensive abil-ity to screen and synthesize massive information into coherent long-form reports. To address scale, WideSearch (Wong et al., 2025) targets breadth-oriented acquisition. Yet, despite the increased throughput, the task remains limited to Structured Information Extraction (e.g., compiling tables). This primarily tests recall in repetitive tasks, lack-ing the depth required for cross-document compar-ative analysis and the integration of heterogeneous information needed in deep research. 

End-to-End Research System Evaluations 

Deep Research tasks inherently require agents to complete the full workflow from open-web search-ing to long-form report generation (Search-Read-Write). To align with this real-world application scenario, recent works have adopted an end-to-end evaluation paradigm; however, this setting intro-duces confounding variables that are difficult to isolate. For instance, PDR-Bench (Liang et al., 2025) and DeepResearchBench (Du et al., 2025) focus on personalization alignment and LLM-based subjective scoring for complex tasks, respectively, whereas ReportBench (Li et al., 2025) assesses the Search-Read-Write capabilities of end-to-end sys-tems by reverse-engineering arXiv survey papers. Despite these significant explorations in system-level assessment, this open-web evaluation method-ology faces two major methodological challenges. The first challenge is the Coupling of Retrieval and Generation . The dynamic drift of rankings 

causes context variations over time, precipitating a 

reproducibility crisis where it is indistinguishable whether gains stem from model enhancements or better retrieval luck. Furthermore, retrieval noise (e.g., ads, broken links) prevents accurate measure-ment of the model’s intrinsic cognitive capability in processing complex information. The second is the 

Subjectivity of Evaluation . Existing methods pre-dominantly rely on LLMs to assign holistic scores (score-based) to reports, lacking objective verifi-cation of specific factual point coverage and re-maining susceptible to biases inherent in the judge models. 

3 Benchmark Construction 

We propose a benchmark construction framework that uses high-quality survey papers as reference answers. Formally, given a survey report R, we first reverse-engineer a query qgen for which R serves as an answer; we term this the General Question 

(qgen ) (e.g., “Summarize recent advances in reason-ing models”). From each survey, we extract two types of evaluation checklists. The first is the Gen-eral Checklist , which captures the core content that a comprehensive report should cover under 

qgen . Each item in this checklist corresponds to a specific, verifiable fact derived from the reference survey. The second is the Constraint Checklist ,which captures structural or organizational require-ments imposed on the report, such as a particular 33807 taxonomy, grouping principle, or presentation for-mat. Intuitively, one might evaluate a model by directly comparing the generated report ˆR =

M(qgen ) with the original survey R. In practice, however, directly comparing two long, unstruc-tured reports is both computationally expensive and metrically unreliable. Checklist-based evaluation provides a more tractable alternative by decompos-ing the reference survey into verifiable evaluation points. The distinction between the two checklist types is important because survey writing is in-herently subjective. Different authors may adopt equally valid but different strategies to organize the same body of literature. For example, bench-marks for Large Language Models (LLMs) may be categorized by capability domain (e.g., reading comprehension, mathematics, coding) or by task format (e.g., multiple-choice versus open-ended generation). As a result, structural organization is often not directly comparable unless the intended organizing principle is explicitly specified. To address this issue, we extract structural re-quirements from the reference survey and formu-late them as Constraint Questions (qcon ), which are appended to the general prompt. For exam-ple, we may require the model to organize effi-cient reasoning methods into three specific cate-gories: (1) Shortening Chain-of-Thought, (2) De-veloping Small LLMs with reasoning capabilities, and (3) Enhancing decoding efficiency. The as-sociated Constraint Checklists are used to eval-uate whether the generated report follows this prescribed organization. Importantly, Constraint Checklists are valid only when the corresponding constraint question qcon is provided. Without qcon ,alternative organizational strategies may still be rea-sonable and should not be penalized. By imposing 

qcon , we reduce the editorial freedom of the synthe-sis task and make structural aspects of the output objectively comparable. This design also enables a more rigorous evaluation of the model’s instruction-following ability in complex writing tasks. Overall, given a composite input consisting of the general question qgen and a set of constraint questions Qcon ,the model synthesizes a report based on the refer-ence bibliography of the original survey. An eval-uation system then verifies the generated report ˆR

against the full checklist set C = Cgen ∪ Ccon to determine whether each requirement is satisfied. 

3.1 Automated Construction Pipeline 

The construction of this writing benchmark itself could be very labor-consuming. In this paper, we utilize LLMs to generate candidates and human labor to review and cross-validate the dataset. To construct the benchmark efficiently while maintaining high quality, we employ a multi-stage generation pipeline utilizing LLMs. 

General Question Generation ( qgen ). This step is straightforward. We treat the survey title and abstract as the core intent and employ an LLM to directly reverse-engineer the corresponding re-search request. This produces the general question 

qgen (e.g., “Provide a comprehensive survey on ...”), which serves as the foundation of the task. 

General Checklist Generation ( Cgen ). Directly generating a comprehensive set of fine-grained checklists from a lengthy document is challeng-ing for current models, often resulting in low recall or hallucinations. To mitigate this, we adopt a hier-archical Topic Anchor strategy. First, we prompt the LLM to synthesize several distinct topics based on the survey’s section headers and content (e.g., “Core Algorithms”, “Evaluation Metrics”, “Future Directions”). Then, using these topics as anchors, we invoke the LLM to generate specific check-list items for each topic individually. This divide-and-conquer approach yields K groups of general checklists, denoted as Cgen = ⋃Kk=1 Cgen ,k .

Constraint Pair Generation ( Qcon , C con ). For structural constraints, we employ a Structure An-chor approach to generate coupled pairs of con-straint questions and checklists. 1. Anchor Extraction: The model first scans the survey to identify key organizational ele-ments, such as specific taxonomies (classifica-tion schemes) or summary tables. 2. Guidance Generation ( q(m)con ): Based on an extracted anchor (e.g., a table summarizing reasoning datasets), the model generates a 

Constraint Question q(m)con that acts as ex-plicit guidance. For instance, if the anchor is a dataset table, q(m)con might be: “List a table summarizing recent reasoning datasets, spec-ifying their data scale, question source, and answer source.” 

3. Checklist Extraction: Simultaneously, the model extracts the corresponding cell val-ues or classification criteria from the anchor to form a group of Constraint Checklists 

Ccon ,m .33808 This process yields M groups of constraint check-lists, denoted as Ccon = ⋃Mm=1 Ccon ,m .

3.2 Human Verification 

We construct DeepSynth-Eval using a hybrid strat-egy that combines expert curation and assisted ex-pansion, where each survey paper defines exactly one evaluation task. We first asked computer sci-ence experts to identify 60 representative survey papers, then expanded this seed set through an auto-mated arXiv-based retrieval pipeline, yielding 322 candidate tasks. For each survey, we collected its bibliography and constructed the Oracle Context using struc-tured metadata from the Semantic Scholar API, supplemented by a local index from the Hugging-Face arXiv dataset; long-tail references were re-covered by parsing PDFs with Qwen3-235B and matching titles through the arXiv API. We then conducted a two-stage human verifica-tion process with six computer science experts, re-taining 129 candidates after the first round and 96 fi-nal benchmark instances after cross-validation and refinement. During screening, we identified and discarded problematic instances characterized by two common issues: (1) over-specification, where constraints made checklist items trivially inferable; and (2) contextual ambiguity, especially in table-based extractions lacking sufficient textual context for verification. 

3.3 Task Formalization 

Formally, we define a deep synthesis evaluation task in DeepSynth-Eval as a tuple T = ( P, D, C ),where: • P denotes the Unified Input Prompt . It is constructed by concatenating the general ques-tion qgen with a set of M constraint questions 

{q(1) con , . . . , q (M )con }. Formally: 

P = qgen ⊕ q(1) con ⊕ · · · ⊕ q(M )con 

where ⊕ represents the string concatenation operation. • D represents the Oracle Context , constructed from the reference bibliography associated with the original survey. This serves as the closed-world knowledge boundary for the task. • C is the Evaluation Standard , composed of the union of general checklist groups and con-straint checklist groups: C = Cgen ∪ Ccon .

4 Experiments and Evaluation 

4.1 Evaluation Settings 

We aim to evaluate the deep synthesis capability of models, formalized as ˆR = M(P, D ). As ob-served in Figure 3, the total length of the refer-ence materials underlying the Oracle Context D

exceeds 1 million words for a significant portion of tasks. Since the context window of most main-stream LLMs falls below 256k tokens—with only a few extending to 1 million—directly feeding the full context often results in context overflow. Nevertheless, we believe that direct reading of such massive contexts is a challenging task that holds significant value for benchmarking emerging long-context capabilities and memory mechanisms. Unlike standard reading comprehension, which often relies on retrieving specific “needles in a haystack” (Liu et al., 2024; Hsieh et al., 2024), our report generation task requires high information throughput, making it a more challenging testbed for global context understanding. Qwen3-235B and DeepSeek-V3.2 form a middle tier with sim-ilar Overall scores (24.8 and 23.4), but without a corresponding advantage on Constraint (23.3 and 27.0), indicating that their main bottleneck lies in 

organizing and covering required content rather than in factuality once content is produced. In con-trast, Qwen3-30B largely collapses in the single-turn regime (Overall 6.3; General 4.8), consistent with Figure 4, where most requirements fall into the not-mentioned category, highlighting a strong capacity dependence for long-context consolida-tion. 

Workflow effect: agentic multi-turn improves synthesis quality. Switching from single-turn generation to the agentic multi-turn workflow con-sistently improves Overall and General across all evaluated models: Qwen3-30B increases from 6.3 →17.3, Qwen3-235B from 24.8 →35.5, and GPT-5.2 from 28.3 →33.3. We note that GPT-5.2 often fails to rewrite the full text during polishing, instead referring to the previous draft (Appendix E); excluding these invalid responses reveals a higher Overall score of 37.0 (Table 1). We then perform a finer-grained distributional analysis over tasks to go beyond mean scores. As shown in Figure 5, the agentic workflow shifts a larger portion of tasks into higher score bins than E2E, indicating more consistent improvements across tasks rather than gains driven by a small sub-set. To further inspect subtle workflow differences, we analyze the checklist requirement status com-position under the same model. Figure 6 shows that the agentic workflow increases the fraction of correctly satisfied requirements while reducing in-correct ones, suggesting that staging the process as plan →reading →write improves grounding and reduces unsupported completions. Overall, these results suggest that the agentic multi-turn workflow is generally beneficial for long-form synthesis: it helps models better allocate attention over full ref-erence passages, produce more faithful content, and consequently achieve higher checklist pass rates in aggregate.

To complement checklist pass rates, we conduct a qualitative case analysis to identify recurring fail-ure modes and to understand how workflow design affects post-retrieval synthesis behaviors. 

Failure modes under the E2E single-turn work-flow. Across low-scoring models, a common pat-tern is under-elaboration : instead of consolidating as many relevant points as possible from the Ora-cle Context, the model produces a high-level but sparse narrative that omits many checklist items. This behavior disproportionately harms the General score, as required coverage is missed due to insuf-ficient detail and weak synthesis effort. Moreover, under constrained requirements, these models not only omit required organizational elements (e.g., missing categories/attributes) but also more fre-quently introduce unsupported statements when at-tempting to satisfy constraints, leading to apparent “hallucination”-like outputs (i.e., claims or compar-isons not grounded in the provided materials). 

5 Conclusion 

We introduce DeepSynth-Eval to address the lack of objective benchmarks for long-form report gen-eration. Instead of relying on subjective holis-tic scoring, we ground our evaluation in atomic, verifiable checklists derived from expert surveys. On average, each evaluation task involves 199.7 checklist items, providing a granular lens to quan-tify the quality of open-ended generation. Be-yond the dataset, we establish a foundational eval-uation framework by implementing and testing diverse baselines, ranging from standard end-to-end workflows to sophisticated agentic systems. Experiments demonstrate that even with agentic workflows, state-of-the-art (SOTA) models achieve scores of only around 37%, highlighting the chal-lenging nature of this task and the significant room for improvement. Furthermore, this verifiable checklist mechanism paves the way for designing precise reward signals in long-form generation, of-fering a promising avenue for future reinforcement learning optimization. 

Limitations 

Currently, our dataset is predominantly composed of computer science surveys, a choice driven by data accessibility and annotation feasibility. However, the proposed checklist-based evaluation methodology is domain-agnostic and holds the po-tential to be extended to broader scientific disci-plines.

In this section, we provide a step-by-step textual de-scription of the two synthesis workflows presented in Section 4.1, as illustrated in Figure 7. 

A.1 Monolithic E2E Single-turn Workflow 

The E2E workflow (Figure 7a) is designed to test the model’s ability to perform long-context consoli-dation in a single pass. The process consists of two main steps: 1. Oracle Context Construction: First, we par-allelize the processing of all bibliography en-tries. Each reference paper di is compressed into a dense prose summary si using a ded-icated summarization prompt. These sum-maries are then concatenated to form the Or-acle Context . Crucially, each summary is prefixed with a stable numeric citation identi-fier (e.g., “[1]”, “[2]”) to allow the model to ground its claims explicitly. 2. One-Shot Generation: We construct a uni-fied prompt that combines the reconstructed research request, the extracted constraint ques-tions, and the Oracle Context . The model is then instructed to generate the full survey re-port in a single inference pass. No intermedi-ate reasoning steps or external retrieval actions are permitted; the model must rely entirely on its internal attention mechanism to organize and synthesize the provided summaries. 

A.2 Agentic Multi-turn Workflow 

The Agentic workflow (Figure 7b) decomposes the synthesis task to mimic the plan-read-write process of a human researcher. It operates in three phases to ensure better organization and grounding: 

Phase 1: Global Planning (Intellectual Skele-ton). Instead of writing immediately, the model acts as an “Architect.” It analyzes the Oracle Con-text (containing summaries) to construct an Intel-lectual Skeleton . This skeleton is a high-level plan that defines the section taxonomy, identifies key comparison axes, and allocates specific content to each section. This plan is then parsed into a struc-tured list of sub-tasks (e.g., JSON objects), where each task corresponds to writing one section of the report. 

Phase 2: Serial Iterative Writing Loop. The workflow executes a loop for each sub-task derived from the skeleton. For each section k, the process involves three specialized steps: 1. Relevance Selection: The model evaluates the requirements of the current section against the reference bibliography to identify a subset of critical papers (represented by IDs) that are necessary for drafting this specific part. 2. Selective Deep Reading: For the identified critical papers, the agent retrieves their full text from the underlying reference collection. It then generates detailed reading notes fo-cused specifically on the current section’s topic. This step allows the model to access fine-grained details that might have been lost in the initial summaries, without overflowing the context window with all papers at once. 3. Context-Aware Writing: The model writes the section. The generation is conditioned on three inputs: (1) the Intellectual Skeleton (for structure), (2) the Deep Reading Notes (for de-tails), and (3) the Writing History (the draft of previous sections). This ensures the new sec-tion is logically coherent with the preceding text. 

Phase 3: Final Polish. Once all sections are drafted, they are concatenated. An “Editor” agent performs a final pass to ensure global consistency, unify terminology, and format the document before outputting the final report.

# Retrieval-Augmented Generation: Foundations, Methods, Multimodal Extensions, Systems, and Evaluation ## 1. Introduction and Formalization Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) provides a principled way to couple the parametric knowl-edge stored inside large language models with non-parametric, dynamically updatable external knowledge through retrieval and context curation. We formalize a generic RAG pipeline as y = G(x, C(R(x, K))), where x denotes the user query, R is a retriever that selects evidence from a knowledge base K, C is a context curation function that filters, orders, compresses, or structures the retrieved content for the generator, and G is the generator that produces the final output conditioned on both x and curated context. This formulation encompasses common operational regimes, including open-book and closed-book usage; single-hop and multi-hop question answering; and iterative procedures in which retrieval and generation interleave or recur. Treating retrieval outputs as latent variables that are marginalized at decoding time connects RAG to probabilistic formulations introduced in early hybrid models, while instantiations like Fusion-in-Decoder characterize spe-cific architectural choices for integrating multiple retrieved passages into sequence-to-sequence generation [133, 125]. ...
