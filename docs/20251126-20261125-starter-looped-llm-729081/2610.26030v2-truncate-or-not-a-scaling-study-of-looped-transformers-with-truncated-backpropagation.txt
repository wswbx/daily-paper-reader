Looped transformers reuse a shared block of layers to increase computational depth without increasing
the number of trainable parameters. They are trained with backpropagation through time (BPTT).
To save training compute, BPTT is often truncated to the last few loops, but truncation yields biased
gradients that may weaken the benefit of looping. In this work, we study the value of truncation in
looped transformer training in a systematic way: we jointly vary model size, training tokens, and
forward and backward loop counts across nearly 600 training runs and fit a unified scaling law that
captures the effects of looping and truncation through effective parameters. Despite the training-
compute savings of truncation, our fitted law favors full BPTT when forward and backward loop
counts are jointly optimized. In matched-compute runs, full BPTT through fewer loops consistently
outperforms truncated settings of the same compute. The law further shows that at fixed model size,
the compute-optimal loop count and training-token budget both grow with compute. When model
size is also free, the law identifies two fully trained loops as the best looped setting. Experimentally,
this setting achieves lower validation loss than the compute-optimal non-looped model with about
27% fewer non-embedding parameters and only 8% more training compute.

Introduction
Looped transformers have attracted attention for combining strong performance with efficient use
of parameters and training memory. By repeatedly applying a shared block of layers, they increase
computational depth without increasing the number of trainable parameters (Dehghani et al., 2019;
Geiping et al., 2025). To reduce the cost of training these repeated blocks, several looped language
models use truncated backpropagation through time (BPTT), propagating gradients only through
the final loops while executing the earlier loops without tracking gradients (Geiping et al., 2025;
Huang et al., 2026b; McLeish et al., 2025; Prairie et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a). Truncation
introduces a new tradeoff: it saves backward computation and activation memory during training
but can weaken the gains from looping (Schwethelm et al., 2026). To achieve the best performance
under a fixed training compute budget, we must therefore decide how to divide compute among
forward loops, backward loops, model size, and training tokens.
Existing scaling studies examine prescribed backpropagation recipes rather than jointly modeling
forward and backward loop counts. Schwethelm et al. (2026) compare full BPTT with truncation
to half the loops, fitting a separate scaling law to each recipe. Parcae (Prairie et al., 2026) varies
backward-loop counts in small-model experiments but fixes them to half the forward-loop count in
its scaling study, leaving their joint optimization to future work. These studies characterize specific
recipes but do not provide a unified law for choosing both loop counts across model sizes and token
budgets (Table 1). We thus ask the following question:
How do model size, training tokens, and forward and backward loops jointly affect loss,
and should we truncate backpropagation under a fixed training compute budget?
To characterize how these quantities affect loss, we conduct nearly 600 training runs, including a
scaling suite spanning 58M–919M non-embedding parameters, over three orders of magnitude in
training tokens per parameter, and up to 16 forward loops. We independently vary forward and
backward loop counts across model sizes and token budgets and fit a unified scaling law. The
scaling law expresses the benefit of looping through an effective parameter count—the size of a
non-looped model that achieves the same loss at the same token budget. Fully trained loops increase
the effective parameter count with diminishing returns, while truncation discounts this gain.
We then use the scaling law to decide whether to truncate, and the answer depends on whether the
forward-loop count is prescribed. When it is, the law recovers the benefit of truncation reported in
prior work (Schwethelm et al., 2026): the saved backward compute supports a larger model and
more training tokens, which more than offset the discounted loop gain. When both loop counts
can be chosen, however, the law favors full BPTT. Reducing the forward-loop count frees compute
for additional backward loops until every remaining loop is trained, and under the fitted law this
reallocation yields more effective parameters at the same compute than the original truncated
setting. Separately fitted laws and matched-compute experiments support this advantage (§5).
Building on this preference for full BPTT, we additionally study how to allocate compute among loop
count, training tokens, and model size. Consider first a setting where model size is predetermined,
for example due to device memory constraints. At fixed model size, increasing the loop count is an
effective way to increase the effective parameter count; however, additional loops offer diminishing
gains while increasing the cost per token. The law precisely characterizes the optimal tradeoff
between loop count and training tokens, allocating most additional compute to tokens and increasing
the loop count only slowly. This result extends Parcae’s fixed-size allocation analysis (Prairie
et al., 2026) beyond a prescribed truncation rule. When model size is also free, compute can
instead support more unique parameters; under our leading-FLOP approximation, the law favors a

Our contributions are threefold:
• A unified scaling law based on effective parameters. We develop and validate a law
that expresses loop gains and truncation discounts through an effective parameter count, jointly
modeling model size, training tokens, and independent forward and backward loop counts (§4).
• An explanation of when truncation pays. We show why truncation can pay at a prescribed
forward-loop count and why joint forward–backward allocation favors full BPTT under the
fitted law, supported by matched-compute experiments (§5).
• Compute-allocation rules and parameter-efficient training. We derive allocation rules
for fixed and free model size and demonstrate that two fully trained loops nearly match compute-
optimal non-looped models while having substantially fewer trainable parameters (§6).

at fixed model size the best loop count grows with compute; two fully trained loops match the loss
of a non-looped model with fewer parameters for a little more compute.
Related work
Looped transformersDepth-wise parameter sharing in Universal Transformers and ALBERT
decouples computational depth from the number of trainable parameters (Dehghani et al., 2019; Lan
et al., 2019). Theoretical and controlled studies show how recurrence supports iterative programs,
in-context learning algorithms, and depth-sensitive reasoning (Giannou et al., 2023; Saunshi et al.,

2025; Yang et al., 2024). In language model pretraining, Huginn uses a prelude–recurrent–coda
architecture (Geiping et al., 2025), while Ouro scales looped pretraining to 7.7T tokens (Zhu et al.,
2025). Other work adds recurrence to pretrained models (Bae et al., 2025a; McLeish et al., 2025)
or adapts depth at inference (Bae et al., 2025b; Jeddi et al., 2026). Recent architectures improve
recurrence through residual and hyper-connections (Fu et al., 2026; Marchenko et al., 2026; Prairie
et al., 2026; Zeitoun et al., 2026) or MoE routing (Chen et al., 2026a; Csordás et al., 2024; Gao
et al., 2026; He et al., 2026). These studies show that looping improves parameter efficiency across
architectures; we instead fix the architecture and ask how to train its loops and allocate compute.
Training through recurrent depthTruncated BPTT limits gradient propagation to a subset of
recurrent steps, saving activation memory and backward compute at the cost of biased gradients
(Tallec and Ollivier, 2017; Williams and Peng, 1990). It is the common recipe for looped language
models, which backpropagate through the last few loops or half of them (Geiping et al., 2025;
McLeish et al., 2025; Prairie et al., 2026; Schwethelm et al., 2026), and recursive reasoning models
often keep only the last step (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025; Wang et al., 2025). §A surveys these
recipes in more detail. Implicit models likewise differentiate only a few steps at the fixed point (Bai
et al., 2019; Geng et al., 2021b), and truncated BPTT in looped transformers can be read as a
truncated Neumann series of the fixed-point gradient (Huang et al., 2026b; Movahedi et al., 2026).
The few studies that measure what truncation costs compare training recipes at a fixed forward
depth (Fein-Ashley and Rashidinejad, 2026; Huang et al., 2026a; Ren and Liu, 2026; Salvatori et al.,
2026). We instead vary the backward depth independently of the forward depth, weighing the
compute that truncation saves against the loop gain it costs.
Scaling laws for recurrenceSize–data scaling laws quantify how parameters and training tokens
jointly determine loss (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020; Videau et al., 2026). For looped
models, some laws fit each architecture separately (Chen et al., 2026c; Lee et al., 2026; Wang et al.,
2026b), while others model the loop count explicitly (Table 1) through additive loss terms (Zhu et al.,
2025) or effective parameters with linear, power-law, or bounded gains (Chen et al., 2026b; Prairie
et al., 2026; Schwethelm et al., 2026). Closest to our work, Iso-Depth (Schwethelm et al., 2026) fits
separate laws for full and half-length backpropagation and finds that truncation weakens the loop
gain but lowers the loss at a fixed forward-loop count by freeing compute, while Parcae (Prairie
et al., 2026) finds at fixed model size that loops and tokens should grow together with compute but
ties the backward loops to half the forward loops and leaves their joint choice open. Our law instead
treats the backward-loop count as a separate variable, combining coupled size–data scaling (Videau
et al., 2026) with bounded loop gains (Chen et al., 2026b). It recovers the truncation benefit at a
prescribed forward-loop count but favors full BPTT when both counts are free (§5). Looped MoE
models commonly use two loops (Gao et al., 2026; He et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). For dense
models, our law ranks two fully trained loops first among looped settings, since they provide nearly
the effective size of two untied copies (§6.2), and in our runs they nearly match compute-optimal
models without looping with substantially fewer non-embedding parameters (§6.3).

Truncated BPTTA loop setting (T, B) with 1 ≤B ≤T runs T loops forward and backpropagates
through only the last B: the state sT−B is detached (Figure 3a), so the first T −B loops carry no
gradient and store no activations. The setting B = T is full BPTT, B < T is truncated BPTT,
and (1, 1) is a standard transformer. The recurrent weights θ and, through the input injection, the
prelude receive only the gradient of the last B loops. The coda’s gradient is exact. Evaluation always
runs all T loops. A backward loop costs twice the FLOPs of a forward loop (§B), so truncation
trades backward compute and activation memory for a biased gradient (Tallec and Ollivier, 2017;
Williams and Peng, 1990). §B.2 writes out both gradients and discusses this bias.

A scaling law for forward and backward loops
Should a looped transformer truncate backpropagation? At a fixed model size and training compute,
truncation saves backward computation that can be spent on more forward loops or more training
data, but it also reduces the benefit of looping. We fit a scaling law that separates forward and
backward loop counts to quantify this tradeoff. This section fits and validates the law and interprets
it in terms of effective parameters. §5 then uses the law to decide whether to truncate, and §6 uses
it to divide compute among loops, data, and model size.

increasing their storage capacity. We therefore treat saturation as an empirical modeling assumption
and learn its ceiling from the data.
These requirements motivate the form in (4.1). Nonnegativity motivates an exponential parameteri-
zation, ensuring g(T, B) > 0 for T, B > 0. With c, p, q > 0, increasing either loop count decreases
T −p · B−q, so g(T, B) grows toward the finite ceiling exp(c). The shared coefficient c enforces
g(1, 1) = 1, and continuous extension as B →0+ gives g(T, 0) = 0. At fixed model size and token
budget, p governs the loss reduction from extra forward loops at fixed B, while q governs the loss
reduction from extra backward loops at fixed T. These three loop coefficients, c, p, q, together with
the six coefficients E, A, H, α, β, κ of the coupled size–data law (3.2), give nine fitted coefficients.

Illustrating the scaling law with effective parameters
How many parameters is a loop setting worth? (4.1) allows us to decompose the answer into the
gain from fully trained loops, g(T, T), and the discount for truncation, g(T, B)/g(T, T). We use the
empirical looping coefficients introduced above to compare the law with the runs in Figure 9; their
construction is detailed in §C.2.
The looping coefficient saturates for large T With full BPTT, B = T, and the gain is g(T, T) =
exp[c · (1 −T −(p+q))]. The runs in Figure 8a vary both loop counts together at fixed model size and
token budget. Each doubling of trained loops adds less than the one before (Figures 8a and 9a), as
Chen et al. (2026b) also find with all loops trained. The power-law gain of prior work (Schwethelm
et al., 2026) keeps growing, whereas ours approaches ec. Our runs support the bounded form over
the power law, predicting held-out forward-loop counts more accurately (§C.4).
Doubling B outweighs doubling T aloneAt fixed T, truncation reduces B and hence g(T, B),
consistent with the smaller effective sizes in Figure 9b and higher losses in Figure 8b. Figure 9c
clarifies the relative roles of T and B by grouping settings with the same B. The fitted bars and
empirical estimates change relatively little as T increases within a group, but show larger gains as
B increases across groups, including comparisons at the same T. The fitted exponents quantify this
asymmetry: q > p (Table 4) implies g(T, 2B) > g(2T, B) for 2B ≤T. From the same initial setting
at fixed model size, doubling B thus adds more effective parameters than doubling T alone; the
small loss gains from extra forward loops at fixed B support the same conclusion (Figure 8c).

trained loop adds effective parameters, with diminishing returns toward a finite ceiling.
Truncation discounts this gain, and from the same setting, doubling B adds more effective
parameters than doubling T (Figure 9c).

Free forward-loop countfull BPTT is optimal
When T can also be chosen, we optimize the split between forward and backward loops at equal
compute. Fix the model architecture and token count D. In our FLOP accounting, each forward
pass through the recurrent block costs one unit and each backward pass costs two (§D.1). The
recurrent block thus costs T + 2B units per token; all other model components have the same
cost across loop settings. Holding T + 2B fixed therefore holds total compute fixed: for example,
(T, B) = (4, 4) and (8, 2) both cost 12 recurrent units per token.

looping, well beyond the spread across seeds, while it still has 27% fewer non-embedding parameters
(Figure 15; Table 11). Appendix E also compares the looped model with an untied model, which
gives each loop its own block, at a fixed number of training tokens.

Conclusion
Truncated BPTT saves compute, but whether it lowers the loss depends on which quantities are
held fixed. We fit a scaling law that expresses every loop setting as effective parameters, with a
gain that saturates and shrinks under truncation (§4). Under this law, truncation helps only when

the forward-loop count is prescribed. When both loop counts are chosen, full BPTT is optimal at
every model size and compute we analyze (§5).
The law also determines how to allocate compute. At fixed model size, most additional compute
should go to data, and the number of fully trained loops grows only as about C0.24 (§6.1). When
model size is free, the law favors no looping, but two fully trained loops come close: with 27–32%
fewer non-embedding parameters they nearly match the compute-optimal non-looped model at
equal compute, and with 27% fewer parameters they achieve lower loss at 8% more compute (§6.3).
LimitationsOur main limitation is the focus on dense models: we do not study mixture-of-experts
(MoE) architectures, so whether our conclusions on truncation and compute allocation extend to
looped MoE models remains open. Second, we measure validation loss only and do not establish
whether the observed gains translate to downstream task performance or reasoning ability. Third,
our experiments are capped at T ≤16, so the gain ceiling and later switches to more loops remain
extrapolations. Related scaling sweeps reach eight loops in Schwethelm et al. (2026) and mean
training loop counts of twelve in Parcae (Prairie et al., 2026). Much deeper recurrence can exacerbate
training instability, including residual explosion and loss spikes (Fu et al., 2026; Prairie et al., 2026);
developing stable training recipes for that regime is beyond our scope. Finally, we fix the ratio of
non-embedding parameters in the combined prelude and coda to those in the recurrent block for
each model size. Varying this ratio to study how parameter allocation affects the gains from looping
and the compute-optimal loop counts is a direction for future work.

A Extended related work
Training through recurrent depthTruncated BPTT limits gradient propagation to a subset of
recurrent steps, saving activation memory and backward compute at the cost of biased gradients
(Tallec and Ollivier, 2017; Williams and Peng, 1990). Looped language models often supervise the
final output and backpropagate through only the last few loops. Huginn and retrofitted models
use at most eight backward steps (Geiping et al., 2025; McLeish et al., 2025), Parcae and Iso-
Depth backpropagate through half of the loops (Prairie et al., 2026; Schwethelm et al., 2026),
URM runs the first loops without gradients (Gao et al., 2025), and looped MoE models supervise
within recurrent segments (He et al., 2026). The same truncation trains looped models converted
from pretrained language models (Park et al., 2026), recurrent-depth models for robot control
and dynamics forecasting (Lai et al., 2026; Tur et al., 2026), and looped refinement in diffusion
language models (Sun et al., 2026). Recursive reasoning models use even shorter backward horizons.
Hierarchical recurrent models backpropagate through only the final steps (Han, 2026; Wang et al.,
2025, 2026a), and TRM and its successors run all but the last recursion without gradients (Asadulaev
et al., 2025; Baek et al., 2026; Freinschlag et al., 2026; Hakimi, 2026; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025;
Nejati Manzari et al., 2026; Salvatori et al., 2026; Sghaier et al., 2026). For algorithmic extrapolation,
recurrent networks and looped transformers run a random number of iterations without gradients
before the supervised ones, or compute the loss over a window of the last loops (Bansal et al.,
2022; Labovich, 2026; Yang et al., 2024). Implicit models replace backpropagation through the
solver with implicit differentiation (Bai et al., 2019) or with one-step, Jacobian-free, and phantom
gradients that differentiate only a few steps at the fixed point (Geng et al., 2021a,b; Wu Fung et al.,
2022). Such approximations train implicit models for optical flow, diffusion, and language modeling
(Bai et al., 2022; Bai and Melas-Kyriazi, 2024; Fein-Ashley and Rashidinejad, 2026; Pokle et al.,
2022; Schöne et al., 2025), and truncated BPTT in looped transformers can be read as a truncated
Neumann series of the fixed-point gradient (Huang et al., 2026b; Movahedi et al., 2026). Few studies
measure what truncation costs. Fein-Ashley and Rashidinejad (2026) find that the one-step gradient
nearly matches the perplexity of the exact implicit gradient at a fraction of its memory, Huang et al.
(2026a) find that truncation lowers or raises Sudoku accuracy depending on the latent structure,
Salvatori et al. (2026) find that the required gradient window grows with the propagation distance
of the graph, and Ren and Liu (2026) argue that the one-step gradient delays the stabilization of
hierarchical reasoning models. These studies compare training recipes at a fixed forward depth; we
vary the backward depth independently of the forward depth, weighing the compute that truncation
saves against the loop gain it costs.
Scaling laws for recurrenceSize–data scaling laws quantify how parameters and training tokens
jointly determine loss (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020; Videau et al., 2026), and their
compute-optimal predictions depend on how the laws are fitted and compute is counted (Besiroglu
et al., 2024; Choshen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Pearce and Song, 2024; Porian et al., 2024).
Extensions account for architectural choices such as MoE sparsity (Abnar et al., 2025; Clark et al.,
2022; Krajewski et al., 2024). For looped models, some laws fit each architecture separately (Chen
et al., 2026c; Lee et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b), while others model the loop count explicitly
(Table 1) through additive loss terms (Zhu et al., 2025) or effective parameters with linear, power-law,
or bounded gains (Chen et al., 2026b; Prairie et al., 2026; Schwethelm et al., 2026). Among these
scaling-law studies of looped transformers, Iso-Depth (Schwethelm et al., 2026) and Parcae (Prairie
et al., 2026) are particularly related to our work. Iso-Depth fits separate laws for full and half-length
backpropagation and finds that truncation weakens the loop gain but lowers the loss at a fixed
forward-loop count by freeing compute. At fixed model size, Parcae finds that loops and tokens
should grow together with compute, but ties the backward loops to half the forward loops and leaves

their joint choice open. Our law instead treats the backward-loop count as a separate variable,
combining coupled size–data scaling (Videau et al., 2026) with bounded loop gains (Chen et al.,

when both counts are free (§5). In looped MoE models, two loops are the common choice (He et al.,

two-loop models nearly match compute-optimal models without looping at equal compute while
having substantially fewer non-embedding parameters (§6.3).

BModel architecture
Figure 3 draws the model of §3 for the loop setting (4, 2). In a training step (Figure 3a), the prelude
maps the tokens to s0, every loop reads s0 again through the input injection, and the state sT−B
entering the last B loops is treated as a constant. The gradient therefore reaches the coda, the last B
loops and, through the injection, the prelude; the first T −B loops run forward only, and evaluation
runs all T loops. Each loop (Figure 3b) applies Winj to [ s0 ; st−1 ], the ℓr pre-norm transformer
layers of R and an RMSNorm, with the same weights in every loop. At 2 FLOPs per parameter and

the error of a single gradient, whereas this bias enters every update, and how it accumulates over
training and shapes the final model is not known. Prior measurements compare recipes at a fixed
forward-loop count (§2) and so cannot separate the compute that truncation saves from the loop
gain it costs. We therefore measure the net effect directly: the scaling law of §4 asks whether the
saved compute outweighs this bias in the loss that training actually reaches.
BExperimental setup
ModelsWe take the architecture and the training recipe of Schwethelm et al. (2026), the reference
recipe below, which builds on the prelude–recurrent–coda layout of Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025) (§3).
Its blocks use pre-norm attention with query–key normalization and rotary position embeddings,
head dimension 128, a squared-ReLU feed-forward layer of width 4d, no biases, and untied input
and output embeddings. We change only what is held fixed. Schwethelm et al. (2026) hold the
executed depth fixed, so that more loops mean fewer unique layers; we hold the unique parameters
fixed, as Prairie et al. (2026) do, so that a loop setting changes the compute and not the model.
The main grid spans five model sizes, from 58M to 919M non-embedding parameters (Table 2);
width and depth grow together, and the recurrent block with the input injection holds 51% of the
non-embedding parameters at four sizes and 56% at 120M. Short size names round N; including
the untied input and output embeddings, total parameter counts run from about 107M to about
1.06B. We quote budgets per parameter as D/N. The scaling suite supplements this grid with two
compute-matched pairs, adding the 79M and 162M models at one configuration each (§E).

Functional formThe looping coefficient is

The normalization is exact: g(1, 1) = 1, so (T, B) = (1, 1) gives Neff = N. For c, p, q > 0, g(T, B)

is an extrapolation, not an observed asymptotic limit. With full BPTT the looping coefficient

r = B/T, which approaches one as T grows.

D Training compute and allocation
We first define the training-compute cost, then discuss the fixed-size switch points and their
experimental checks. §D.4 gives the allocation derivations for prescribed forward loops, joint loop
counts, and fixed or free model size.
DTraining compute
Training compute counts the FLOPs of the forward and backward passes.
We use the usual
convention of 2 FLOPs per parameter and token for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass
(Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020). The prelude and coda run forward and backward once
per token. Each forward loop runs the recurrent block forward once, and each backward loop adds
a backward pass through it, which costs twice as much. The compute of the passes per token is
F(T, B) ≈6Nonce + (2T + 4B) Nrec + Fattn(T, B) + Fout = 6Ncomp + Fattn(T, B) + Fout, (D.1)
where Fattn counts the attention products of every executed layer and Fout the output projection.4
The attention products of the recurrent block follow the same count, one unit per forward loop and
two more per backward loop, so every loop-dependent term grows with T + 2B, as in the accounting
of truncated BPTT by Prairie et al. (2026) and Schwethelm et al. (2026); for a given model, (2, 2)
and (4, 1) therefore cost the same per token, as do (4, 4) and (8, 2).
For a model of size N we write this compute per token as FN(T, B) = uN + vN(T + 2B), where uN
collects the prelude, coda and output and vN > 0 one forward pass through the recurrent block
with its attention, so that D training tokens cost
C ≃FN(T, B) D.
(D.2)
At fixed N and D, the compute depends on the loop counts only through T +2B. (5.1) is its leading
term, without the attention and output costs.
The optimizer update is negligibleMuonH also spends FLOPs on each update: five Newton–Schulz
iterations per weight matrix (Jordan et al., 2024), a cost Fopt per step that depends on the model
but not on T or B, so that a run of nstep steps costs
C = F(T, B) D + Fopt nstep.
(D.3)
Across our runs the update adds 0.06–5.7% to the compute of the passes. It cannot change the
split between forward and backward loops, since it does not depend on either; leaving it out moves
the fixed-size switch points of §6.1 by less than 0.5%, and it gives the models with more trainable
parameters, the untied model and the model without looping at its compute-optimal size, 0.5–0.75%
more compute, or about 0.001 nats in loss (§E). Like prior scaling studies (Prairie et al., 2026;
Schwethelm et al., 2026), our analysis therefore omits it and uses (D.2); the measured compute in
figures, tables and §B.3 includes it unless stated otherwise.

therefore requires an explicit architectural scaling family and a fresh numerical envelope. Its results
should be reported separately from the analytic ratios, using the same adopted fit.

EThe untied model
The untied model of a looped model is the same network in which each loop has its own copy of the
recurrent block, input injection and normalization. For two loops it holds Nonce + 2Nrec parameters,
about 1.5 times as many, at the same compute per token. Tying layers to save parameters goes
back to ALBERT (Lan et al., 2019), and Bae et al. (2025a) relax the tie of looped models converted
from pretrained ones. Each copy is initialized like the looped model; with all copies set equal, the
untied model reproduces the looped model’s loss and logits exactly.
Design and resultsEach size trains at about 18 tokens per parameter of the looped model, close
to the compute-optimal ratio of Hoffmann et al. (2022): 60M at D0, 120M at 2D0, 240M at 4D0
and 480M at 8D0, with the recipe of its (2, 2) run and three seeds per model. Alongside the
untied model, we train (2, 2) at the same width and (2, 2) widened to about 80% of the untied
model’s non-embedding parameters, on the same tokens (Table 12). At the untied model’s width the
looped model trails by 9 × 10−3–1.7 × 10−2 nats (1.3 × 10−2 averaged over the four sizes), against
(3–4) × 10−3 by the law, whose error at these (2, 2) runs is at most 4 × 10−3 nats. The wider looped
model uses 15–22% more forward-and-backward compute per token than the untied model and
reaches a lower loss by 5 × 10−3–1.7 × 10−2 nats, in all twelve seed pairs.

H Training curves
Figure 20 shows the training curves of the 385 runs behind the 343 configurations of §G, one panel
per loop setting; Figure 4 in §4.1 repeats its first row. Each curve is the training loss of every tenth
step, the cross-entropy of that step’s batch, against the training compute spent so far, counted as in
§D.1 with the optimizer update; it is not the validation loss on held-out documents that we report
(§B.3). Each token budget is a separate run whose cosine schedule ends at that budget, so a run
reaches its final loss only at its end. The highlighted line therefore joins run ends only (dots), those
that end below every run with less compute, and needs no fit. As in the training-curve envelopes of
Hoffmann et al. (2022, Figure A5), larger models take over this frontier as compute grows; at the
highest compute of each panel it follows the largest model of that setting, 1B, or 480M for T = 16.
