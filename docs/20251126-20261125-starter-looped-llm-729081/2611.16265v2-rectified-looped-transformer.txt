ABSTRACT
Looped Transformers increase effective depth by repeatedly applying a shared
block, offering a parameter-efficient approach to language modeling. However,
most existing works directly reuse each loop’s output as the next’s input, which
can lead to hidden-state scale growth and perturbation amplification as recurrence
deepens. We propose Rectified Looped Transformer (RLT), which redesigns
feedback between loops using an interpolation schedule inspired by flow-based
modeling. At each loop, RLT forms the next input by linearly interpolating between
the current state and its prediction. Such interpolation distributes state changes
across loops, encouraging a smoother trajectory from the initial input to the final
output. Theoretical analysis proves that, under appropriate assumptions, RLT
keeps state magnitudes uniformly bounded across loops and reduces the worst-
case perturbation amplification. We evaluate RLT on language modeling corpora
and downstream benchmarks. At matched training and inference depths, RLT
consistently achieves lower perplexity than a standard looped Transformer, while
exhibiting greater robustness to input perturbations and variance in inference
depth. Consistent with the theoretical results, latent trajectory analysis shows that
RLT maintains more stable hidden-state norms across loops, which also facilitates
extrapolation-based decoding strategies. Notably, RLT achieves these gains without
requiring additional trainable parameters or extra recurrent block evaluations, thus
providing a simple yet more stable feedback mechanism for looped Transformers.

INTRODUCTION
Improving language model capabilities while controlling parameter count is an important challenge
in model scaling. Looped Transformers address this challenge by repeatedly applying a shared
stack of layers, increasing effective computational depth without increasing the number of physical
parameters. This makes recurrence depth an additional axis for scaling language models. Early
work (Saunshi et al., 2025) establishes the reasoning potential of looped Transformers through
controlled experiments and theoretical analysis. Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025) demonstrates their
feasibility at scale, while Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) further scales pretraining to trillions of tokens,
achieving performance competitive with larger standard LLMs and gains on tasks requiring multi-hop
reasoning. Together, these advances establish latent recurrent computation as a promising route to
more capable, parameter-efficient language models.
A key design choice in recurrent computation is how each loop’s output becomes the next loop’s
input. GRUs (Cho et al., 2014) use interpolation between existing and candidate states for effective
sequence modeling (Chung et al., 2014); Thinking with Looped Flows (Suleymanzade et al., 2026)
couples answer-space flow updates with recurrence to improve structured reasoning. In the direct-
feedback formulation (hereafter referred to as Standard Looped Transformer, SLT) commonly used
in current looped language models, including Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025), Loopie (Gao et al., 2026),
and LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026), the network output fully replaces the current input, without
retaining it as a separate component of the feedback update. From a dynamical systems perspective,
this applies the entire change from the current state to the network prediction at every loop, coupling
prediction with state advancement. As the shared network is repeatedly applied, these changes can
accumulate into hidden-state scale growth and perturbation amplification. STARS (Yang et al., 2026)
links performance degradation at larger recurrent depths to unstable latent dynamics; our SLT likewise

prediction yield more effective and stable recurrent computation?
To address this gap, we propose Rectified Looped Transformer (RLT; Figure 1(a)), which organizes
recurrent feedback through linear interpolation inspired by rectified flow (Liu et al., 2023). RLT
treats the network output at each loop as an endpoint prediction and combines it with the current
input state to form the next input. The interpolation weight on the endpoint prediction increases
as the remaining number of loops decreases. Intuitively, each loop re-estimates the endpoint and
distributes the remaining displacement over the remaining steps. This provides a geometric intuition
of approximately linear progress toward a shared target region, while allowing the endpoint estimate
to evolve across loops (Figure 1(b)). The design separates prediction from state advancement and
explicitly organizes how network updates accumulate across loops. Our theory establishes depth-
uniform bounds on feedback-state and output magnitudes under time-dependent residual control.
Even when the network can amplify perturbations, RLT reduces the worst-case amplification bound
for initial perturbations from exponential to polynomial in depth under a uniform sensitivity bound.
Empirically, with identical uniform all-step supervision, RLT consistently achieves lower perplexity
than the direct-feedback SLT when training and inference depths are matched. Besides, experiments
show greater robustness to perturbations and changes in inference depth, including inference with
both fewer and more loops than used during training. Trajectory analyses reveal differences in
scale accumulation and prediction evolution, while improved scale stability also benefits contrastive
decoding and guidance. Notably, these gains require only elementwise interpolation, with no
additional trainable feedback parameters or recurrent-block forward passes, highlighting the efficiency
of RLT’s feedback design.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We introduce Rectified Looped Transformer (RLT), which interprets each loop’s output
as an endpoint prediction and organizes latent-state feedback through a remaining-time
interpolation schedule.

• We theoretically analyze state and prediction magnitudes and perturbation amplification,
establishing depth-uniform magnitude bounds under time-dependent residual control and
polynomial bounds on initial-perturbation amplification under a uniform Lipschitz condition.
• We empirically demonstrate improvements in language modeling and robustness to changes
in loop count and numerical perturbations, supported by latent-trajectory analyses and
extrapolation-based decoding experiments.

RELATED WORK
Looped Transformers for Language Modeling.
Looped Transformers repeatedly apply a shared
stack of layers, increasing effective computational depth without adding physical parameters. Early
architectures such as Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) and ALBERT (Lan et al.,
2020) established parameter sharing across depth, while subsequent theoretical and empirical studies
demonstrated its potential for algorithm execution (Giannou et al., 2023) and reasoning (Saunshi et al.,
2025). Recently, Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025), Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025), and Loopie (Gao et al., 2026)
have extended this paradigm to large-scale language modeling, achieving performance competitive
with larger standard LLMs on commonsense and reasoning benchmarks while using fewer physical
parameters. Existing efforts seek to improve the performance and efficiency of looped language
models through sparse experts (Gao et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2026), depth-specific
adapters (Bae et al., 2025a), and adaptive allocation of recurrent computation (Bae et al., 2025b; Fu
et al., 2026; You et al., 2026), etc. In this work, we focus on state feedback and propose RLT to
improve language modeling and recurrent stability.
State Feedback and Recurrent Stability.
State feedback determines how each loop’s output
becomes the next input. The most common practice in looped Transformers is direct feedback,
which reuses the output as the next recurrent state, as in Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025), Loopie (Gao et al.,
2026), and LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026). Recent work has begun to explore feedback control
by scaling the residual update between the current state and the network output. ELR (Ladhak
et al., 2026) seeks to preserve one-loop performance while benefiting from additional loops, using
learned gates to scale residual updates; TAPS (Wang et al., 2026) aims to improve recurrent inference
efficiency by adaptively adjusting update magnitudes according to persistent progress and fluctuation
estimated from the trajectory. Recent studies such as STARS (Yang et al., 2026) also reveal that
looped Transformers can become unstable as recurrence deepens. To stabilize these dynamics,
Fixed-Point Reasoners (Movahedi et al., 2026) use damped feedback to suppress oscillation and
promote convergence toward fixed points. Our RLT uses a predefined remaining-time interpolation
schedule, with theoretical analysis establishing tighter control over state magnitudes and perturbation
amplification than direct feedback.
Flow-based Modeling and Iterative Reasoning.
Flow-based generative modeling (Lipman et al.,
2023) connects individual state updates to continuous transport between distributions through learned
ODEs. Rectified Flow (Liu et al., 2023) uses linear interpolation between source and target samples
as training paths, encouraging straighter flows that support sampling with fewer steps. Recent work
combines looped computation with flow-based generation. In structured reasoning tasks such as
Sudoku and maze solving, Thinking with Looped Flows (Suleymanzade et al., 2026) couples a
noise-to-answer flow with recurrent hidden states and local denoising supervision; Unrolled Flow
Models (Izermine et al., 2026) use a DiT (Peebles & Xie, 2023) backbone to learn flow-based answer
generation through their own latent rollouts with a terminal prediction loss. In contrast, our RLT
applies flow-inspired interpolation to state feedback in autoregressive language models, combining
each loop’s output with its input state to form the next input.

EXPERIMENTS
We compare SLT and RLT in language modeling, recurrent stability, and latent-state dynamics.
Matched architectures and training settings allow us to examine the effect of state feedback.
Following LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026), we adopt a GPT-2-style looped Transformer conditioned
on time and step size (Figure 1(a)). Each loop maps its input state to a hidden output through
the shared Transformer stack. SLT feeds this hidden output back as the next loop’s input state,
whereas RLT constructs the next input by interpolating between the current input state and the hidden
output using a remaining-time schedule. These feedback rules are formalized in Sections 3.1–3.2.
Our default configuration is 3 ⊗8: three shared Transformer layers applied over eight loops, with
278M physical parameters. We denote the training and inference loop counts by Ktrain and Ktest,
respectively. Unless otherwise specified, inference follows the uniform time grid defined in Eq. (1),
with tk = k/Ktest and ∆t = 1/Ktest.
Both models are trained from scratch on the deduplicated version of The Pile (Gao et al., 2020), with
the training data comprising 6.88B tokens and uniform all-step next-token prediction supervision.
We train for 14,000 optimizer steps with a peak learning rate of 6 × 10−4 and a global batch
size of 480 sequences of 1,024 tokens. We measure perplexity on The Pile (Gao et al., 2020),
FineWeb-Edu (FWE) (Penedo et al., 2024), and OpenWebText (OWT) (Gokaslan & Cohen, 2019),
and evaluate zero-shot accuracy following lm-eval harness (Gao et al., 2023). Our downstream
evaluation covers ten well-established benchmarks spanning commonsense reasoning, reading
comprehension, scientific question answering, and discourse completion: COPA (Roemmele et al.,
2011), HellaSwag (HS) (Zellers et al., 2019), LAMBADA (LB) (Paperno et al., 2016), OpenBookQA
(OBQA) (Mihaylov et al., 2018), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), RACE (Lai et al., 2017), SciQ (Welbl
et al., 2017), ARC (Clark et al., 2018), Social IQA (SIQA) (Sap et al., 2019), and WinoGrande
(WG) (Sakaguchi et al., 2021). We report accuracy or normalized accuracy as appropriate, averaging
ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge before computing the ten-task mean.
Further details on the model architecture, training hyperparameters, and datasets are provided in
Appendices C, D, and E, respectively.

Robustness to perturbations.
We first inject RMS-scaled Gaussian noise into the initial embedding
input x0 and measure the resulting displacement of the final hidden output. Across all three corpora
and all tested noise levels, RLT exhibits smaller absolute output displacements (Figure 3), in line

We then inject noise at every loop, either into the block output h before feedback or into the state x

added after feedback enters the next loop directly. See Appendix F.2 for experimental details.

4.3
LATENT DYNAMICS ACROSS INFERENCE DEPTHS
We first compare SLT and RLT across inference depths, then use PCA visualizations to examine the
latent dynamics underlying their performance differences. Both models are trained with eight loops
and evaluated at smaller and larger inference budgets.
Adaptation to inference depth.
We first reduce the inference budget while keeping the trained
weights fixed. Table 2 shows that RLT achieves lower perplexity on all three corpora at lower budgets.
At Ktest = 2, it reduces PPL by 35.9%, 48.2%, and 47.1% on Pile, FWE, and OWT, respectively,
while increasing average downstream accuracy from 39.24% to 40.76%. These results demonstrate
that RLT better preserves language modeling quality when fewer recurrent steps are available.
We then extend the sweep to inference depths between 2 and 32 (Figure 8). The results show that
RLT achieves lower Pile perplexity at every evaluated depth and exhibits a flatter performance profile
around the training depth. Beyond eight loops, SLT’s perplexity rises rapidly and its downstream
accuracy declines, whereas RLT tolerates the increased depth more effectively. At Ktest = 32, RLT
achieves a PPL of 17.72 versus 57.72 for SLT and an average accuracy of 42.88% versus 38.05%.
RLT thus supports a broader range of inference budgets, with improved performance retention both
below and above the training depth. Evaluation details are provided in Appendix G.1.
Hidden-state trajectories.
To examine the latent dynamics behind these performance differences,
we use 3D PCA to visualize the final token’s hidden outputs h1, . . . , hK in three examples from
randomly sampled OpenWebText windows (Figure 9). Each panel overlays inference budgets Ktest ∈
{4, 6, 8, 12, 24} in a common projection, with the matched-depth endpoint at Ktest = Ktrain = 8
highlighted as a reference. Trajectory extraction, PCA construction, and visualizations of additional
windows are provided in Appendix G.2.
Figure 9 clearly shows that RLT dynamically adjusts its state-update step sizes using time information
as the inference budget changes, while keeping its final hidden outputs concentrated around the
matched-depth endpoint. Several RLT trajectories near the training depth also exhibit more uniform
step-to-step changes than their SLT counterparts. In SLT, fewer loops fall short of the matched-
depth endpoint, whereas additional loops drift beyond it and substantially expand the trajectory.
For example, the terminal PC1 coordinate in Window 2 increases from 31.2 at Ktest = 4 to 62.5
at Ktest = 8 and 388.3 at Ktest = 24, reaching over six times the matched-depth value. These
contrasting behaviors agree with the schematic dynamics in Figure 1(b) and connect RLT’s greater
tolerance of inference-depth changes with more controlled representation dynamics. They also align
with RLT’s remaining-time feedback schedule, which adjusts the amount of state advancement to the
chosen loop budget.

5
CONCLUSION
We introduced Rectified Looped Transformer (RLT), which improves recurrent computation through
a flow-inspired feedback rule without additional trainable feedback parameters or recurrent-block
forward passes. RLT interpolates between the current input state and each loop’s endpoint prediction
according to the remaining time, separating prediction from state advancement. Our analysis
establishes depth-uniform state and output bounds under time-dependent residual control and reduces
worst-case initial-perturbation amplification bounds from exponential to polynomial in depth under
a uniform Lipschitz condition. Experiments demonstrate consistently lower perplexity at matched
depths, greater robustness to perturbations and inference-depth changes, and more controlled latent
dynamics than direct feedback. Improved scale stability also supports more effective contrastive
decoding and guidance. Our findings highlight state feedback as an effective design choice for jointly
improving language modeling and the stability of latent recurrent computation.

As illustrated in Figure 1(a), we adopt the LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026) architecture, with token
embeddings and learned absolute positional embeddings forming the initial state x0. The shared stack
contains three Transformer layers, each comprising causal multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and a
two-layer feed-forward network (FFN) with GELU activations. Time and step-size conditioning uses
AdaLN-style modulation (Peebles & Xie, 2023) with RMSNorm: the conditioning vector controls
the scales of normalized features and the gates on the attention and feed-forward residual branches.
