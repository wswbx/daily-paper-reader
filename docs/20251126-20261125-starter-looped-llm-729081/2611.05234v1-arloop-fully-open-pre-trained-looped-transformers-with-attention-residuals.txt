ABSTRACT
Looped transformers increase effective depth by repeatedly applying a shared set
of transformer layers, offering a parameter-efficient way to scale computation.
However, standard residual connections aggregate information across recurrent
depth into a single stream, providing limited control over which intermediate
representations from preceding layers should be reused. In this paper, we introduce
ARLOOP, a looped transformer architecture based on attention residuals (Kimi
Team, 2026), where the input of each layer is selectively aggregated through
attention over model depth. Distinct from standard transformers, we find that looped
transformers do not require access to the full residual history: sliding-window depth
attention matches or outperforms full-history attention at larger recurrent depths.
Across controlled experiments with multiple parameter scales (140M and 370M)
and recurrent depths (R=1 to 5), ARLOOP consistently outperforms prior residual
designs for looped transformers. More importantly, we further scale ARLOOP to
0.6B and 1.2B parameters under a realistic LLM pretraining recipe, demonstrating
that the gains transfer to realistic pretraining, with particularly strong improvements
on math and coding tasks. Our 1.2B model, pretrained on only 0.5T tokens with
three recurrences, outperforms Huginn-3.5B (the only fully-open looped LLM as
of our release date) across all evaluated tasks and is competitive with similar-sized
non-looped models such as Qwen3.5-2B despite being trained on substantially less
data.1

INTRODUCTION
Looped transformers repeatedly apply a small set of shared transformer layers, increasing effective
depth without increasing the number of distinct parameters (Dehghani et al., 2019; Yang et al.,
2024; Saunshi et al., 2025). This provides an appealing alternative to conventional depth scaling:
the same parameters can be reused for additional computation, and the number of recurrences
provides an explicit axis along which training and inference compute can be varied. Recent work
has demonstrated that such models can improve generalization on controlled multi-hop reasoning
tasks (Kohli et al., 2026). Meanwhile, growing interest has focused on scaling looped transformers
for parameter-efficient pretraining of large language models (LLMs) (Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al.,
2025). A central question, however, is how to make looped transformers effectively exploit the
additional computation provided by recurrent depth.
Increasing recurrent depth repeatedly produces new intermediate representations, but standard trans-
former residual connections, such as PreNorm (Xiong et al., 2020), provide only a limited mechanism
for controlling how information produced at earlier depths is reused. Under PreNorm, residual up-
dates from previous depths are accumulated into a single residual stream with fixed unit coefficients.
As recurrence increases, more updates are accumulated into the same state, while later computations
have no explicit mechanism for selecting which representations produced at earlier depths are useful.

Existing work has improved looped transformers through residual scaling or normalization (Geiping
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Prairie et al., 2026), but limited systematic comparisons leave it unclear
which residual design best enables models to make use of recurrent computation. Moreover, these
prior residual designs have mostly been evaluated under relatively small-scale pretraining setups,
leaving their effectiveness on more complex generative tasks such as mathematical reasoning and
code generation underexplored.
Motivated by recent advances in attention residuals for standard transformers (Kimi Team, 2026),
we investigate how selective residual access should be adapted to recurrent depth. Compared with
PreNorm, attention residuals replace the uniform aggregation of earlier residual updates with attention-
weighted aggregation, allowing each layer to selectively access representations from preceding depths.
Unlike standard transformers, however, recurrent depth repeatedly applies the same parameters and
can produce highly redundant representations across recurrences (Zeitoun et al., 2026; Wang et al.,
2026a), raising the question of whether access to the full residual history is necessary.
Our contributions are twofold. First, we introduce ARLOOP, a looped transformer architecture that
adapts attention residuals to recurrent depth. Across multiple parameter scales (140M and 370M)
and recurrence depths (R = 1 to R = 5), ARLOOP consistently improves validation perplexity over
PreNorm and outperforms prior residual designs (Geiping et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Prairie et al.,
2026) under our rigorous controlled evaluation setting. Distinct from standard transformers, we find
that access to the full residual history is unnecessary for looped transformers: sliding-window depth
attention matches or outperforms full-history attention at larger recurrence depths. Our analysis
further shows that depth attention concentrates on recent residual sources and that representations
across recurrences are highly redundant, explaining the effectiveness of sliding-window attention.
More importantly, we demonstrate that ARLOOP scales to work under a realistic LLM pretraining
recipe. Despite using a much smaller training budget (i.e. 0.5T tokens), our strongest ARLOOP-1.2B
substantially outperforms Huginn-3.5B (Geiping et al., 2025) (the only fully-open looped LLM
as of our release date), and achieves performance competitive with strong non-looped LLMs such
as Qwen3.5-2B. At scale, we find that ARLOOP generally outperforms PreNorm, with especially
promising improvements on math and coding tasks (e.g. +5.2 points on GSM8K and +3.1 points on
MBPP). We release training code and checkpoints, making ARLOOP fully-open for future study.

RELATED WORK
Looped transformers.
Looped transformers, also known as recurrent-depth transformers or univer-
sal transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024), are widely studied as
2


memory-efficient alternatives to transformers. As they are increasingly scaled to LLM pretraining
(Csord´as et al., 2024; Geiping et al., 2025; Bae et al., 2025), recent work has explored residual
designs for better information propagation across recurrent depth, including Parcae, DeepLoop, and
HyperLoop (Li et al., 2026; Zeitoun et al., 2026; Prairie et al., 2026; Knupp et al., 2026). Com-
pared with these approaches, ARLOOP consistently matches or outperforms them across parameter
and recurrence budgets in our controlled experiments. We further show that these gains extend
beyond language modeling perplexity to mathematical reasoning and code generation, which remain
underexplored in prior work.
Pretraining looped transformers at scale.
Recent work has begun scaling looped transformers
for pretraining. Geiping et al. (2025) introduced Huginn, a 3.5B-parameter looped LLM trained
with up to 64 recurrences, while Zhu et al. (2025) further scaled the training data with Ouro, a 1.4B
model using up to four recurrences. Subsequent work has explored looped LLMs with mixture-
of-experts architectures such as Loopies (Gao et al., 2026), SMELT (Wang et al., 2026b), as well
as larger data scales for advanced capabilities, including Nanbeige4.2-3B (Lab et al., 2026) and
LoopCoder-v2 (Yang et al., 2026). Despite these promising results, many looped LLMs release only
model weights or do not release weights at all, leaving the training and data recipe unclear. The
only exception is Huginn, which releases all training artifacts. In particular, Huginn reports that
PreNorm does not effectively exploit recurrent depth and instead relies on conservative optimization
setups for stable training. Our work systematically studies residual design for looped transformers
and further validates the resulting architecture under a realistic LLM pretraining recipe, substantially
outperforming Huginn despite using smaller parameter, data, and recurrence budgets.

ARLOOP
We first describe the formulation of our looped transformer and then how we adapt attention residuals
for it. We refer to the resulting model as ARLOOP.
3.1
PRELIMINARIES
Terminology.
Throughout the paper, we use sublayer to refer to either a self-attention or a feed-
forward network (FFN) module, transformer layer to refer to a pair of self-attention and FFN
sublayers, and residual block to refer to a group of consecutive sublayers used as a source in attention
residuals, as introduced below.
Standard Transformer with PreNorm residual.
We start by defining a standard decoder-only
transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017; Radford et al., 2019) with a PreNorm residual stream. Let x be a
sequence of tokens, and let h0 ∈Rn×d denote their embeddings. A standard transformer consists of
L layers, each of which contains a self-attention sublayer followed by an FFN. For the i-th sublayer,
its input hi−1 and output hi can be written as
hi = hi−1 + fi(Norm(hi−1)),
i = 1, . . . , 2L

where fi denotes either a self-attention or an FFN function. We can then define hi = Fi(hi−1), and
the entire transformer can be written as y = LMHead ◦F2L ◦· · · ◦F1(h0), where LMHead denotes
the language-modeling head that maps the final hidden states to output logits.
Looped transformer.
Following the notation above, an L-layer looped transformer reuses the same
transformer stack S (e.g. F4 ◦F3 ◦F2 ◦F1) for R recurrences. Although S can consist of any
subset of the L distinct transformer blocks, in this paper we only consider looping the full stack
of transformer blocks (i.e. S = F2L ◦· · · ◦F1). Hence, our looped transformer can be written as
y = LMHead ◦S(R) ◦· · · ◦S(1)(h0), where S(1) = · · · = S(R) = S, since the same transformer
stack is shared across all recurrences. The resulting unrolled network therefore has an effective depth
of 2LR sublayers, while containing only 2L distinct transformer sublayers.

At each sublayer, attention is computed over the input embedding, the representations of preceding
completed blocks, and the partial sum of residual updates within the current block. Thus, residual
updates are accumulated additively within a block as in PreNorm, while attention-based aggregation
is performed across blocks. This reduces the number of stored residual sources from individual
sublayer outputs to block-level representations, substantially reducing the overhead of full attention
residuals. We use block attention residuals for all ARLOOP models unless stated otherwise.
3.2
APPLYING ATTENTION RESIDUALS TO LOOPED TRANSFORMER
Since attention residuals only change how each sublayer aggregates representations from preceding
depths, they can be naturally applied to looped transformers. Figure 2a illustrates a simplified example
4


in which each recurrence contains two sublayers. As the depth grows, residual updates are grouped
into one or more residual blocks following Equation 4. Each effective sublayer has its own learned
query vector (i.e. qi in Figure 2b), which attends over the residual sources available at that depth.
Compared with PreNorm-based looped transformers, this allows sublayers at deeper recurrences to
selectively access representations produced at earlier recurrent depths.
Sliding window depth attention.
Standard attention residuals allow each depth-attention operation
to access all preceding residual blocks as candidate sources, which we refer to as full-history depth
attention. In a looped transformer, however, the same set of parameters is repeatedly applied across
recurrences, potentially introducing redundancy among representations produced at different recurrent
depths. Indeed, prior work has observed high similarity between hidden states across recurrences
in looped transformers (Zeitoun et al., 2026). This motivates us to ask whether access to the full
residual history is necessary, or whether recent residual states already provide sufficient information.
Specifically, we study if sliding-window depth attention (Figure 2c) can match full-history depth at-
tention. Let B = {b0, b1, . . . , bm} denote the residual sources available to a depth-attention operation,
where b0 denotes the input embedding and bm is the most recent residual block. Given a window size
W, sliding-window depth attention restricts the source set to BW = {b0, bmax(1,m−W +1), . . . , bm}.
Thus, the input embedding remains directly accessible at every depth, while depth attention is per-
formed only over the W most recent residual blocks. Note that sliding-window depth attention
has also been studied in standard transformers, where it reduces the gains of attention residuals
over PreNorm (Kimi Team, 2026). In contrast, as we will show in Section 4, sliding-window depth
attention can match and even outperform full-history attention in looped transformers, particularly at
larger recurrence depths. We therefore make sliding-window depth attention the default in ARLOOP
for R > 2.
4
CONTROLLED EVALUATION OF ARLOOP
We first use controlled experiments to evaluate how effectively ARLOOP reuses information across
recurrent depth, examining both its benefits for language modeling and the contribution of its key
design choices.
General setup.
We evaluate ARLOOP under a controlled setup using 140M- and 370M-parameter
decoder-only transformers with 6 and 12 transformer layers, respectively. Following Prairie et al.
(2026), we pretrain these models on 11.2B and 29.6B tokens from FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al.,
2024), respectively, with a context length of 2048. Following the general architecture of Karpathy
(2025), each transformer layer uses multi-head attention and a standard MLP. For optimization, we
use AdamW (Kingma & Ba, 2017) with a cosine learning rate schedule. Complete architecture and
training details are provided in Appendix A.1.
ARLOOP training setup.
For all ARLOOP models, we use fixed recurrence, such that the number
of recurrences R is fixed throughout training. For the 140M and 370M models, we group every 6
and 12 consecutive sublayers, respectively, into a residual block. Since each recurrence contains
12 sublayers at 140M and 24 sublayers at 370M, this yields two residual blocks per recurrence at
both scales. Following the design in Section 3.2, we use full-history depth attention for R ≤2
and sliding-window depth attention for R > 2. For the sliding-window variant, we retain the input
embedding and the three most recent residual blocks.
Baselines.
We use a PreNorm-based looped transformer with the residual in Equation 1 as our
default baseline, which we call PRENORMLOOP. We also compare against previously proposed
residual designs for looped transformers, including Sandwich Norm (Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al.,
2025), DeepLoop (Li et al., 2026), and Parcae (Prairie et al., 2026), both with and without input
injection (Bansal et al., 2022). We provide the formulations of these baselines in Appendix E. We
train all models with a fixed number of recurrences R = 1, 3, 5 at the 140M scale and R = 1, 3 at
the 370M scale due to computational cost. We implement and train these methods under a unified
training setup, keeping all training hyperparameters fixed across methods except for the learning rate.
Details of learning-rate tuning are provided in Appendix A.1.

Why sliding-window depth attention works for looped transformers.
To understand why recent
residual blocks are sufficient, we visualize the attention weights assigned to each residual source by
ARLOOPfull with R = 4 in Figure 3 (Left). Depth attention places most of its mass on recent residual
sources (e.g., at sublayer 47, the three most recent residual blocks and the embedding block receive
96% of the total attention weight), suggesting that direct access to distant residual history contributes
relatively little to the representations used at deeper recurrences. We hypothesize that this locality
arises from parameter sharing across recurrences: repeatedly applying the same transformer layers
produces redundant representations, while more recent sources have already undergone refinement
through additional recurrent computation. Consequently, retaining direct access to distant residual
sources may provide little additional information and could even introduce unnecessary interference.
To examine this hypothesis, we further visualize the pairwise cosine similarity between residual
blocks in Figure 3 (Right). Residual sources across recurrences exhibit high similarity, supporting
the presence of substantial redundancy across recurrent depth. We observe the same pattern across
other parameter scales and recurrence settings, with complete results provided in Appendix A.5.
Importantly, this behavior differs from a standard transformer with matched effective depth, where
representations exhibit substantially lower cross-depth similarity, and applying the same sliding-
window restriction degrades performance (Appendix A.5). Together, these results suggest that local
depth attention is particularly well suited to the recurrent-depth structure of looped transformers.

3.5B parameters and up to 64 recurrences).
Note that we do not compare with other residual methods such as Parcae or DeepLoop (Prairie et al.,
2026; Li et al., 2026; Zeitoun et al., 2026) in this realistic pretraining comparison. Such previous
work generally validates their methods only at small data scales (e.g. below 50B tokens) or with
a single pretraining source (e.g. FineWeb-Edu), leaving the capabilities of the resulting models on
more complex tasks such as math and coding open. Our contribution specifically targets this question,
aiming to show ARLOOP remains effective on such generative tasks under realistic pretraining
settings.

FURTHER DISCUSSION
Dynamic versus fixed recurrence.
Existing looped language models often vary the number of
recurrences during training to support multiple effective depths (Geiping et al., 2025; Prairie et al.,
2026; Kohli et al., 2026; Bae et al., 2025). We compare such dynamic-recurrence training with fixed
R = 4 training and find that fixed recurrence consistently achieves better validation perplexity and
downstream performance at both the 140M and 370M scales. We therefore use fixed recurrence in
our main experiments. Complete training setups and results are provided in Appendix D.1.
Inference-time scaling.
We also investigate whether the improved residual access of ARLOOP
enables inference-time recurrence extrapolation, i.e. benefiting from more recurrences than observed
during training (Fan et al., 2025; Kohli et al., 2026). Consistent with prior observations for looped
language models (Prairie et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025), we find that performance improves up to the
maximum training recurrence but does not further improve beyond it. This suggests that selective
access to earlier representations alone does not unlock recurrence extrapolation: effectively composing
9


information within the trained depth range and generalizing recurrent computation beyond that range
appear to be distinct problems. Complete experimental details are provided in Appendix D.2.

7
CONCLUSION
We introduce ARLOOP, a looped transformer architecture that adapts attention residuals to recurrent
depth. Through controlled experiments across multiple parameter scales, ARLOOP consistently
outperforms PreNorm and prior residual designs for looped transformers. Distinct from standard
transformers, we find that looped transformers do not require access to the full residual history:
sliding-window depth attention can match or outperform full-history attention at larger recurrence
depths. More importantly, we demonstrate that these gains transfer to realistic LLM pretraining. At
the 0.6B scale, ARLOOP substantially improves over a matched PreNorm model, with particularly
large gains on math and coding tasks. Our strongest model ARLOOP-1.2B substantially outperforms
the fully-open looped LLM Huginn and is competitive with Qwen3.5-2B despite being trained on
substantially less data. Together, these results demonstrate that selective residual access provides an
effective way for looped transformers to better exploit recurrent computation at scale.

CONTROLLED EXPERIMENT DETAILS
A.1
ARCHITECTURE AND TRAINING.
Table 5 specifies the 140M and 370M model families used in Section 4. The table describes the shared
backbone of PreNorm, ARLOOPfull, and ARLOOPsw. Each recurrence reuses the entire physical layer
stack; increasing R increases the effective depth without adding Transformer layers. Depth-attention
queries and normalization parameters are specific to each effective sublayer. At the final effective
depth, we apply a separate attention-residual aggregation to produce the representation passed to the
language modeling head. We use a scaled-zero initialization, which is the same strategy as Prairie
et al. (2026). The additional baselines retain the same model dimensions and use their respective
residual updates and initialization rules.
Value residuals.
Following the default setup of Karpathy (2025), we use value residuals (Zhou
et al., 2025) in all experiments. Specifically, at alternating effective layers (e.g., the 1st, 3rd, and 5th
layers), we inject a gated token embedding into the value vectors of the self-attention sublayer. These
token-specific embedding parameters are distinct from the input embeddings. Value residual learning
has been shown to improve LLM pretraining (Zhou et al., 2025) and has also been adopted in recent
looped-transformer architectures (Prairie et al., 2026).
Although value residuals are not a contribution of our work, we find them important for obtaining
strong looped-transformer performance, particularly at smaller scales. Importantly, removing value
residuals substantially degrades our sliding-window depth-attention variant. They are even more
important for Sandwich-Norm looped transformers: without value residuals, training becomes
unstable and the loss remains near 7.5 rather than continuing to decrease. This pattern is consistent
with the failed Sandwich-Norm training run reported by Geiping et al. (2025, Figure 5, Bad Run
1). We hypothesize that this instability is related to representation collapse at large effective depths,
where representations of different tokens become increasingly similar, and repeated injection of
token-specific information through value residuals mitigates this effect. Empirically, adding value
residuals eliminates the observed training failure and makes the Sandwich-Norm variant trainable in
our setting.

A.6
THE EFFECT OF RESIDUAL BLOCK AND SLIDING WINDOW SIZES
We additionally study the effect of residual block size, i.e. the number of sublayers contained in
each residual block, and sliding-window size, i.e. the number of recent residual blocks retained by
sliding-window depth attention. We primarily use the 140M ARLOOP for these studies.
Table 10 reports the validation perplexities of models trained with different residual block sizes across
recurrence settings. We find that using finer-grained residual blocks (i.e. smaller block sizes) does not
consistently improve validation perplexity. While finer-grained blocks are beneficial for non-recurrent
transformers, consistent with Kimi Team (2026), we find that their benefit diminishes as the number
of recurrences increases, and smaller blocks can even hurt performance at larger R. We hypothesize
that this is related to the same phenomenon observed in Section 4.1: residual-block representations
are highly redundant across recurrences, and using smaller blocks introduces more residual sources
from earlier recurrences, potentially further increasing this redundancy.
Table 11 reports the results of ARLOOPsw with sliding windows retaining the most recent 2, 3, 4,
or 5 residual blocks. Among the settings we evaluate, retaining three residual blocks performs best
overall. We leave a more systematic exploration of the optimal sliding-window design to future work.

D.2
INFERENCE-TIME RECURRENCE EXTRAPOLATION
We present the details of the study on inference-time recurrence extrapolation of ARLOOP.
Setup.
We evaluate the per-sequence Poisson model described above at each recurrence R =
1, . . . , 8, using the same validation set as in Section 4. During training, the recurrence is bounded
by R ≤4; evaluation at R > 4 therefore requires computation at effective depths that are never
encountered during the original training. Since our attention-residual mechanism uses a learned query
for each effective sublayer, evaluating beyond the maximum training recurrence introduces queries
at previously unseen depths. We randomly initialize these additional queries and train only their
parameters on an additional 0.5B FineWeb-Edu tokens, while freezing all other model parameters.
This provides a favorable setting for recurrence extrapolation: rather than requiring the model to
reuse or extrapolate query parameters to unseen depths, the newly introduced queries are explicitly
optimized to aggregate the residual history available at those depths.
Results.
Figure 10 shows that perplexity does not continue to improve beyond the maximum
training recurrence at either model scale. This remains the case even when the query parameters at
unseen depths are explicitly trained to learn the optimal aggregation of information from preceding
depths. These results support the conclusion in Section 6: in our experiments, improved access to

TRAINING COST
We train our models on NVIDIA A100 80GB, H100, and B200 GPUs. The controlled experiments
require approximately 12K GPU hours in total, including early-stage exploration. Pretraining the
PRENORMLOOP-0.6B and ARLOOP-0.6B models takes approximately one week on 8 H100 GPUs.
The ARLOOP-1.2B model is trained on 16 B200 GPUs for approximately two weeks.
