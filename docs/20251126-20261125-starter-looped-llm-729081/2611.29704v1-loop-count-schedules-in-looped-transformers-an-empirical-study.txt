Loop-Count Schedules in Looped Transformers: An Empirical Study
Konstantin Kolokolov
Abstract
Looped transformers apply one block of layers several times with shared weights, trading parameters
for repeated computation. We study how the schedule of the loop iteration count R during training
determines whether each extra pass acts as a full layer or as a small correction, using a controlled ladder of
46M–110M-parameter models trained on 2B tokens with learned residual gates on every loop pass and
three schedules: R fixed to 2, R sampled uniformly from {1, . . . , 6}, and R sampled from {2, 3, 4}. We find
that the lower bound of the sampling range is the decisive choice. If the sampled range includes R=1,
so that some training steps run the block only once and compute the loss on that single pass, the gates
collapse to 0.1–0.5 and each additional pass becomes a small correction to the first; if the range starts at
R=2, so that every training step runs the block at least twice, the gates stay at 0.6–0.9 and each pass
keeps the strength of a full layer, while the model can still be run at any R from 2 up to twice the largest
trained value with validation loss within a few hundredths of a nat of its best. The {2, 3, 4} schedule gives
the best looped model, 2.909 nats against 2.951 for the parameter-matched plain model, and degrades by
only 0.038 nats when run at twice its trained depth. We further show that under a fixed R the direction of
the residual stream converges geometrically beyond the trained depth while the loss diverges, which refines
a recent finding that static-depth training does not converge, and that reusing the first pass’s keys and
values on later passes costs 0.01–0.03 nats in every regime while making the per-token iteration uniformly
contractive and halving the inference cache. The loop recovers about a third of what real depth provides
at equal parameters. The code, trained weights, and per-token traces are released.
1. Introduction
Reusing one block of a transformer across several passes
has been proposed by several groups over the past years,
first for parameter efficiency and adaptive computation
and more recently as a route to latent reasoning. De-
hghani et al. (1) introduced the Universal Transformer,
which applies a single block recurrently with a step
embedding and an adaptive halting mechanism; Lan et
al. (2) shared weights across all layers of ALBERT to
cut parameters at a small cost in quality. A second line
of work treated the looped transformer as a machine
for iterative algorithms: Yang et al. (3) showed that
it learns in-context learning algorithms better than an
unrolled stack, and Fan et al. (4) that it generalizes to
longer inputs when the number of loops scales with the
input.
The current motivation is latent reasoning at language-
model scale. Bae et al. (5) revisited parameter sharing
with per-loop low-rank adapters and, in Mixture-of-
Recursions (6), added token-level routing of the re-
cursion depth together with several KV-cache sharing
policies. Geiping et al. (7) trained a 3.5B-parameter
recurrent-depth model with the loop count sampled
during training and the input injected at every step,

and reported that extra passes at inference improve
reasoning benchmarks. Zhu et al. (8) pre-trained the
Ouro family on 7.7T tokens with an entropy-regularized
objective that learns how much depth to allocate per
input, attributing the gains to better manipulation
of knowledge rather than larger capacity; Marchenko
et al. (9) reduced that budget to 310B tokens with
a from-scratch recipe and a minimal procedure for
converting pretrained dense models. Jeddi et al. (10)
trained on trajectories of varying length with a shortcut-
consistency objective so that short runs give usable
states and long runs refine them, and Wang et al. (11)
found that untying the attention parameters across
passes of a looped mixture-of-experts helps while the
experts stay shared. Huang et al. (12) reframed the
loop transition as an optimizer step with a learned,
monotonically decreasing step size, and observed that
perplexity understates the generative gains of looped
models; Zeitoun et al. (13) widened the residual stream
into several streams that are mixed after each loop
and matched depth-matched baselines with about half
the parameters. Alongside these are studies of where
recurrent compute is best allocated (14, 15) and of how
to decode looped models efficiently (16, 17). Ladhak
et al. (18) and Fishman et al. (19) carried the recipe
to 4B parameters and above, with loop counts mixed

Two questions about the loop’s dynamics have received
less attention than its benchmarks. The first is whether
the iteration converges. Dong et al. (20) showed that
a stack of pure self-attention layers loses rank dou-
bly exponentially and that the feed-forward sublayer
is what prevents the collapse, so the two halves of a
block pull in opposite directions when iterated. Lo-
gan (21) measured per-token fixed-point convergence in
depth-recurrent transformers with the KL divergence
between consecutive passes’ output distributions and
found models saturating at their training-mean depth;
Pappone et al. (22) observed that the loop steps of such
a model become smaller and increasingly orthogonal
over training and built an early-exit rule on the second-
order difference of the step size. Most directly, Prairie
et al. (23), in independent work developed in parallel
with this study, trained looped models at 140M–1.3B
parameters with and without a stochastically sampled
depth and showed, using the relative step of the resid-
ual stream, that stochastic-depth training drives the
representations toward a common limit across passes
whereas static-depth training does not; they use this
property to share the KV cache of the final pass back-
ward into the earlier ones after training, zero-shot for
models pretrained with sampled depth and with a short
finetuning stage otherwise. The second question is what
reusing keys and values across passes does. Jaegle et
al. (24) iterated a latent array against a fixed input
array in the Perceiver, which is the earliest form of a
loop with a static memory; Bae et al. (6) measured
the quality cost of reusing the first recursion’s cache;
Brandon et al. (25) and Sun et al. (26) share caches
across distinct layers rather than across passes. In
our own earlier work on contextual biasing for speech
recognition (27) we iterated a transducer joiner to self-
consistency against a fixed set of hint embeddings, a
loop whose only feedback path is the attention query,
and observed convergence within three iterations; the
contrast between that fast, bounded iteration and the
slow contraction of a looped language-model block is
the origin of the static-KV variant studied here.
What this literature leaves open is which property of the
loop-count schedule produces the convergent regime,
what the model gives up to obtain it, and whether the
two can be separated. The present paper answers these
questions in a setting small enough to change one thing
at a time, and reads the answer off two instruments that
the larger studies do not report: the learned residual
gates on each loop pass, and per-token traces of the
residual stream’s direction and norm across passes.
2. Model and schedule settings
We use a decoder-only transformer whose layers are
standard pre-norm blocks: RMSNorm (28) before each
sublayer, multi-head attention with rotary position em-
beddings (29) and query-key normalization (30), and a
SwiGLU feed-forward network (31); token embeddings
are tied to the output projection, and the output head
is a final RMSNorm, a linear layer and a softmax over
the vocabulary. The plain model applies each block
once. The looped model applies each block R times
with shared weights, as in Figure 1.
The looping version of the transformer block is orga-
nized the following way. The first pass is ungated and
identical to a plain block. All following loop passes
(from 2 to R) add gated residual updates with separate
learned scalars on the attention and FFN paths:
x ←x + αattn Attn(Norm1(x)),
x ←x + αff FFN(Norm2(x)).
Here x is the residual stream, the per-token hidden vec-
tor of width d that the skip connections carry through
the block; every sublayer reads it through its pre-
norm and adds its output back into it. Norm denotes
RMSNorm throughout; there is no LayerNorm any-
where in the model.
Each gate is parameterized as α = σ(θ), with σ the
logistic sigmoid and θ a trainable parameter, so it lies
in (0, 1); both are initialized at 0.5 (θ = 0), excluded
from weight decay, and logged every step. A gate near
1 means the pass writes at the full strength of a plain
layer; a gate near 0 means the pass is nearly skipped.
At R=1 the block is a plain block, which we use as
a correctness test. All blocks loop with the same R;
there is no step embedding, no input injection, and no
truncated backpropagation. The gates are the minimal
form of the learned step size that Huang et al. (12)
make input-dependent and monotone across loops.
Loop iteration schedules. The number of passes R
is a training-time choice, and we compare three ways
of making it. In all three, R applies to every block
of the model on a given forward pass, the loss is the
ordinary next-token cross-entropy on the output of the
final pass, and gradients flow through all passes.
R counts every pass, so R=1 is the ungated first pass
alone, i.e. a plain block.

Forward static KV. Attention forms queries Q, keys
K and values V by three linear projections of the nor-
malized residual stream; each is a tensor of shape (se-
quence length × head dimension) per head, and we
write KV for the pair. Under static KV, keys and
values are projected on the first pass and reused on
later passes; only the query is recomputed from the
updated state. The attention output on a later pass is
a convex combination of fixed value vectors steered by
a fresh query, the KV projections are skipped on later
passes, the inference cache is that of a plain model with
the same block count, and token i’s update no longer
depends on the current state of token j. We call this
forward sharing, since the first pass’s cache serves the
later passes (Bae et al. (6) share the first recursion’s
cache in the same direction); the converse, broadcasting
the final pass’s cache to all earlier passes after train-
ing, as Prairie et al. (23) do, we call backward sharing.
The forward variant transfers to the transformer block
the structure of the looped context joiner of (27), in

changing from one iteration to the next.

3. Experimental setup
Data. The training corpus is a 2.0B-token mixture
of three public sources: FineWeb-Edu (the sample-
10BT subset) for general text, the Python portion of
codeparrot-clean for code, and FineMath-4+ for math-
ematical text. Documents longer than 16k tokens are
truncated. A 32k-entry BPE tokenizer is trained on
300k documents drawn from the mixture in its training
proportions and used for every run. From each source,
1M tokens of documents are held out before training
as that domain’s validation set, so no validation text
is seen in training. The remaining documents are to-
kenized, separated by a beginning-of-sequence token,
and packed into fixed 1024-token training sequences;
the training stream draws these sequences from the
three domains in the proportions 80% text, 10% code,
10% math, for a total of 2.0B tokens, which is one
pass over the prepared data. Validation uses the same
packing, and the validation loss is computed separately
on each domain’s held-out set; the single validation loss
we report is the mean of the three per-domain losses
weighted by the training proportions, so it corresponds
to the loss on a stream drawn like the training data.
Per-domain losses appear in Table A4 and differ from
each other, but nowhere in this paper do the domains
differ in their response to the loop beyond single-seed
noise, so the text reports the weighted validation loss
throughout.
Models. All models share one width and differ only in
depth and in whether they loop. The residual stream
has a hidden dimension of 768; attention uses 12 heads
of 64 dimensions each; the feed-forward sublayer ex-
pands to an inner dimension of 2048 in the SwiGLU
form, whose two input projections give it the same
parameter count as a conventional MLP with an inner
dimension of 8/3 × 768, so plain and looped models
are matched on purpose. The vocabulary has 32,768
entries, and the embedding matrix is tied: the same
32,768 × 768 matrix that maps a token id to its input
vector is reused, transposed, as the output projection
that maps the final hidden state to logits over the vo-
cabulary, so the model carries one such matrix rather
than two. Each block holds about 7.1M parameters
and the tied embedding about 25.2M, so the models
have 21.2M, 42.5M and 85.0M parameters outside the
embedding (3, 6 and 12 blocks) and 46.4M, 67.6M and
110.1M in total. Looping adds only the gates: two
scalars per block.
Training. Every run uses the same optimizer, sched-
ule and token budget, so the only differences between
runs are the ones named in §2. We train with AdamW
(β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95) at a peak learning rate of 6×10−4,
4


warmed up linearly over the first 2,000 steps and then
decayed with a cosine schedule to 10% of the peak by
the end of training. Weight decay of 0.1 is applied to
the weight matrices only, not to normalization parame-
ters, biases or the loop gates. Gradients are clipped to
a global norm of 1.0. Computation is in bfloat16 with
float32 master weights. Each optimizer step sees 32,768
tokens (32 sequences of 1,024 tokens, accumulated over
several micro-batches whose size depends on the con-
figuration’s memory footprint), and training runs for
61,000 steps, which is one pass over the 2.0B-token cor-
pus. Checkpoints and validation are taken every 2,000
steps, so the last evaluated checkpoint, from which
every number in this paper is taken and which is the
one released, is at step 60k. Each run occupies one
RTX 5080 (16 GB) for between one and four days de-
pending on depth and loop schedule. Every run uses
random seed 42 for initialization and data order. The
four 6-block runs that carry the headline comparison,
the plain 6-block model and the looped 6-block model
under each of the three schedules with fresh KV, were
repeated with seed 43; where both seeds exist, Tables 1,
2 and 5 and the text report the two-seed mean, and the
two seeds agree to within 0.003 nats at every trained
loop count (Appendix A).
Runs. The ladder consists of three plain models with
3, 6 and 12 blocks (plain_3, plain_6, plain_12) and
looped versions of the 3- and 6-block models. The
6-block looped model is trained under each of the three
loop-count schedules, and each of those both with keys
and values recomputed on every pass and with the
static-KV variant, giving six looped 6-block runs. The
3-block looped model is trained under the fixed R=2
and the R ∼{1, . . . , 6} schedules, again with both
KV policies, giving four looped 3-block runs. That is
thirteen runs in total; their names and final numbers
are listed in Appendix A.
Evaluation. Every 2,000 training steps we compute
the validation loss on each domain’s held-out set, first
at the loop count used as the headline for that run (R=2
for every looped model, so the headline numbers are
comparable), and then again at every R ∈{1, . . . , 6, 8},
by looping the same weights a different number of times
with no retraining; this gives the loss-versus-R curves of
§5.2 at every checkpoint. At the same checkpoints we
run the convergence trace of §2 on the fixed validation
batch, to 6 passes for the fixed-R models and to 8 for
the sampled-R models, and save the per-token arrays.
The loop gates are logged at every training step. We
also record training throughput in tokens per second
and peak GPU memory for each run, since the cost of
the variants is part of the comparison.
4. Problem statement and summary of
findings
Nearly every recent recipe samples the number of passes
R during training (7–10), and Prairie et al. (23) show
at scale why that matters for inference: stochastic
depth makes the residual stream converge across passes,
which is what makes the KV cache shareable. We ask
which property of the sampling is responsible, what
the model gives up to obtain it, and whether the two
can be separated.
We answer in a controlled setting.
We train plain
transformers of 3, 6 and 12 layers and looped versions
of the 3- and 6-layer models with learned residual gates
on every loop pass, on one 2B-token mixture, and
change only the R schedule and the KV policy. The
gates turn out to be the observable: their learned values
say directly how much work a loop pass does. Three
schedules give three answers.
• Fixed R=2. Gates in deep blocks go to ≈1. The
loop is an extra layer. The model is 0.032 nats
better than the parameter-matched plain model
at R=2 and loses between half a nat and a full nat
at R=6, depending on the seed.
• R sampled from {1, . . . , 6}. Gates collapse to
0.1–0.5. The loop is a damper: each pass is a
small correction to a first pass that does most of
the work. Loss is flat for R from 2 to 8 and the
total gain from extra passes is at most 0.09 nats.
• R sampled from {2, 3, 4}. The only change from
the previous schedule is that R=1 is never drawn.
Gates stay at 0.6–0.9, the loss is the best of all
looped models (2.909 at R=3) and flat over the
trained range, and the penalty at R=8 is 0.038
nats.
The lower bound of the schedule is therefore the switch.
Asking the model to be usable after one pass pushes the
computation into the first pass and makes every further
pass small, which is the convergent regime observed at
scale and also the regime in which extra passes have
little to add. Excluding R=1 keeps the loop a full layer
while sampling over 2–4 keeps it robust there.
Two observations about the loop’s dynamics support
and refine this. First, the fixed-R loop does converge,
in the sense that matters for a pre-norm model: the
direction of the residual stream settles geometrically
past the trained depth while its norm grows, and the
loss diverges because the direction settles toward a state
the output projection never saw. The relative residual
step is dominated by the norm and reads this as non-
convergence. Second, freezing keys and values after
5


the first pass and reusing them forward, the opposite
direction to backward cache sharing, costs 0.01–0.03
nats in every regime, makes the per-token iteration
uniformly contractive, and halves the inference cache.
The contributions are: (i) the lower bound of the loop-
count schedule, not sampling itself, decides whether
a looped block learns an extra layer or a damped it-
eration, with the learned gates as the observable and
a one-flag intervention as the test; (ii) a direction-
versus-norm account of hidden-state convergence un-
der fixed depth that reconciles geometric settling with
diverging loss; (iii) the cost and the dynamics of for-
ward static KV trained from scratch; (iv) a parameter-
and FLOP-matched ladder quantifying how much of
real depth the loop recovers (35% at 3 blocks, 24%
at 6). The training code (the gatedloop-transformer
package), every configuration in this paper, and all
runs and per-token traces are released at https://
github.com/kvoyager/gatedloop-transformer, and the
trained weights at https://huggingface.co/konstank/
gatedloop-transformer.
5. Results
5.1. The lower bound of the schedule sets the
gates
The gates say directly how much a loop pass does
(Figure 2, Table 1). Under fixed R=2 the deeper blocks
run the second pass at full strength (block 4: αattn =
1.00, αff = 0.96). Under R ∼{1, . . . , 6} every gate
drops to 0.1–0.5. Under R ∼{2, 3, 4} the profile returns
to within 0.15 of the fixed-R profile with the same shape,
ramping from the first block to block 4. The three
profiles are the same in both seeds: no block-mean gate
differs between the seeds by more than 0.07, against a
gap of 0.3–0.5 between the R ∼{1, . . . , 6} profile and
the other two. Attention gates exceed FFN gates in
the early and middle blocks of every variant, consistent
with attention being the contractive sublayer and the
FFN the expansive one (20); the last block reverses the
order, and the first block loops least everywhere.
The interpretation is mechanical. A model that must
produce a usable prediction after a single pass has to
put the computation into that pass, and every later
pass can then only be a small correction; a model
that is never evaluated at R=1 is free to spread the
computation across passes and use each at full strength.
The {2, 3, 4} run is the test: the schedule is the only
difference from R ∼{1, . . . , 6}, and the gates recover.
The 3-block models show the same ordering with a
milder collapse (mean gate 0.81 fixed, 0.53 under R ∼
{1, . . . , 6}; Appendix A), plausibly because three blocks

5.2. Loss vs inference R
The three gate profiles correspond to three loss curves
(Figure 3, Table 2). The fixed-R model is a two-pass
network: 4.96 at R=1, best at R=2, +0.09 at R=3 and
+0.83 at R=6, where the two seeds disagree most (4.06
and 3.43), so its loss beyond the trained depth is not
a stable property of the schedule. The R ∼{1, . . . , 6}
model is flat from R=2 to R=6 (spread 0.016) and
usable at R=1 (3.00, against 2.951 for the plain model,
so the one-pass regime costs 0.05 nats for having been
trained as part of a loop), but the whole gain from
R=1 to its best point is 0.085 nats and that best point
(2.916 at R=4) only matches the fixed-R model’s R=2
(2.919). The R ∼{2, 3, 4} model is the best looped
model (2.909 at R=3), flat over its trained range (2.927
/ 2.909 / 2.911), degrades by 0.038 at R=8, and is not
usable at R=1 (4.60) because it never trained there.
Its margin over the fixed-R model is 0.010, small but of
the same sign and size in both seeds (0.009 and 0.010)
against a seed-to-seed difference of 0.001–0.003 at any
trained R. Sampling costs 0.008 (R ∼{2, 3, 4}) and
0.013 (R ∼{1, . . . , 6}) nats at the matched R=2. The
optimum is at R=3–4 at every checkpoint from 10k
steps on, in both seeds, and the per-domain curves
have identical shape.
None of the three schedules shows more than 0.09 nats
of test-time scaling on language-modeling loss, and all
saturate by R ≈4. We read this as a property of the
metric and the scale rather than of the loop: Huang et
al. (12) report looped models whose perplexity trails
a dense baseline while their generative accuracy leads,
and the reasoning gains of Geiping et al. (7), Zhu et
al. (8) and Marchenko et al. (9) are on tasks where a
pass can change a discrete answer. What the schedule
buys at no quality cost is the freedom to run the model
at any R in its trained range.

5.3. Direction settles, norm grows
Prairie et al. (23) report, using the relative residual
step, that stochastic-depth training converges to a fixed
point across recurrences and static-depth training does
not. Our traces reproduce the relative-step picture
and add a distinction. Under fixed R=2 the median
direction change δi falls by 0.6–0.8× per pass in every
block out to pass 6 (block 5: 0.047, 0.017, 0.010, 0.006,
0.004 at passes 2–6), while the residual norm grows by
about 9% per pass (Table A3) and the relative step
floors at 0.12–0.20; at pass 6 the two account for the
floor together, since rel2 ≈(r−1)2+2δ with r the norm
ratio gives 0.0892 + 2 × 0.0031 = 0.1192 against the
measured 0.124. In a pre-norm block every sublayer and
the output projection read Norm(x), so the direction
is the state and the norm is invisible to them: by the
metric that matters, the fixed-R loop converges. The
loss over the same passes rises from 2.92 to 4.06. The
extra passes move the direction a little, consistently,
toward a state the output projection never saw; the
static-depth failure is in where the iteration converges,
not whether.
Under sampled R the two measures agree: the direction
settles through pass 8 and the loss is flat through pass

Two consequences follow. Norm-based stopping rules
are unsafe for a fixed-depth looped model, and sampled-
depth training is what makes hidden-state settling a
usable proxy for output stability; the step-size exit rule
of Pappone et al. (22) is defined on a sampled-depth
model and is consistent with this. And the relative
step, because it carries the norm, is the wrong metric
for diagnosing why a static-depth model fails to share
its cache; the direction metric isolates the drift that
matters.
5.4. Forward static KV
Reusing the first pass’s keys and values on later passes
costs 0.012–0.026 nats at the best inference R in ev-
ery regime (Table 4), less at 6 blocks than at 3, con-
stant through training and uniform across domains
(Table A4). Training throughput rises by 2% at fixed
R=2, 7% at mean R=3 and 8% at mean R=3.5, be-
cause only the KV projections of the loop passes are
skipped and the 32k-vocabulary head dominates per-
token FLOPs at this size.
At inference the model
decodes with the cache of a plain model of the same
block count, which is the same memory saving that
backward cache sharing obtains after training (§6).
The dynamics change more than the loss. With static
KV the attention output on a later pass is a convex
combination of fixed vectors, so a drifting query can
only re-weight a fixed set; under fixed R=2 this halves
the damage of over-iteration (+0.60 at R=6 vs +1.15),
and under R ∼{1, . . . , 6} the per-token iteration is
uniformly contractive (ratios 0.7–0.9 in every block,
no expansive block) with 44% of (token, block) pairs
with δ2 < 10−3 against 11% for fresh KV. The gates
also shift: with a fixed memory the attention gate
of early blocks drops (block 0 of the 3-block fixed-R
model: 0.99 →0.53) and the second pass’s work moves

5.5. Context: the depth ladder
For reference, looping recovers a fraction of what real
depth provides. Looping a 3-block model twice im-
proves on the plain 3-layer model by 0.057 nats, 35% of
the 0.161 gap to the plain 6-layer model, at the same
FLOPs and throughput (86k vs 89k tok/s). Looping
the 6-block model recovers 24% of the gap to the plain
12-block model (0.032 of 0.133). At equal compute the
plain model wins by about 0.10 nats in both pairs, and
the fraction recovered falls with depth. The gain is the
same size on fineweb, code and math (Table A4).

both. A block applied R times can be a deeper network
with shared weights or an iteration toward a fixed point,
and the training objective decides which.

produces the former: the second pass is a full layer,
with gates that approach 1 in the later blocks, and since
training only ever runs and scores the two-pass output,
no other depth is ever trained for, so the model is usable
only at R=2. Sampling down to R=1 produces the
latter: every pass must be safe to skip, so every pass
is small, with gates of 0.1–0.5. The {2, 3, 4} schedule
shows the two are separable. What the lower bound
removes is the demand for a one-pass answer; what the
sampling over 2–4 keeps is robustness in that range.
For practitioners the recommendation is short: sample
R from a range that starts at 2, never at 1, unless a
one-pass model is itself a requirement.
Relation to recent recipes. Prairie et al. (23) obtain
convergence through stochastic depth drawn from a
Poisson distribution, which includes small depths, and
observe that it is necessary but not sufficient for lossless
cache sharing at 770M and 1.3B parameters. The gate
collapse we see under a lower bound of 1 is a candidate
explanation for the insufficiency: a loop whose passes
are near-skips has little to share.
Jeddi et al. (10)
train on trajectories of varying length with a shortcut-
consistency loss that explicitly asks short runs to be
usable and long runs to refine; the {2, 3, 4} schedule is
the minimal version of that idea, one flag rather than
a new objective, and it would be natural to measure
where the two differ. Huang et al. (12) learn a monotone
step size across loops; our constant per-block gates are
the simplest member of that family, and their collapse
under R ∼{1, . . . , 6} is the model choosing the step
size that Huang et al. would schedule.
Direction versus norm. The pre-norm architecture
reads only the direction of the residual stream, and a
trained block applied past its trained depth rotates that
direction by a shrinking amount each pass while adding
to its norm. This defeats both obvious stopping rules,
i.e. rules that watch the change between consecutive
passes and stop looping once it is small. A norm-based
rule (the relative step reli, or any measure that includes
the vector’s length) never fires under the model trained
with fixed R=2, because the norm grows about 9%
per pass and the step floors at 0.12–0.20; it reports
non-convergence while the direction has in fact settled.
A direction-based rule (δi) fires by pass 4–5, and is
right about the hidden state, but the loss over those
passes rises from 2.92 to 4.06, because the direction is
settling toward a state the output projection never saw;
it reports convergence while the predictions are getting
worse. Neither signal, taken from the hidden state
alone, says whether another pass helps. A stopping
rule for a looped model therefore has to be stated in
output space (the successive-output KL of Table 3,
or the loss itself), or the model has to be trained so
that hidden-state settling and output stability coincide,
which sampled depth does: under the model trained
with R ∼{2, 3, 4} the loss is flat over the range in
which the direction settles.
Forward versus backward KV. Freezing the first
pass’s memory decouples the per-token fixed points and
makes the iteration contractive, at a small and regime-
independent cost; broadcasting the last pass’s memory
backward after training recovers the same cache saving,
zero-shot when pretraining sampled the depth and with
a short finetuning stage otherwise. The forward vari-
ant is a training-time choice with a dynamics benefit,
the backward one a post-training choice that leaves
pretraining unchanged; which is preferable depends on
whether the model is being trained anyway.
What the loop buys. At equal parameters, about
a third of what doubling the depth provides; at equal
FLOPs, nothing, on language-modeling loss at this
scale. The case for looping rests on parameter efficiency
and on gains that perplexity does not measure, which
is consistent with how (7–9, 12) report their results.

Future work: what to loop. Every looped language
model we know of, including ours, iterates the full block;
where adapters appear they ride on the looped block
(5), install the loop in a pretrained one (32), or select
which tokens receive extra full-backbone passes (33).
Our gates point at a cheaper loop body: in the early
and middle blocks of every fresh-KV looped model the
attention gate exceeds the FFN gate (Table A5; the
block means are 0.71 vs 0.63 under fixed R=2 and 0.65
vs 0.62 under R ∼{2, 3, 4}, with the last block the
exception at 0.5–0.7 vs 0.9), so re-reading the sequence
with a refined query was worth more than re-running
the per-token FFN, which is also the largest single cost
of a pass; under static KV the ordering reverses, as §5.4
showed. We therefore intend to compare three loop
bodies, i.e. three choices of what runs on passes 2 to R
while pass 1 stays a full block, each with fresh and with
static KV, under the {2, 3, 4} schedule, on loss against
inference FLOPs. The first is the full block studied
here: attention and FFN on every pass. The second
keeps attention but computes the FFN only on the first
pass; on later passes a small low-rank adapter takes the
FFN’s place and the first pass’s FFN output is added
back unchanged, which makes a loop pass cost roughly
40% of a full one. The third drops attention as well
and runs only the adapter, so a pass is a per-token re-
finement with no cross-token communication, at about
1% of a full pass’s cost. Dong et al. (20) showed that
pure attention collapses in rank and that the FFN is
what prevents it, so the adapter is the minimal nonlin-
earity that should keep the attention-only loop from
over-smoothing, and the re-injected first-pass output is
the anchor that made the self-consistent joiner of (27)
converge in three iterations. The R ∼{2, . . . , 6} con-
trol for §5.1, deep supervision at intermediate passes,
looping only the deeper blocks, and a replication at
150M–600M parameters with downstream tasks com-
plete the list.
7. Limitations
All models have 21–85M non-embedding parameters
and were trained on 2B tokens on one consumer GPU;
the effects are 0.01–0.1 nats and we make no claim
about scale. The headline comparison (the plain 6-
block model and the looped 6-block model under each
of the three schedules) has two seeds, which agree to
within 0.003 nats at every trained loop count and give
the same gate profiles; every other run is single-seed,
and the pattern’s consistency across two depths, three
domains and both KV policies is our evidence that
it is not noise. We evaluate language-modeling loss
only, so we cannot say whether the shallow test-time
scaling reflects the metric or the models. The block
has no input injection, step embedding or truncated
backpropagation, which keeps the comparison minimal
but differs from the recipes of (7–9); all blocks loop with
the same R although the gates suggest the first block
should not. The convergence analysis is in hidden-state
space; the output-space metric (the successive-output
KL of Table 3) is reported for one seed. We did not test
tasks that require tokens to read each other’s refined
states, which is where forward static KV should cost
most.
8. Conclusion
What matters in a loop-count schedule is its smallest
value of iteration count, not the fact that R is sampled.
If training ever runs the block only once, every extra
pass learns to be a small correction (the gates settle
at 0.1–0.5); if every training step runs the block at
least twice, each extra pass learns to act as a full layer
(gates of 0.6–0.9). The learned gates make this visible,
and changing just the lower bound of the sampling
range from 1 to 2 is enough to switch between the
two regimes. A model trained with R sampled from
{2, 3, 4} matches the quality of the model trained at
fixed R=2 and, unlike it, can be run at any R in its
trained range and beyond without loss.
The loop converges, and it converges in direction: with
every pass the residual stream rotates by a shrinking
amount while its norm keeps growing, and since a
pre-norm block reads only the direction, the iteration
approaches a self-consistent state, one that a further
pass maps back onto itself, in the same way that the
self-consistent joiner of our earlier work (27) settled
against a fixed memory. With sampled R the state
settles and the loss stays flat; with fixed R the state
settles toward a point the output layer never saw, so
the loss gets worse. A fixed-R model therefore cannot
tell from its hidden state when to stop looping.
Forward static KV, which freezes the first pass’s keys
and values for the later passes, costs 0.01–0.03 nats in
every regime, makes the iteration uniformly contractive,
halves the inference cache, and is complementary to
backward sharing of the final pass’s cache after training.
These are measured claims at small scale, released with
their runs and traces so they can be checked at larger
ones.
