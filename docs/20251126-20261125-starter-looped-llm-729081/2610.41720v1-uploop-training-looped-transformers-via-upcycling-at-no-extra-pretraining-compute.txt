Compute

Abstract
Looped language models can strengthen reason-
ing through repeated computation with shared
weights, increasing execution depth without
adding independent parameters. However, each
additional pass increases training floating-point
operations (FLOPs), making these gains more ex-
pensive to obtain. To address this cost, we propose
UpLoop, a pretraining schedule that uses upcy-
cling to train looped Transformers while exactly
matching a standard dense baseline in final pa-
rameter count, total training tokens, and training
FLOPs. Specifically, UpLoop begins with a shal-
low Transformer, progressively grows its depth
by copying learned layers, and uses the compu-
tation saved early to support repeated execution
of a shared middle block later in training. Experi-
ments across model scales show improvements in
language modeling and downstream performance,
with the average score across 22 downstream tasks
increasing from 22.99 to 27.52 at 1.3B parame-
ters. These gains also extend to mathematical
reasoning after supervised fine-tuning.
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong
capabilities in mathematical reasoning (Muennighoff et al.,
2025; Xin et al., 2025) and agentic coding (Wang et al.,
2025; Yang et al., 2024a; 2025). These capabilities have
advanced substantially with increases in model size and
training data (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Alongside scaling
parameters and data, looped language models introduce an
additional axis for scaling through iterative computation
(Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Alabdulmohsin &
Zhai, 2025; Bae et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2026). Specifi-
cally, they repeatedly apply a shared Transformer block to
increase execution depth at a fixed number of independent

However, the benefits of repeated computation come with

parameters, training tokens, and training FLOPs?
To accommodate the additional computation required by
looping within the dense baseline’s training budget, we
introduce a pretraining schedule that reallocates computa-
tion across training stages. A fixed total compute budget
constrains the accumulated training cost, while allowing dif-
ferent stages to use different execution depths. We allocate
less computation to early tokens and progressively increase
depth, carrying learned parameters forward across stages
(Shen et al., 2022; Du et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2024; Saunshi

weight looping. Specifically, UpLoop first trains a shallow
Transformer and progressively copies its learned layers to
increase depth. Once the model reaches its target depth,
it repeatedly executes a middle block with shared weights,
gaining additional execution depth without adding model

Across three model scales, UpLoop improves held-out lan-
guage modeling and average downstream performance over
the dense baseline under equal training token and FLOP bud-
gets. Specifically, the average score across 22 downstream
tasks increases from 22.99 to 27.52 at 1.3B parameters.
These gains also extend to downstream mathematical rea-
soning after supervised fine-tuning. Further analyses show
more aligned gradients across shared calls, functional dif-
ferentiation among copied layers, and improved contextual
retrieval.
2. Related Work
Looped and recursive language models.
Reusing a block
along the depth axis goes back to Universal Transformers
(Dehghani et al., 2019) and has recently been revived as a
way to buy iterative computation at a fixed parameter count
(Yang et al., 2024b; Saunshi et al., 2025; Geiping et al.,
2025; Zhu et al., 2025), sometimes with the shared block
relaxed by per-pass adapters or experts (Csord´as et al., 2024;
Bae et al., 2025a;b), with the reported gains concentrated in
reasoning (Saunshi et al., 2025; Kapl et al., 2026). Because
a looped model executes more layers than the dense model
it is compared against, those comparisons hold parameters,
and sometimes tokens, fixed and pay for the extra execution
depth with extra compute at both training and inference time.
Concurrent scaling-law work makes the dependence explicit:
at matched parameters and data, looping lowers loss (Prairie
et al., 2026), but at matched computational depth more
recurrence is worse once model size and data are jointly
optimised (Schwethelm et al., 2026), and compute-matched
gains have so far been reported for sparse looped mixtures
of experts (Wang et al., 2026). Our budget is stricter than
any of these: both the tokens and the training FLOPs are the
dense baseline’s, to the batch.
Depth growth and upcycling.
A parallel line reuses a
trained model to initialise a larger one by stacking or du-
plicating layers (Chen et al., 2015; Gong et al., 2019; Shen
et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Yao et al., 2024; Kim et al.,
2024; Du et al., 2024), or by turning a dense checkpoint
into a sparse one (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023). The origi-
nal motivation is saving training FLOPs—Du et al. (2024)
find depthwise stacking the most efficient operator, and the
grown models of Kapl et al. (2025) reach the baseline’s final
depth with roughly 77% of its training compute—and Saun-
shi et al. (2024) then showed that duplicating in the middle
of the stack carries an inductive bias toward reasoning, a
view Kapl et al. (2026) extend into one picture of growing
and looping as implicit versus explicit repetition. Closest to
the control we need, Chen et al. (2026) grow a looped core
with its weights either tied or untied at matched FLOPs and
report that both improve the compute-optimal scaling expo-
nent, untied ahead of tied by a constant factor; the choice
of which block to duplicate also matters (Karp et al., 2024).
Grown and upcycled models keep the duplicated weights
independent and are dense at inference. Growth can stop at
the baseline’s final depth, saving compute at fixed tokens,
or use the saved compute to train on more tokens at fixed
FLOPs. UpLoop spends the dense baseline’s budget exactly

models by repeatedly applying shared weights. This addi-

trade-off motivates using the computation saved during early
shallow training to support later looping.
4. Methodology
We present UpLoop, a four-stage pretraining schedule that
combines progressive depth growth with middle-block loop-
ing, as shown in Figure 3. Specifically, UpLoop progres-
sively expands a shallow Transformer through layer copying
and trains the model at each depth before enabling shared-
weight looping in the final stage. Through progressive depth
growth and the allocation of training tokens across stages,
UpLoop accommodates the additional computation required
by looping while exactly matching the dense baseline in
final parameter count, total training tokens, and analytical
training FLOPs.

5. Experiments
In this section, we begin by presenting the experimental
setup (Section 5.1), followed by a comparison between
UpLoop and the dense baseline under equal training tokens
and FLOPs (Section 5.2.1).
5.1. Experimental Setup
Models and Datasets. We evaluate UpLoop through lan-
guage modeling and downstream tasks across three model
scales. (i) Pre-training. We pre-train decoder-only Trans-
formers based on the OLMo 2 Ladder architecture (Team
OLMo, 2025) on FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024), with
370M, 760M and 1.3B non-embedding parameters. All
models are trained at 100 tokens per parameter, five times
the Chinchilla token budget. At each scale, UpLoop and
Vanilla use the same training tokens and total training
FLOPs. Detailed model configurations and training bud-
gets are provided in Appendix A. (ii) Downstream eval-
uation.
We evaluate the pretrained checkpoints using
two benchmark suites. First, we evaluate all three model
scales on eight zero-shot tasks: PIQA (Bisk et al., 2019),
SIQA (Sap et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019),
WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), ARC-c (Clark et al.,
2018), ARC-e (Clark et al., 2018), OBQA (Mihaylov et al.,
2018) and BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), using the standard
prompts in lm-evaluation-harness (Sutawika et al., 2025).
Second, we evaluate the 370M, 760M and 1.3B models on
22 downstream tasks covering closed-book and open-book
question answering, language understanding, math word
problems and reasoning primitives, following Kapl et al.
(2026). The mathematics and reasoning primitive tasks use
five-shot evaluation. Detailed evaluation protocols and ag-
gregation rules are provided in Appendix C. (iii) Language
modeling. We evaluate language modeling performance us-
ing negative log-likelihood (NLL) on a fixed held-out set of
2,048 FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024) sequences, each
containing 4,096 tokens, shared across all models.
Baselines. We compare UpLoop with the following base-
lines: (i) VANILLA, a standard dense Transformer following
OLMo 2; (ii) Loop from scratch, a model trained with
the looped architecture throughout training, starting from
random initialization; (iii) Loop with dense initialization,
which first trains a full-depth dense model and then con-
tinues training with looping; (iv) Dense-20, which uses in-
dependent layers to match UpLoop’s final execution depth,
with either matched training tokens or matched training
FLOPs; and (v) LIDAS, which progressively grows a dense

5.2.1. RESULTS ACROSS MODEL SCALES
Language Modeling. Figure 2 and Appendix Table A2
compare UpLoop with Vanilla across three model scales,
from 370M to 1.3B, under equal training token and FLOP
budgets. UpLoop achieves lower NLL at all three scales. At
370M, it reduces NLL from 2.78 to 2.74, while at 1.3B, NLL
decreases from 2.43 to 2.40. These results demonstrate that
UpLoop consistently improves language modeling across
all model sizes under equal training budgets.
Zero-shot Commonsense Reasoning. Table 1 reports zero-
shot performance on eight benchmarks under equal training
token and FLOP budgets. At 370M, 760M and 1.3B, Up-
Loop improves average accuracy from 48.43% to 49.78%,
from 51.96% to 53.52%, and from 54.91% to 56.47%, re-
spectively. It outperforms Vanilla on all six eligible tasks at
370M, all seven at 760M, and five of seven at 1.3B. These
results show that the pretraining gains consistently translate
into improved zero-shot performance across all evaluated
model scales.
Reasoning and Knowledge Evaluation.
Table 2 compares UpLoop with Vanilla on 22 downstream
tasks covering question answering, language understanding,
mathematics and reasoning under equal training token and
FLOP budgets. At 370M, 760M and 1.3B, UpLoop im-
proves the average score from 18.95 to 20.90, from 21.38 to
24.82, and from 22.99 to 27.52, respectively. It outperforms
Vanilla on 13 of 13, 16 of 21, and 18 of 22 eligible tasks at
the three scales. These results demonstrate that UpLoop im-
proves overall downstream performance across all evaluated
model scales.
The largest gains in Table 2 occur on reasoning primitives,
particularly variable assignment. At 1.3B, UpLoop im-
proves accuracy from 58.10% to 91.10% on VarAssign-
math and from 66.60% to 91.20% on VarAssign-code.
Open-book question answering also benefits, with CoQA
F1 increasing from 38.85 to 44.38 at the same scale. These
results highlight UpLoop’s advantage in strengthening rea-
soning and context-based question answering under equal
training token and FLOP budgets.
UpLoop also improves performance on math word prob-
lems. At 1.3B, it outperforms Vanilla on all six eligible
benchmarks, increasing average accuracy from 5.35% to
7.55%. For example, accuracy improves from 6.18% to
9.94% on ASDiv and from 4.43% to 7.20% on MAWPS
SingleEq. These results show that UpLoop’s benefits extend
to mathematical reasoning under equal training budgets.

5.4. Further Analysis
To understand these performance gains, we further analyze
the final pretrained models. We first compare UpLoop with
the looping baselines in gradient alignment across calls

To examine how ancestry relates to layer function, we trace
each layer through the full growth history to its initial source
and compare substitutions within and across ancestry groups.
A replacement layer processes the target layer’s input, and
its attention or feed-forward (MLP) update replaces the orig-
inal after tokenwise norm matching. Figure 5 shows that
substitutions between layers sharing an initial ancestor incur

with different ancestors. The lower yet nonzero substitu-

these layers retain a common computational basis while
developing distinct computations.

Hyperparameters.
All pretraining experiments use
AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), ϵ = 10−8 and weight de-
cay 0.1 on every parameter except the input embedding,
gradient clipping at 1.0, and an auxiliary z-loss of 10−5.
The learning rate warms up linearly over as many tokens
as the model has non-embedding parameters and then de-
cays by a cosine to 10% of its peak; the schedule runs on
a token clock that is identical for both arms, so a stage
switch changes the architecture and never the learning rate.
Peak learning rates are the Ladder values for each width
(Table A1) and are not retuned for UpLoop, which is deliber-

keeps the configuration it was tuned for and UpLoop inherits
it unchanged. Compute is BF16 with FP32 master weights
and Adam states, and activation recomputation is off. We
periodically evaluate held-out NLL during pretraining to
monitor training dynamics. Downstream evaluations use the
final checkpoint of each run. Every run uses seed 42 unless

GSM8K training split for three epochs using AdamW, a peak
learning rate of 2 × 10−5, six warmup steps followed by co-

a maximum sequence length of 1,024 tokens, with no train-

greedy decoding without a calculator, a 512-token gener-

following ####. The fine-tuning compute is not matched.
Both models exceed the random-answer bound of 1.74%,
and UpLoop improves over Vanilla by 3.34 percentage
points. The comparison uses one training seed (42).
Five-shot evaluation uses five fixed chain-of-thought exam-

F. Additional Mechanistic Analysis
We further analyze the final pretrained models through cross-
call output substitution, attention-component interventions,
and task-specific information reading. The first two analyses
compare the 370M UpLoop, Loop with dense initialization,
and Loop from scratch models, which share the same final
looped architecture. The contextual retrieval analysis uses
760M and 1.3B UpLoop and Vanilla models. Experimental
details are provided in Appendix G.
F.1. Cross-call Output Compatibility
Shared parameters process different hidden states across
calls, motivating us to examine whether updates from the
first call remain usable in the second. In each intervention,
we replace the attention or MLP update at one second-call
core layer with the update from its first call, match the native
second-call norm tokenwise, and recompute the remaining
network. Table A7 reports the mean ∆NLL across separate
substitutions at the four core positions.
UpLoop incurs substitution costs of 0.00919 for attention
and 0.01929 for MLP, both lower than those of the two
looping baselines. These results indicate greater functional
compatibility between sublayer updates produced across
calls in UpLoop.

F.2. Compatibility and Difference Propagation along the
Value Path
F.2.1. ATTENTION WEIGHTS AND VALUES
To localize the components underlying attention-output com-
patibility, we separately reuse first-call attention weights and
value vectors, and additionally norm-match the reused val-
ues. Figure 7 shows that norm-matched value reuse incurs
a cost of 0.00352 in UpLoop, compared with 0.00809 for
Loop with dense initialization and 0.01546 for Loop from
scratch. Reusing attention weights alone yields a less dis-
tinct difference between UpLoop and the former baseline.
Values thus provide a clearer component-level instance of
cross-call compatibility, with their lower substitution cost
persisting after amplitude control.
F.2.2. PROPAGATION OF SUBSTITUTION-INDUCED
DIFFERENCES
We next trace how differences introduced by old-value sub-
stitution propagate through the forward path. Keeping native
Q/K at the intervention site, we measure successive RMS
ratios through attention averaging, output projection, output
normalization, and the remaining network. Figure 8 shows
an output-normalization gain of 0.261 for UpLoop, com-
pared with 0.306 and 0.332 for the two baselines. From the
normalized attention update to the final state, the respective
gains are 1.708, 2.322, and 2.445.
These measurements locate lower difference transmission at
output normalization and subsequent propagation: along the
changes induced by actual old-value substitution, UpLoop
attenuates more at normalization and amplifies less through
the remaining network. Output projection exhibits a differ-
ent ordering across models, revealing distinct responses to
substitution-induced differences across stages.

G. Further Analysis: Experimental Details
Gradient statistics.
For Figure 4, we unroll shared calls
into independent copies with identical weights at the final
checkpoint and backpropagate the prediction loss to obtain
call-specific gradient contributions. Cosine similarity uses
gradients over all parameters within each layer, averaged
first over the four shared layers and then over 12 validation
windows of 256 tokens each.
State perturbations.
For Table 5, we inject a single
random-direction perturbation per token after the first core
pass, with magnitude equal to 1% of its pre-core state norm.
Trajectory distance is the mean tokenwise L2 distance be-
tween the perturbed and unperturbed states, normalized by
the distance immediately after perturbation. Results average
512 windows.
Layer substitution.
For Figure 5, targets are the four first-
pass core layers. Replacement layers are grouped by initial
ancestry traced through the full growth history, excluding
the target itself. Each replacement layer processes the tar-
get layer’s input, and its attention or MLP update replaces
the original after tokenwise norm matching. Each of the
64 windows contains 256 tokens, with the final 64 scored.
Substitution costs are averaged within each ancestry group
per window, then across windows.
VarAssign position groups.
Position analysis in Figure 6
uses all 1,000 examples per task from the main evaluation,
grouping targets in the first four assignments and the final
assignment. Each group uses the same random-answer cri-
terion as the main text, with eligible tasks weighted equally.
Math and Code are eligible in every group and both enter
the average. Reading blocks and binding swaps use 128
examples per task.
Reading blocks.
For Table 6, attention from all tokens at
and after the query start to the target-value token is blocked
in all four core layers during the second call and renormal-
ized over the remaining positions. The control blocks an
unrelated value token in the same example. Each task uses
the same 128 original examples as the variable-binding swap
experiment in Appendix F.3.1.
