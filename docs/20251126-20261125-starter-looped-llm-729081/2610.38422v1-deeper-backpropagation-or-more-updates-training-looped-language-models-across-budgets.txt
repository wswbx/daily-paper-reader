Deeper Backpropagation or More Updates?
Training Looped Language Models Across Budgets
Jiarun Shen
Xiao Li
The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen
jiarunshen@link.cuhk.edu.cn
lixiao@cuhk.edu.cn
Abstract
Repeating transformer layers can improve lan-
guage model performance without adding pa-
rameters. However, the added computational
depth increases both forward and backward
computation costs during training. Training
such models requires choosing a backward win-
dow that controls how many final layer repeti-
tions retain gradients during backpropagation.
Longer windows improve per-token learning
efficiency but increase per-update cost, while
shorter windows reduce per-update cost but
sacrifice per-token efficiency. Understanding
which window performs better requires exam-
ining different training budgets. Prior work
compares backward windows across compu-
tational budgets. When training budgets in-
crease, it remains unclear whether the same
window continues to perform best. We measure
how the relative performance of two windows
shifts as training budgets grow. Our method iso-
lates per-token efficiency by training both win-
dows on identical data, and compares complete
training recipes at approximately equal training
time at each budget. Results show that shorter
windows lose much of their advantage as bud-
gets grow. Controlled experiments show that
both benefits decrease at larger budgets, with a
greater decline in the benefit of extended train-
ing. Backward window comparisons depend on
the available budget, and the preferred window
among the tested candidates can change as the
budget grows.

Introduction
Repeating transformer layers can improve language
model performance without adding parameters (De-
hghani et al., 2019; Prairie et al., 2026). Each
pass through the shared layers constitutes one re-
currence iteration, producing multiple stages of
computation from the same parameters. Repeating
layers increases computational depth, which raises
both forward and backward computation costs dur-
ing training. Training such models requires choos-
ing how many recurrence iterations to retain for
backpropagation. We define the backward window
as the maximum number of trailing recurrence iter-
ations through which gradients propagate. Truncat-
ing the backward window reduces backward com-
putation cost while preserving all forward passes.
Architecture and inference computation remain un-
changed regardless of which backward window is
used during training.
Choosing a backward window determines how
training time divides between computation per up-
date and total number of updates. Deeper back-
propagation improves per-token efficiency when
training data is held fixed. Shorter windows reduce
per-update cost, permitting more updates within
a fixed training-time budget. Two factors govern
backward window selection. First, per-token ef-
ficiency measures how much deeper backpropa-
gation improves learning from a fixed quantity of
training data. Second, gains from extended training
quantify the benefit of running additional optimiza-
tion updates. When training budgets increase, per-
token efficiency and extended training gains may
shift by different magnitudes. Understanding back-
ward window selection requires examining whether
a window that performs best under limited compute
continues to perform best when more compute be-
comes available.
Prior work establishes components of the back-
ward window trade-off. Parcae compares backward
windows at a fixed token count and documents
per-token efficiency gains from deeper backprop-
agation (Prairie et al., 2026). Schwethelm et al.
(2026) examine truncated backpropagation across
multiple compute budgets, including how saved
computation supports additional training tokens.
We build on this work by measuring matched-time
differences at multiple budgets with paired runs
that share random seeds, and decomposing these
differences using additional equal-token controls.
Paired comparisons at each budget measure how

shorter windows perform when allocated more up-

controls isolate per-token efficiency from extended
training contributions, quantifying how each factor
shifts with budget scale.
We conduct two complementary comparisons at
each budget level. Equal-token comparisons train
models with both backward windows on identical
training data and measure per-token efficiency dif-
ferences. Matched-time comparisons allocate ap-
proximately equal training time to each backward
window, giving the shorter window more updates
within the same time budget. The first compari-
son isolates per-token efficiency by holding data
fixed. The second comparison measures combined
gains from both per-token efficiency and extended
training. Extended training here refers to a com-
plete training recipe with additional optimization
updates and a schedule adjusted for the larger up-
date count.
We apply this method to backward windows of
four and eight recurrence iterations in a looped
language model. Model architecture, forward re-
currence iteration distribution during training, and
inference iteration count remain fixed within each
paired comparison. Each comparison uses the same
random seed for both backward windows. The
main experiment compares a baseline training bud-
get with a budget four times larger. Measured per-
update costs determine how many additional up-
dates the shorter window receives in matched-time
comparisons.
At the smaller budget, the shorter window
trained with additional updates achieves lower test
loss than the longer window across all training
seeds. At the larger budget, this advantage shrinks
in all seeds evaluated at both budgets. Controlled
experiments show that per-token efficiency from
deeper backpropagation and gains from extended
training both diminish at larger budgets. Extended
training gains diminish more, explaining the shift
in matched-time comparisons. Training budget
shapes the balance between backward window
depth and extended training.
Our contributions are:
• Empirical evidence that shorter backward win-
dows consistently see diminished matched-
time advantage as training budgets grow,
demonstrated across training seeds, learning
rates, and model configurations.
• Controlled decomposition of matched-time
shifts into per-token efficiency and extended
training gains, showing that extended training
gains diminish more than per-token efficiency
gains at larger budgets.

Related Work
Backward-window ablations.
Universal Trans-
formers introduced recurrence over shared transfor-
mations (Dehghani et al., 2019). Public recurrent-
depth models reuse shared blocks to increase com-
putation without proportionally increasing unique
parameters. Huginn truncates backpropagation to
the last eight recurrent steps to control training
compute and memory (Geiping et al., 2025). We
use Parcae’s architecture and optimizer framework
(Prairie et al., 2026). Parcae’s Appendix I, Fig-
ure 16 and Table 10 vary backward windows from 4
to 12 at mean forward recurrence 20 and 8.5B train-
ing tokens. Parcae establishes per-token efficiency
gains from deeper backpropagation at fixed token
counts. We examine whether per-token efficiency
gains justify their cost when shorter windows use
saved time for additional updates, and whether this
trade-off shifts across training budgets.
Compute allocation and recurrence value.
Schwethelm et al. (2026, §5.1) study truncated
backpropagation across compute budgets and
widths, allocating saved computation to additional
tokens. Their study already includes multi-budget
truncation experiments and fixed-model compar-
isons. We measure the same-seed change in the
relative loss of two backward windows, test its sen-
sitivity to learning-rate selection and model con-
figuration, and add equal-token endpoints to deter-
mine whether the window gap itself grows. These
controls clarify the observed contraction beyond
the established use of truncation savings for more
tokens. The comparisons do not provide a predic-
tive scaling law.
Gradient estimation and evaluation.
Adaptive
truncation controls gradient bias (Aicher et al.,
2020); randomized truncation and telescoping con-
struct compensated estimators (Tallec and Ollivier,
2017; Beatson and Adams, 2019). We compare
completed fixed-window recipes. Pretraining loss
and downstream performance may not align (Liu
et al., 2023; Saunshi et al., 2025), so downstream
scores provide additional evidence beyond NLL
comparisons. We distinguish training-seed varia-
tion (Sellam et al., 2022) from block-resampling

uncertainty that conditions on fixed checkpoints
(Dror et al., 2018).

Experimental Design
We measure language modeling by negative log-
likelihood (NLL) in nats per token, where lower
is better. For downstream evaluation, CORE is
the mean chance-adjusted accuracy across 22 tasks.
CORE×100 has 0 at the tasks’ chance baselines
and 100 at perfect accuracy. The CORE metric
is distinct from the model’s shared Core layers.
LAMBADA measures final-word accuracy. Higher
downstream scores are better.
3.1
Models and backward windows
The primary Parcae configuration has 144,323,136
parameters and width 768. Two input Transformer
blocks (the Prelude) produce representation e from
token embeddings. Each iteration applies the same
two shared Core blocks to the recurrent state, inject-
ing e at each step. Two output blocks (the Coda)
and a prediction head produce next-token logits.
The second configuration has 388,003,328 param-
eters, width 1,024, and four blocks each in the
Prelude, Core, and Coda.
Training draws per-sequence forward depths
Ti = max(1, Poisson(16)). A backward window
k retains gradients through the final min(k, Ti) ac-
tive iterations. Earlier iterations execute in the for-
ward pass without retaining gradients. We detach
the recurrent state at the boundary; retained in-
jections of e preserve gradient paths to the Pre-
lude.
All original parameters remain trainable.
Architecture and sampled forward depths remain
fixed within each configuration, and primary eval-
uation uses Teval= 16.
Four-, six-, and eight-
iteration backpropagation are denoted K4, K6, and
K8. FULL retains every sampled recurrence. Fig-
ure 1 illustrates backward window truncation; Al-
gorithm 1 details batching.
Training uses a document-deduplicated packed
FineWeb-Edu subset (Penedo et al., 2024), the Par-
cae tokenizer, and sequence length 1,024. Each
update accumulates 16 microbatches of eight se-
quences, consuming 131,072 targets. The longest
run uses 2,633,891,840 targets from the 5.088B-
token stream. Appendix A records data, licenses,
hyperparameters, and software.

Evaluation and uncertainty
Dev and test NLL use 1,950,720 and 10,485,760
targets. All 36 new endpoints have fixed-T16 dev
and test results. These endpoints add neither down-
stream evaluations nor depth curves to the original

Relation to diminishing returns.
A smaller ex-
tension benefit can arise from ordinary diminishing
returns. For illustration, suppose a fixed-recipe
endpoint curve follows f(D) = L∞+aD−β, with
a, β > 0. Extending its token budget by a fixed
factor q > 1 gives
f(D) −f(qD) = a(1 −q−β)D−β,
which decreases with D. This assumed curve is
not fitted to our results. It also cannot determine
the matched-time ordering without the second win-
dow. The additional equal-token runs supply that
comparison. The measured window gap decreases
too, but by less than the extension benefit. An in-
creasing per-token efficiency gap between K4 and
K8 therefore cannot explain contraction in these
controls. Each endpoint uses a complete horizon-
dependent recipe, so the comparison does not iso-
late a data-only effect or an optimization mecha-
nism. Two main budgets and one single-seed inter-
mediate point do not determine a crossover location
or the shape of either learning curve.

K6 has lower test NLL than K8 in all three

6.2
Downstream scope
Downstream task performance does not follow
a uniform pattern. At the small budget, the ex-
ploratory LAMBADA mean favors K8 by 0.375
percentage points, whereas both replication seeds
favor extended four-iteration training, averaging
−0.669 points. At the large budget all four accu-
racy point estimates favor K8, but three individ-
ual source intervals cross zero. The exploratory
large-budget CORE difference, +2.246, becomes
+0.254 in the replication cohort, with mixed indi-
vidual signs (Table 21).
Small net differences can conceal offsetting out-
put changes.
Short-budget seed 1103 has 280
LAMBADA examples correct only for K8 and
277 correct only for K4+. Across six exploratory
pairs, no per-pair joint rectangle resolves an oppos-
ing word-loss/accuracy preference. Appendices E
and J retain all task families, margins, and finite-
window comparisons. The new selected-rate and
388M models add NLL evidence, not new CORE
or LAMBADA results.
7
Conclusion
The matched-time NLL advantage of shorter back-
propagation diminishes between the tested train-
ing budgets.
This direction persists across the
measured common learning rates, after separate
dev-based rate selection, and in a second Parcae
configuration. The better of the two windows at
the larger budget remains configuration-dependent.
Additional equal-token controls show that the win-
dow gap also decreases, but less than the benefit of
extended training. These controls characterize the
change without identifying a causal optimization
mechanism. The intermediate-window compari-
son and measured training memory give further
reasons to evaluate candidate recipes under their
intended constraints. Backward windows should
be compared at the intended training budget and
resource constraints, rather than selected once from
a small-budget ranking.

Limitations
Our experiments study two Parcae configurations
trained on predominantly English web text across
the reported training budgets. The comparisons
evaluate complete training recipes, with extended
runs using additional tokens and optimization
schedules defined for their full training horizons.
The reported timing and evaluation results describe
the measured A800 setting and selected bench-
marks, with computational and reproducibility de-
tails provided in Appendices A.3 and A.4.
Ethical Considerations
The experiments reuse web-text and benchmark
resources and do not recruit human participants or
commission new human annotation. Training text
is English; the evaluation also includes multilingual
language-identification inputs. Web-derived data
may contain personal information, stereotypes, and
harmful content; deduplication does not remove
these risks. The study evaluates training allocation
and does not establish the safety or suitability of
any checkpoint for deployment. Repeated training
and evaluation incur computational and potential
environmental costs. Appendix A.3 reports the
recorded hardware-time budget and its omissions;
power and energy were not measured, so we do not
estimate carbon emissions. The supplementary evi-
dence contains numerical observations and hashes,
not raw web documents or benchmark text.

Batched backward window.
For a batch with
sampled depths Ti, let Tmax = maxi Ti. Active re-
currences are right-aligned: sequence i updates its
recurrent state only when the zero-based recurrence
index r ≥Tmax −Ti. Earlier dense Core calls still
execute, but their outputs are masked out. The re-
tained window begins at b = Tmax−min(k, Tmax).
If Ti < k, this boundary precedes that sequence’s
first active call, so all its active recurrences retain
gradients. The input representation e remains con-
nected to the Prelude through retained injections.
Algorithm 1: Batched implementation of a fixed
backward window
1. Compute e = Prelude(Embed(tokens)) and initial-
ize recurrent state x.
2. Set Tmax = maxi Ti and b = Tmax −min(k, Tmax);
set b = 0 for full backpropagation.
3. For r = 0, . . . , Tmax −1:
(a) If r = b > 0, set x = stopgrad(x).
(b) Record gradients if and only if r ≥b.
(c) Compute z = Core(Adapter(x, e)).
(d) Set xi = zi if r ≥Tmax −Ti; otherwise retain
xi.
4. Record gradients when computing the token loss
through projection, Coda, and the prediction head.
The notation K1/K2/K4/K6/K8 in the archived
ledger denotes fixed windows k = 1, 2, 4, 6, 8.
FULL denotes full backpropagation through every
sampled recurrence, rather than a fixed window of
16. Projection, normalization, and attention details
are omitted from Algorithm 1.

independent replications.

J.2
Offsetting correctness and loss
contributions
Short-budget seed 1103 has 280 examples cor-
rect only for K8 and 277 correct only for K4+:
557 discordant examples produce a net differ-
ence of three. Its exact McNemar p = 0.9325,
rather than evidence of a resolved advantage. The
four word-NLL contributions are +0.004589 (both
correct), +0.068533 (K8 only), −0.075481 (K4+
only), and −0.050293 (both wrong). The single-
correct groups largely cancel; jointly incorrect ex-
amples account for most of the net K4+ word-loss
gain.
Several other pairs show a negative both-wrong
contribution, but long-budget seed 1102 reverses
it (+0.052338, favoring K8). Tokenization strata
also differ: in short-budget 1103, multi-token tar-
gets contribute −0.054459 nats/word and single-
token targets +0.001808, with opposing accuracy
contributions. These identify where measured dif-
ferences arise, not a universal tokenization effect.
Teacher-forced token margins are reported in Ap-
pendix E; no calibration experiment identifies over-
confidence or a neural mechanism.
J.3
K4/K6/K8 at the same short-time budget
In seed 1102, K6 has higher CORE and LAM-
BADA than both alternatives but higher test NLL
than K4+. Its favorable task ordering does not recur
consistently (Table 3). In 1104, K4+ has lower test
loss and higher CORE and LAMBADA than K6,
but is slightly slower. All three common-three-seed
paired task-score intervals cross zero (Table 25).
Tiny timing differences prevent strict four-objective
dominance even when the quality point estimates
favor one recipe. The finite set therefore supplies
no consistent multi-metric winner.
