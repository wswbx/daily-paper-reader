ABSTRACT
Looped Transformers increase effective depth by repeatedly applying shared lay-
ers, offering a way to scale computation without increasing parameter count.
However, full-stack looping places context construction and iterative state re-
finement within the same recurrent computation, without explicitly distinguishing
their roles. We introduce the Asymmetric Looped Transformer (AsymLoop),
which separates single-pass context encoding from recurrent state refinement. A
single-pass causal encoder produces layer-specific Global key–value (KV) caches
that remain fixed across iterations, while a weight-tied recurrent decoder generates
Local KV from its evolving state. Joint attention over both sources under a single
softmax allows each update to integrate persistent contextual information with the
current intermediate state. For training, we apply a heavy-tail-guided layerwise
learning-rate method to the encoder and decoder. Experiments on FineWeb-Edu
across four model scales, from 60M to 1B, show consistently lower validation per-
plexity and higher average downstream accuracy than Full Loop under matched
effective depth and training token budgets. At 1B, AsymLoop improves aver-
age zero-shot accuracy across seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks from
49.58% to 50.85%. Further experiments extend these benefits to longer training
budgets, an OLMo-style backbone, and sparse mixture-of-experts models. These
results support asymmetric context encoding and state refinement as an effective
design for recurrent language models.

INTRODUCTION
Scaling language models typically involves increasing parameters, training data, and computation
together (Henighan et al., 2020). Looped Transformers offer another dimension for scaling compu-
tation: repeatedly applying a shared block increases effective depth without assigning independent
parameters to every executed layer (Dehghani et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2020). Recent work further
demonstrates that recurrent depth can improve reasoning performance through additional compu-
tation at test time (Geiping et al., 2025). This opportunity makes the organization of recurrence a
central design concern. Performance depends not only on how many iterations a model executes,
but also on which computations it repeats and how contextual information remains available across
iterations.
Recurrence can be organized through different layer topologies, depending on which layers are
repeated and which non-recurrent layers precede or follow them (Huang et al., 2026). A straight-
forward choice is to loop the entire Transformer stack, feeding the output of each pass back into the
same stack for the next (Zhu et al., 2025); we refer to this topology as Full Loop. However, analyses
of pretrained language models suggest that different depths support distinct computational roles,
including contextualizing input tokens, refining features, and forming predictions (Lad et al., 2025).
Repeating the entire stack places these roles together within the recurrent computation, without ex-

plicitly separating input processing from iterative refinement. Analyses of looped models likewise
observe multiple feedforward-like processing stages recurring within each iteration (Blayney et al.,
2026). These findings motivate organizing recurrence around a division of roles: distinguishing con-
text construction that can be performed once from state refinement to which additional computation
can be allocated.
Partial looping has been explored in recurrent language models, repeating only a selected subset
of layers while executing the remaining layers once (Geiping et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026).
Controlled comparisons with matched parameter counts, effective depth, and training budgets report
improvements over full-stack looping on several multi-step reasoning benchmarks (Huang et al.,
2026). Such designs make the distinction between recurrent and non-recurrent computation explicit,
but choosing the scope of recurrence alone does not determine how the two components should
work together. We argue that their different computational roles should guide a broader asymmetric
design: non-recurrent layers can specialize in context construction, while recurrent layers perform
iterative state updates, with information exchange connecting these two types of computation.
We introduce the Asymmetric Looped Transformer (AsymLoop), which connects a single-pass
causal encoder to a weight-tied recurrent decoder through joint attention to fixed Global KV and
evolving Local KV. A causal encoder runs once and projects its output into per-decoder-layer Global
key–value (KV) caches. These caches serve as contextual anchors that remain fixed across all iter-
ations of a forward pass. A weight-tied decoder generates Local KV from its current hidden states
at each iteration. The two sources offer complementary views: Global KV preserves access to the
context encoded by the encoder, while Local KV reflects the decoder’s current intermediate state.
Queries allocate attention jointly across both sources under a single softmax, enabling each update
to integrate global contextual information with the current local state. All components are trained
end to end.
For optimization, we adopt the heavy-tail-guided layerwise learning-rate method of He et al. (2026)
for the encoder and decoder. The same spectral allocation rule is applied to matrices in both compo-
nents. Encoder–decoder learning-rate differences arise from their measured spectral statistics. We
use Asymmetric Learning Rate Schedule (ALRS) as a shorthand for this training configuration and
examine the resulting allocation patterns and their empirical effects in our experiments.
Our main contributions are:
• An asymmetric recurrent architecture. We jointly design one-time context encoding and
weight-tied recurrent updates, connecting them through joint attention to fixed Global KV
and dynamic Local KV.
• Analysis of context access under recurrence. Through component ablations and compar-
isons across recurrent depths, we examine the roles of fixed Global KV and evolving Local
KV in recurrent state refinement.
• Performance gains across model sizes. Across four model sizes, AsymLoop achieves
lower validation perplexity and higher average zero-shot accuracy than Full Loop under
matched training budgets and effective depth. Perplexity improves by 0.98 and 0.30 at
135M and 350M, respectively, while the seven-task average accuracy at 1B rises from
49.58% to 50.85%.

RELATED WORK
Looped Transformers and Recurrent Depth. Weight-tied recurrence in Transformers traces back
to Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019) and ALBERT (Lan et al., 2020), which estab-
lished that parameter sharing across layers can achieve competitive linguistic representations. The-
oretical investigations have formalized looped Transformers as programmable computers (Gian-
nou et al., 2023) capable of learning complex in-context algorithms (Yang et al., 2024) and latent
thoughts (Saunshi et al., 2025). More recently, scaling up test-time compute through recurrent depth
has emerged as a promising alternative to chain-of-thought token generation (Geiping et al., 2025).
Studies such as Parcae (Prairie et al., 2026) and FLT (Fu et al., 2026) explore stability in looped mod-
els. Beyond full-stack recurrence, partial-loop architectures already separate recurrent computation
from non-recurrent input and output processing (Geiping et al., 2025). Our focus is the information

Global KV from a single-pass encoder, jointly accessed with evolving Local KV at every iteration.

language models: build a causal representation of the input once, then let a recurrent stack iteratively
refine its state while retaining access to that representation.

ASYMMETRIC ENCODER–DECODER DESIGN
As shown in Figure 1, AsymLoop consists of a non-recurrent causal encoder and a weight-tied
recurrent decoder. The encoder uses the same causal Transformer blocks as a standard decoder-only
language model, without changing their architecture. We split L physical layers into E encoder

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate AsymLoop on LLaMA-style architectures at the 60M, 135M, 350M, and 1B scales
following He et al. (2026). Section 4.1 defines the baselines, training budgets, and effective-depth
comparisons, with full configurations and hardware details in Appendix A. We then report pre-
training results across model sizes (Section 4.2), downstream performance (Section 4.3), the effect of

4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Pre-training. All model families in the main experiments use the LLaMA-style backbone config-
urations following He et al. (2026) and are trained from scratch on FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al.,
2024). To study the scaling behavior of AsymLoop, we evaluate the 60M, 135M, 350M, and 1B

provided in Appendix A.
Baselines and depth matching. We compare AsymLoop with two vanilla baselines: a standard
non-recurrent Transformer (Dense), and a homogeneous looped Transformer that repeats the en-
tire Transformer stack with weights shared across iterations (Full Loop). We compare recurrent
models at matched effective depth, defined as the total number of Transformer layers executed per
forward pass. The additional parameters and computation introduced by Global KV are detailed in

4.2
PRE-TRAINING UNDER THE CHINCHILLA SCALING LAW
To examine the scalability of AsymLoop, we jointly increase model size and training token budget
following the Chinchilla scaling law. Figure 2(a, b) shows that AsymLoop consistently achieves
lower validation loss than Dense and Full Loop across all four scales, from 60M to 1B. Its ad-
vantage over Full Loop is maintained at matched effective depth and training budgets within each
scale. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of AsymLoop across model sizes, with sustained
improvements at the billion-parameter scale. These gains also extend to an OLMo-style back-
bone (Groeneveld et al., 2024), where AsymLoop reduces validation perplexity by 14.8% relative to
the depth-matched Full Loop (Appendix G).
AsymLoop consistently improves both language-modeling loss and zero-shot downstream accuracy
over Full Loop across all evaluated scales. Notably, the downstream advantage grows monotonically
with model scale, from 0.13 percentage points at 60M to 1.27 points at 1B. We examine the task-
level results in Section 4.3.

DOWNSTREAM EVALUATION
We evaluate the pretrained models on seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks to assess their
downstream generalization:
PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), Social IQa (Sap et al., 2019), Hel-
laSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al.,
2018), ARC-Easy (Clark et al., 2018), and OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018). Evaluations cover
all four model sizes under both zero-shot and five-shot settings, using the standard prompts provided
by the LM Evaluation Harness (Gao et al., 2024). Table 1 summarizes the per-task and average ac-
curacies.
As shown in Table 1, AsymLoop consistently outperforms Dense and Full Loop in average accu-
racy across all four model sizes under both zero-shot and five-shot settings. The improvements
extend across multiple benchmarks, with particularly broad gains in five-shot evaluation: Asym-
Loop achieves the best performance on all seven tasks at 350M and six of the seven tasks at 1B.
At 350M, its average accuracy exceeds Full Loop by 2.15 percentage points. These results demon-
strate that the pre-training gains of AsymLoop translate into stronger downstream commonsense
reasoning across both evaluation settings. We further analyze the training dynamics of downstream
performance at the 1B scale in Appendix F.
Furthermore, we evaluate the mathematical reasoning performance of the 1B pretrained models
through supervised fine-tuning on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021). AsymLoop achieves an accuracy
of 11.75%, a 1.52 percentage point improvement over Full Loop. These results suggest that its
downstream benefits also extend to supervised fine-tuning; detailed results and training settings are
provided in Appendix H.
4.3.1
DOWNSTREAM PERFORMANCE UNDER A 5× CHINCHILLA BUDGET
To investigate whether the benefits of AsymLoop persist under extended training, we increase the
training budget of the 350M models from 7B to 35B tokens, corresponding to five times the Chin-
chilla budget. We compare AsymLoop with Dense and Full Loop under the same token budget,
maintaining matched effective depth between the recurrent models. Table 2 presents their zero-shot
and five-shot performance on the seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks.
As shown in Table 2, AsymLoop maintains the highest average accuracy in both zero-shot and
five-shot evaluation under the extended training budget. In five-shot evaluation, it matches or sur-
passes both baselines on all seven tasks, improving average accuracy over Full Loop from 46.67%
to 47.41%. These results demonstrate that the downstream benefits of AsymLoop persist with a
fivefold increase in training tokens, supporting its effectiveness under extended pre-training.

5
CONCLUSION
We presented AsymLoop, an asymmetric looped Transformer that combines single-pass context en-
coding with recurrent state refinement through joint attention to Global and Local KV. We train this
architecture using the spectral layerwise learning-rate method of He et al. (2026) and evaluate its ef-
fect through optimization ablations. Across four model scales from 60M to 1B, AsymLoop achieves
lower validation perplexity and higher average downstream accuracy than the depth-matched Full
Loop baseline. Its language-modeling gains extend to an OLMo-style backbone and sparse MoE
feed-forward layers, while supervised fine-tuning on GSM8K also yields higher mathematical rea-
soning accuracy. Although AsymLoop requires more training FLOPs than the original Full Loop, it
achieves lower validation loss than a widened Full Loop baseline under matched training FLOPs.

Large-scale pre-training uses up to 32 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, each with 80 GB of memory.
We report validation cross-entropy (CE) and perplexity (PPL) to assess pre-training performance.
For downstream commonsense reasoning, we report zero-shot and five-shot accuracy using the stan-
dard prompts provided by the LM Evaluation Harness (Gao et al., 2024).

B.1
ALRS SCHEDULING DETAILS
Following the allocation and scheduling procedure of He et al. (2026), ALRS assigns matrix-wise
learning-rate multipliers in [1, 5]. Allocations are updated at the first optimizer step and every 100
steps thereafter during the first 20% of training, with a 50-step linear transition to each new learning-
rate target. After this initial phase, the multipliers remain fixed while the base learning rate continues
its cosine decay.
