Abstract 6

Hybrid neural differential equations (HNDEs) embed neural network compo-7

nents within mechanistic scaffolds, combining the structural interpretability 8

of domain-derived models with the approximation power of neural dynamics. 9

Despite their growing adoption in biology and engineering, neural augmenta-10 

tion can introduce observational degeneracies that compromise mechanistic 11 

identifiability and scientific interpretability. 12 

In this paper, we develop a theoretical framework for practical preserva-13 

tion of mechanistic identifiability in HNDEs. We formalize bounded neural 14 

correction classes and derive Gronwall-type trajectory and observational dis-15 

crepancy bounds linking neural perturbations to mechanistic parameter am-16 

biguity. We further establish sufficient conditions under which hybrid neural 17 

corrections preserve approximate mechanistic parameter recoverability up to 18 

explicitly quantifiable tolerances. 19 

Empirical likelihood profile analyses on benchmark systems confirm that neural augmentation systematically weakens—but does not eliminate—mechanistic identifiability, revealing a fundamental expressiveness–identifiability trade-off. These results provide theoretical foundations and actionable guidance for deploying HNDEs in scientific intelligent computing. 

Keywords: neural differential equations, mechanistic identifiability, 20 

dynamical systems 21

1. INTRODUCTION 22 

Neural dynamical systems—systems of differential equations whose right-23 

hand sides are parameterized by neural networks—have become fundamen-24 

tal tools in intelligent computing [1, 2]. The conceptual generalization to 25 

learned dynamics—where the neural vector field is fitted from data rather 26 

than prescribed—underlies modern NDEs [3]. This perspective has been ex-27 

tended to scientific computing, where the task is the discovery and simulation 28 

of complex dynamical phenomena from incomplete data [4, 5]. 29 

Scientific machine learning faces a distinctive challenge: models must 30 

be both predictively accurate and mechanistically interpretable [6, 7]. In 31 

biology and engineering, purely data-driven approaches lack the structural 32 

constraints needed for scientific inference, while purely mechanistic models 33 

become intractable when system interactions are only partially character-34 

ized [8, 9]. Hybrid neural differential equations (HNDEs) address this tension 35 

by embedding neural network components within a mechanistic differential 36 

equation models [4, 10, 11]. 37 

HNDEs have antecedents in the grey-box process models of the 1990s, 38 

where neural networks were used to correct incompletely specified mechanis-39 

tic descriptions [12, 13, 14, 15]. Modern formulations integrate the neural 40 

component directly into the ODE right-hand side rather than as an addi-41 

tive error term [4, 16, 17]. Applications span ecological parameter recov-42 

ery [18, 17], chemical bioprocess engineering [19, 10, 20, 21, 22, 23], and 43 

epidemiological compartment discovery [24, 25, 26, 27]. The term “universal 44 

differential equation” of [4] emphasizes the universality of the neural approx-45 

imator; we prefer HNDE following [12] to emphasize the hybrid mechanistic 46 

and data-driven character. 47 

Parameter identifiability is a classical concern in mechanistic modeling [28, 48 

29, 30]. Structural identifiability analysis determines whether parameters are 49 

theoretically recoverable from noise-free observations, while practical identifi-50 

ability methods—Fisher information matrices and likelihood profiles—assess 51

2.1. Identifiability Preservation Theory 126 

Parameter identifiability—the ability to uniquely recover model param-127 

eters from observable data—is a fundamental requirement for scientific in-128 

terpretability, mechanistic validation, and reliable prediction in dynamical 129 

systems [28, 29]. Without identifiability, multiple parameter configurations 130 

may generate indistinguishable trajectories, preventing causal interpretation 131 

of the underlying biological mechanisms. This challenge becomes particularly 132 

critical in HNDEs, where the flexibility of neural components may absorb or 133 

mask mechanistic effects, potentially compromising the interpretability of the 134 

mechanistic backbone. 135 

For additive HNDEs of the form (3), a central question is therefore 136 

whether the introduction of the neural correction term preserves the recover-137 

ability of the mechanistic parameters. The additive structure plays an essen-138 

tial role in this context, as it maintains an explicit decomposition between 139 

the known mechanistic dynamics and the learned residual component. This 140

3. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 256 

We evaluate on two benchmark biological systems that represent comple-257 

mentary sources of model uncertainty: a missing kinetic mechanism (glycol-258 

ysis) and a missing state measurement (three-species Lotka–Volterra). Both 259 

systems are simulated to generate synthetic datasets with additive multiplica-260 

tive Gaussian noise ( μ = 1 , σ = 0 .05 ). 261 

3.1. Model calibration 262 

Both NDE and HNDE models are calibrated using gradient-based meth-263 

ods, with gradients computed via automatic differentiation through a compat-264 

ible ODE solver [34, 35, 36]. We employ the ADAM optimizer [37], consistent 265 

with recent HNDE literature [10, 38]. 266 

We adopt the mini-batch loss formulation: short time windows are sam-267 

pled as initial conditions, integrated forward, and prediction errors accumu-268 

lated across segments before updating parameters. Mini-batch training is 269 

known to improve convergence for time-series neural dynamical models [39, 270 

40, 41] and is used throughout with mean absolute error (MAE). 271
