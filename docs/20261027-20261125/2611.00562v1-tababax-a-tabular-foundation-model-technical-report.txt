Abstract
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) enable in-context prediction on unseen tabular datasets without
task-specific training. Mechanistic work has shown that increasing the depth of the ICL Transformer
yields weak gains, leaving the main headroom for gains in the synthetic pretraining prior and
column–row encoder upstream of the ICL backbone. We introduce TabAbax, a TFM built on
TabICLv2 that focuses all modifications on these two upstream components. On the prior side,
we further improve the quality and complexity of the synthetic pretraining prior. On the encoder
side, a two-pass column–row stack strengthens cross-feature interactions before row compression,
using learned Fourier scalar encoding from TabFM and Causilo.
Evaluations on BeyondArena
and TabArena show strong regression-centric gains: TabAbax reaches 1,238 Elo on the 142-dataset
BeyondArena suite (10th of 41 evaluated methods), 1,676 Elo on TabArena’s overall 51-dataset suite
(11th of 99 methods, +115 over TabICLv2), and 2,024 Elo on TabArena’s regression benchmark
(7th of 99 methods, +336 over TabICLv2, +21 over TabFM). A prior-only ablation confirms the
attribution: the improved prior alone yields +228 Elo for regression, demonstrating that prior
quality is the dominant knob for improving regression performance.

Introduction
Structured data is a foundational modality for scientific analysis and operational decision-making
across a wide range of domains, including healthcare, finance, logistics, and manufacturing. Un-
like language or perceptual data, tabular data organizes heterogeneous variables under explicit
schemas, preserving numerical scales, missingness patterns, and categorical structure that are es-
sential for reliable prediction.
For decades, prediction on tabular data has been dominated by
dataset-specific pipelines, particularly gradient-boosted trees (Chen and Guestrin, 2016; Ke et al.,
2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018; Grinsztajn et al., 2022), which remain highly competitive across
diverse tabular problems; deep alternatives have only recently reached parity (Ye et al., 2025).
Tabular foundation models provide a different route: a single pretrained model learns across a
large distribution of synthetic or real tabular tasks and directly performs prediction on unseen
datasets through in-context learning, using labeled examples from the target dataset as context
rather than retraining model parameters.
Since TabPFN showed that a transformer (Vaswani
et al., 2017) pretrained on synthetic tabular tasks performs strong prediction in a single forward
pass (Hollmann et al., 2023; M¨uller et al., 2022; Garg et al., 2022), subsequent work has scaled the
paradigm through richer priors and more expressive encoders (Hollmann et al., 2025; Grinsztajn
et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025, 2026; TabFM Team, 2026; Nums AI, 2026), real-data pretraining (Ma
et al., 2025; Hosseinzadeh et al., 2026; Adams et al., 2024), and language-model-based routes that
serialize tables as text or scale transfer learning to open-table corpora (Hegselmann et al., 2023;
Gardner et al., 2024); Section 2 reviews these lines in detail.
Recent progress in tabular foundation models has come increasingly from jointly scaling training
data, model capacity, and inference strategies. We take a complementary direction: rather than
scaling, we improve what feeds the ICL stack. A mechanistic study of tabular foundation models
(Biloˇs et al., 2026) supports this choice: architecturally distinct models converge in accuracy and
implement simple similarity-based readouts, and many ICL layers carry little load—the remaining
headroom lies upstream, in the synthetic prior and the column–row encoder, rather than in the ICL
2


transformer itself. The synthetic SCM priors used for pretraining leave clear room for improvement,
and our prior-side changes concentrate on data quality and complexity, acting across the entire
generation pipeline: we enforce strict one-hot width matching so that the emitted categories agree
exactly with the declared cardinalities; we generate class frequencies through multiple distributions,
e.g., a Dirichlet prior π ∼Dirichlet(α); we draw converter functions and their hyper-parameters
from richer families; and, for regression, we disable the post-hoc monotone warping of generated
targets, preserving the native smoothness of the causal generating mechanism (Section 3.3).
We introduce TabAbax, built on TabICLv2: our additions are deliberately small, and all of them
act upstream of the ICL stack. On the encoder side, we introduce a two-pass column–row stack
that deepens cross-feature interaction ahead of row compression, adopting the learned Fourier
scalar encoding established by TabFM and Causilo (Section 3.1). Across the two benchmark suites
(Table 1), TabAbax builds on the TabICLv2 baseline with consistent gains. Its strongest and most
consistent results are observed on regression: TabAbax achieves a 10.1 average per-dataset rank
across the benchmark’s full 13-dataset regression suite, outperforming TabFM (10.8; TabFM Team,
2026), Mitra-v2 (11.2), EXAONE-Tabular (14.5; Eo et al., 2026), and TabPFN-3 (18.3; Grinsztajn
et al., 2026).
Beyond regression, TabAbax remains competitive across mixed-task evaluations,
ranking 11th overall (Section 4).

Related Work
Synthetic-prior tabular foundation models. Prior-fitted networks established that a trans-
former pretrained on synthetic datasets approximates Bayesian in-context prediction in a single
forward pass (M¨uller et al., 2022), with in-context learning theory following for simple function
classes (Garg et al., 2022). TabPFN brought the paradigm to tabular data, pretraining on syn-
thetic datasets sampled from hand-designed structural causal models for small classification tables
(Hollmann et al., 2023); TabPFN v2 broadened the prior family and extended coverage to regression
at scale with a cell-based alternating row/column attention (Hollmann et al., 2025); and TabICL
adopted an SCM-based prior pipeline together with the column-then-row design and row com-
pression for linear-time ICL (Qu et al., 2025, 2026). TabFM combines alternating attention with
row compression and ICL (TabFM Team, 2026), and Causilo inserts an additional row-attention
module before compression (Nums AI, 2026). TabAbax differs from all of these by targeting the
pretraining corpus rather than the attention pattern: we modify the prior at two specific points,
and our two-pass encoding stays within the TabICLv2 framework so that row compression and
TabICLv2’s efficient inference machinery are preserved.
Evolution of encoder designs. The encoder side has evolved through several regimes. TabPFN
v1 lets individual cells interact across both rows and columns with per-cell attention—expressive,
but quadratic in the number of cells and tightly bounded in context length—and TabPFN v2 ex-
tends and scales the same cell-based row–column interaction at substantial compute cost (Hollmann
et al., 2023, 2025). TabDPT takes the opposite, efficiency-first route: each entire row is compressed
into a single embedding, and attention runs over row tokens only (Ma et al., 2025).
TabICL
introduced the now-common middle ground—three column-attention layers followed by three row-
attention layers, then row compression into compact embeddings on which the ICL transformer
operates—trading a bounded amount of pre-compression interaction for linear-time inference (Qu
et al., 2025, 2026). Two lines extend this pattern by iterating the column-then-row pass twice.
TabFM (TabFM Team, 2026) keeps the full feature grid throughout two untied column/row stages
and pools rows only at the end, at roughly double TabICL’s parameters; Causilo (Nums AI, 2026)
3


does so at TabICL’s own budget, with a Set-Transformer-style aggregation in place of CLS pooling.
Causilo matches TabFM on the leaderboards, suggesting that added parameters pay off better in
the pre-compression encoder than inside the ICL stack. On the capacity side, Xiaomi-TabLDM
extends a TabICL-like backbone’s ICL attention to 24 layers, the last eight mixture-of-experts, at
roughly double the parameter count (Xiaomi, 2026), and TabPFN-3 extends its ICL transformer
to 24 layers at a similarly doubled budget (Grinsztajn et al., 2026). Kumo goes further still, iter-
ating column–row interaction repeatedly after compression. TabAbax follows the same recipe at
TabICLv2’s own parameter budget—an additional column-then-row stage before row compression,
with a learned Fourier scalar embedding at the front—and keeps the post-compression ICL stack
unchanged.
Real-data pretraining and prior design. TabDPT shows that large-scale pretraining on real
tables can be competitive with synthetic priors (Ma et al., 2025), and Real-TabPFN improves
TabPFN through continued pretraining on real data (Adams et al., 2024). On the synthetic side,
prior work has focused on prior expressivity—richer SCM families, flexible structural priors, and
controllable difficulty (Hollmann et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2025; Xiaomi, 2026)—while TabICLv2
introduces graph- and dataset-level predictability filtering and studies its effect on pretraining
convergence (Qu et al., 2026).
TabAbax instead targets the differential effect of prior quality
control across task types—which of these aspects affect regression but not classification.
Scalar encodings. Learned sine–cosine (Fourier) encodings of scalar values—multi-scale, bounded
feature maps at learned frequencies—were adapted for tabular foundation models by TabFM and
Causilo (sine–cosine encodings themselves go back to Transformer position embeddings) (TabFM
Team, 2026; Nums AI, 2026). The encoding design itself follows prior work and is not claimed as
novel; likewise, the two-tier predictability filters are TabICLv2’s own. Our new contributions are
the prior-side changes detailed in Section 3.3, together with the two-pass column–row stack.
3
Overall Framework
TabAbax takes a tabular dataset as input and predicts test targets by conditioning on labeled
training examples as in-context demonstrations, without task-specific training. Its design centers
on two components: a improved synthetic pretraining prior, and an extended architecture that
combines Fourier scalar encoding with two-pass column–row encoding.
3.1
Architecture
The architecture of TabAbax is illustrated in Figure 3. Following TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026),
TabAbax chains column-wise embedding, row-wise interaction, and dataset-wise in-context learn-
ing, preserving the efficiency of TabICL with a runtime complexity of O(n2 + nm2) for tables with
n rows and m columns.
The TabICLv2 framework (Qu et al., 2026). Given an input table X ∈Rn×m, the framework
proceeds as follows.
• Repeated feature embedding. Each feature is embedded into multiple groups via circular
shifts. For a table with m columns, the j-th group contains the columns at positions (j, j +
1, j + 3) mod m (0-based), encoded by a shared linear layer Lin : R3 →Rd:

and the shift pattern (0, 1, 3) ensures that no pair of columns appears together in more than
one group for tables with at least 7 columns. This breaks feature symmetries and mitigates
cross-feature confusion when features share similar distributions, at the cost of strict column-
permutation invariance—mitigated in practice by the random-shuffle ensembles of TabICLv2,
which TabAbax inherits.
• Target-aware embedding. Target information is injected early: for training samples, a
target embedding is added to every token, E2[i, j] = E1[i, j] + EmbedTAE(yi), allowing the
model to determine feature relevance before compression.
• Compression then ICL. E2 is processed in three stages: (1) column-wise embedding applies
a set transformer (Lee et al., 2019) TFcol with inducing-point attention to each feature group;
(2) row-wise interaction applies a transformer TFrow with learnable [CLS] tokens, whose
concatenated states collapse each row into a single vector; (3) dataset-wise ICL combines the
row embeddings with target embeddings in a transformer TFicl, where test samples attend
to training samples to predict ˆy. Attention in TFicl and in the inducing-point part of TFcol
uses the query-aware scalable softmax (QASSMax) of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026), which
rescales queries as a function of log n to mitigate attention fading and improve long-context
generalization.
• Quantile regression. Classification and regression models are pretrained separately. The
regressor predicts the 999 quantiles at levels αi = i/1000, i = 1, . . . , 999, trained with a pinball
loss summed across quantiles; point predictions average the quantiles, and the sorted quantiles
reconstruct a full predictive distribution from which prediction intervals and uncertainty
estimates follow.
TabAbax departs from this framework in two design components:
• Fourier scalar encoding. Each observed scalar x is first mapped to sine and cosine features
at K = 16 learned frequencies, ϕ(x) = [sin(2πf1x), cos(2πf1x), . . . , sin(2πfKx), cos(2πfKx)],
whose concatenation replaces the raw scalar inside the grouped linear projection of repeated
feature embedding, following TabFM and Causilo (TabFM Team, 2026; Nums AI, 2026). The
Fourier basis maps every value to a fixed-size, multi-scale, bounded representation, letting the
model express high-frequency functions of its inputs from the very first layer; missing values
use a separate learned embedding instead.
• Two-pass column–row encoding. The column-then-row attention pair (TFcol →TFrow)
is applied twice before row compression and the ICL transformer: the first pass builds initial
feature interactions and row mixtures, and the second pass re-evaluates cross-feature depen-
dencies on the updated representations. Both passes run at the same hidden width with inde-
pendent (untied) weights—separate parameter blocks rather than a shared stack—following
TabFM, which alternates two untied column/row stages and pools rows into fixed-width vec-
tors only after both (TabFM Team, 2026); the folding happens late, so TabICLv2’s efficient
inference machinery is preserved.
The ICL transformer TFicl keeps TabICLv2’s 12 layers (Qu et al., 2026); since row compression fixes
the per-row representation length, ICL depth contributes context-processing steps at no asymptotic
context cost.

3.2
Pretraining
We pretrain classification and regression models separately, following the TabICLv2 pretraining
protocol (Qu et al., 2026). The classifier is trained with a cross-entropy loss and the regressor with
the 999-quantile pinball loss of Section 3.1. TabICLv2 pretrains in three stages that progressively
expand dataset size, with up to 100 features and a batch size of 64 throughout: Stage 1: 500K
6


steps on datasets with 1,024 samples (30–90% for training), maximum learning rate 8e-4; Stage 2:
40K steps on datasets with 400–10,240 samples (log-uniform, ∼80% for training), 1e-4; Stage 3:
10K steps on datasets with 400–60K samples, 2e-5. Optimization uses the Muon optimizer (Jordan
et al., 2024) (replacing AdamW) with weight decay 0.01, gradient clipping at 10 for Stages 1–2,
and a cosine learning-rate schedule. We train TabAbax with the improved prior of Section 3.3: the
three stages are trained on 8×H20 GPUs—around 8–12 days for Stage 1 and 3 days each for Stages
2 and 3, roughly 2,700–3,500 GPU-hours in total.

1. Task configuration. Each synthetic task begins with a draw of its global configuration (Qu
et al., 2026): the number of rows, the number of features, and their ratio; the mix of numerical
and categorical columns together with their cardinalities; the target type—binary, multiclass,
or regression, with the number of classes drawn for classification—and the context–query
partition, which fixes how many labeled examples serve as in-context demonstrations and
how many rows are queried.
2. Graph sampling and propagation. A directed acyclic graph is drawn from TabICLv2’s
random Cauchy mechanism (Qu et al., 2026); latent vectors propagate in topological order,
and each node evaluates a random function of its parents from eight classes spanning smooth
and non-smooth inductive biases. In TabAbax, the function family f and its hyper-parameters
θ are drawn per node from richer families, f ∼p(f), θ ∼p(θ | f), so the columns of X realize
diverse function shapes instead of correlated duplicates.
3. Tabular realization.
Graph nodes are designated as observed features or the target; a
random subset of each node’s dimensions is revealed, the rest remaining latent. Continuous
dimensions feed numerical features and regression targets, categorical ones arise through
discretization or softmax sampling. In TabAbax, the declared cardinality Cj, the emitted
categories, and the one-hot width of feature j agree exactly—no empty or extra slots—so
the embedding layer learns stable category semantics.
Class frequencies are drawn from
multiple distributions, most prominently π ∼Dirichlet(α), reproducing the class imbalance
of real data. TabICLv2’s converters may additionally apply a post-hoc monotone warping to
7


targets; TabAbax disables this step, preserving the native output of the causal mechanism,
which itself still samples non-smooth node functions such as tree ensembles. Datasets failing
the final signal check are rejected and resampled before entering the corpus.
Together, these changes raise the quality and diversity of the synthetic prior: sampled tasks span
diverse nonlinear feature interactions, carry stable categorical representations, and exhibit the
skewed class frequencies of real data.

Evaluation
We evaluate TabAbax on two public tabular learning benchmarks, BeyondArena (Purucker et al.,
2026) and TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), which together cover a broad range of real-world
classification and regression tasks; Table 1 summarizes their composition.
BeyondArena (Purucker et al., 2026) probes generalization beyond standard IID splits with 142
datasets (98 classification, 44 regression), stratified along four axes: task type (IID / Grouped /
Temporal), dataset scale (Tiny / Small / Medium / Large), dimensionality (Low / High), and
feature type (Text / High-Cardinality).
Its size distribution—80 datasets below 10K rows, 35
between 10K and 100K, and 27 above 100K—stresses regimes that IID-only suites underrepresent.
TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025) is a living benchmark that compares tabular foundation mod-
els and tuned classical ML pipelines under a unified evaluation protocol.
Its suite contains 51
datasets—38 classification and 13 regression (the benchmark’s full regression suite)—with sizes
spanning under 10K rows (29 datasets), 10K–100K (20), and over 100K (2). Methods are ranked
by an Elo rating anchored at default Random Forest = 1000; the leaderboard pools 99 evaluated
entries, including the TabPFN family, TabICLv2, TabFM, Causilo, Kumo, LimiX-2 (Zhang et al.,
2025; Wang et al., 2026), TabM (Gorishniy et al., 2025), and tuned/ensembled gradient-boosted
trees, and complements Elo with aggregate per-dataset views (average and harmonic rank, oracle
improvability, and win rate).

5
Limitations
Limitations of the current evaluation. The predictability filters reduce the effective corpus
size, and we have not yet measured whether the diversity lost by filtering trades off against the
quality gained at the extremes of the distribution. Beyond corpus size, filtering risks a coverage gap
for weak-signal, high-noise tasks—exactly the regime of many real-world datasets, which resemble
the problems the filter removes—so generalization on weak-signal real data may be reduced.
Context and memory bounds. The ICL context holds a bounded number of rows; very large
datasets and long-range dependencies remain limited by context size and memory. And like all
in-context learners, predictions depend on the quality of the context rows: corrupted, mislabeled,
or distribution-shifted demonstrations can silently degrade predictions.
6
Conclusion
TabAbax extends TabICLv2 along two upstream axes. On the prior side, generation-time changes
concentrate on data quality and complexity: strict one-hot width matching, class frequencies drawn
from multiple distributions, richer converter function families, and the removal of the post-hoc
monotone warping that artificially introduces non-smoothness into regression targets. On the en-
coder side, Fourier scalar encoding and a two-pass column–row stack adapt the pre-compression
recipe of TabFM and Causilo to TabICLv2’s own parameter budget. The prior-side changes yield
large regression gains without increasing model parameter count or altering the inference archi-
tecture, the architectural changes benefit both task types, and the combination carries both gains
15


at matched asymptotic inference cost. We believe prior quality control is a generally applicable
recipe for SCM-pretrained tabular foundation models. More broadly, our results suggest that for
such models, regression performance can be substantially improved through prior quality control,
without scaling up model size.
7
Future Work
Deeper post-compression interaction.
The encoder evolution from TabICL’s single pre-
compression pass, to TabFM’s two untied passes, to Kumo’s repeated post-compression column–row
interaction suggests that interaction after compression is the next promising axis; we plan to move
in this direction, adding deeper pre- and post-compression interaction stages within the linear-time
inference budget.
Width and capacity scaling. The changes above are all established at TabICLv2’s original
width and parameter budget. We plan to scale the model—enlarging the hidden dimension of the
column–row encoder and ICL backbone together with the parameter count—and study whether the
gains from the prior changes and deeper pre-compression interaction persist, amplify, or saturate
at larger capacity.
