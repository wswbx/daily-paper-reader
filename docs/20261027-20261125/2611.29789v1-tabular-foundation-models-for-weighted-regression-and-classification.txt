Abstract
Weighted regression and classification have applications ranging from missing-
data analysis and causal inference to insurance pricing and particle physics. Exist-
ing weighted methods must be fitted and tuned on each dataset, which is costly for
flexible nonlinear learners. Tabular foundation models avoid this cost. A Trans-
former is pretrained once on many simulated datasets and then predicts on a new
dataset by in-context learning, using its observations as context without further fit-
ting. Recent models such as TabPFN-3.5 surpass tuned conventional methods, but
they cannot use sample weights. We develop a general pipeline that adapts such
models to weighted prediction through weighted attention, a normalized weighted
loss, and pretraining on synthetic weighted prediction tasks from missing-data prob-
lems. Applied to TabPFN-3.5, it yields WeightedPFN, which performs strongly on
real datasets, both in missing-data problems and in out-of-distribution causal tasks,
while running about as fast as linear baselines. We also establish a statistical theory
showing that a suitably pretrained weighted Transformer attains the nonparametric
minimax rate for weighted regression on new datasets without further fitting.
Keywords: causal inference, in-context learning, minimax rate, missing data, tabu-
lar foundation model

Introduction
Weighted regression and classification are fundamental tools that support a wide range
of applications. For example, problems in survey sampling, missing-data analysis, dis-
tribution shift, causal inference, learning from unreliable observations, insurance claim
modeling, and particle-physics simulation can be addressed by weighted regression and
classification (Pfeffermann, 1993; Robins and Rotnitzky, 1995; Shimodaira, 2000; Tian
et al., 2014; Zhao et al., 2012; Ren et al., 2018; McCullagh and Nelder, 1989; CMS Col-
laboration, 2025). We illustrate the applications of weighted regression and classification
through two examples: missing-data prediction and treatment selection.
Example 1 (Missing-data prediction). When covariates or responses are missing, the
complete cases may differ systematically from the original population of interest before
missingness. Under appropriate assumptions, weighting each complete case by the in-
verse of its observation probability can correct this selection bias. Predicting continuous
or categorical responses then becomes a weighted regression or classification problem,
respectively (Rubin, 1976; Robins and Rotnitzky, 1995).
Example 2 (Treatment selection). Treatment selection aims to recommend the treat-
ment that yields the highest expected outcome for an individual. Under standard causal
assumptions, this can be formulated either as weighted regression, which estimates the
conditional average treatment effect (CATE) and assigns treatment according to its sign,
or as weighted classification, which learns a treatment rule directly. The weights are
constructed from the propensity score and outcome information (Tian et al., 2014; Zhao
et al., 2012).
Formally, weighted regression and classification aim to learn a predictor f that mini-
mizes the weighted population risk E{Wℓ(f(X), Y )}, where X denotes covariates, Y the
response, W ≥0 a sample weight, and ℓthe squared-error loss for regression and the
0–1 loss for classification. Conventional approaches include weighted generalized linear
2


models (McCullagh and Nelder, 1989), weighted support vector regression and classifi-
cation (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995; Smola and Schölkopf, 2004), weighted tree ensembles
(Breiman, 2001; Ke et al., 2017), and weighted neural networks (Rumelhart et al., 1986).
These methods trade flexibility against computational cost: weighted generalized linear
models are fast to fit but may miss complex nonlinear relationships, whereas more flexi-
ble nonlinear methods require substantial time for training and hyperparameter tuning.
Moreover, each method must be fitted and tuned separately for every new dataset, so
this cost is incurred repeatedly. This motivates a reusable weighted learner that transfers
knowledge across tasks, combining flexible weighted prediction with fast adaptation to
new datasets without task-specific fitting or tuning.
Transformer-based in-context learning (Vaswani et al., 2017; Hollmann et al., 2023) of-
fers a promising way to build such a learner. Pretrained tabular foundation models based
on in-context learning can combine strong predictive performance with low computation
time on unweighted prediction, using observed examples as context to predict responses
for new observations without task-specific parameter updates or hyperparameter tuning.
Recent models such as TabPFN-3.5 surpass extensively tuned conventional methods in
aggregate performance on TabArena, a benchmark spanning diverse regression and clas-
sification datasets, while offering a favorable accuracy–runtime trade-off (Erickson et al.,
2025; Jäger et al., 2026).
However, existing tabular foundation models are designed
for unweighted prediction and do not directly support sample weights. Adapting these
models to weighted prediction requires a new pipeline for generating synthetic weighted
regression and classification tasks, incorporating sample weights into the Transformer
architecture, and training with weighted losses.
In this paper, we adapt tabular foundation models to weighted regression and classi-
fication. Our contributions are threefold.
First, we develop a reusable adaptation pipeline for weighted regression and classifi-
cation. The pipeline has three components: synthetic weighted tasks for pretraining, a
weighted attention mechanism that allows the Transformer to use sample weights, and a
3


normalized weighted loss. We propose to generate diverse pretraining tasks exclusively
from synthetic missing-data prediction (Example 1). These tasks are designed to teach
the models how to use sample weights, so that they can transfer this capability to other
weighted prediction problems without requiring each application to be included in pre-
training. The pipeline is designed for reuse across attention-based tabular foundation
models, so that advances in the underlying models can be incorporated into weighted
learning.
Second, we apply this pipeline to TabPFN-3.5 (Jäger et al., 2026), yielding Weight-
edPFN, to our knowledge the first tabular foundation model specifically pretrained for
weighted regression and classification1. We fine-tune the released parameters of TabPFN-
3.5 for approximately half an hour on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU and obtain our Weight-
edPFN. Once pretrained, WeightedPFN makes weighted predictions on new datasets with-
out task-specific fine-tuning or hyperparameter optimization. Experiments on 110 real
regression datasets and 156 real classification datasets demonstrate strong performance
in missing-data prediction (Example 1). WeightedPFN also generalizes well to out-of-
distribution causal tasks (Example 2) built from 78 real randomized-experiment datasets.
Across both task families, its runtimes are comparable to those of linear models and sub-
stantially below those of tuned nonlinear methods. The fine-tuned WeightedPFN models
are available at https://huggingface.co/xiajunwen/WeightedPFN, and the inference
code is available at https://github.com/xiajunwen1007/WeightedPFN.
Third, we establish a statistical theory of weighted in-context regression. Generaliz-
ing studies of Transformers as learning algorithms (Garg et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2023;
Kim et al., 2024; Ching et al., 2026), we analyze a weighted Transformer that more
closely reflects the architectures used in tabular foundation models than those in pre-

activations (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016). Under smoothness, boundedness, and task-

1.1
Related work
Our work is most closely related to tabular foundation models for unweighted regres-
sion and classification. TabPFN pioneered tabular foundation models: a Transformer is
pretrained on synthetic datasets and then makes predictions on real datasets in context
(Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025). Subsequent work improves predictive performance, scala-
bility, and computational eﬀiciency. TabICL combines more diverse synthetic pretraining
tasks, scalable attention, and improved optimization to achieve strong regression and clas-
sification performance (Qu et al., 2026). LimiX uses context-conditional masked modeling
to learn joint tabular structure, supporting regression, classification, and missing-value
imputation within a unified framework (Zhang et al., 2026). TabSwift uses a lightweight
row-wise attention backbone to provide eﬀicient regression and classification while re-
taining competitive predictive performance (Liu and Ye, 2026). The recent TabPFN-3.5
model achieves strong accuracy on the TabArena benchmark, outperforming existing
tuned baselines across a broad range of regression and classification tasks (Erickson et al.,
2025; Jäger et al., 2026).
Alongside these advances in tabular prediction, pretrained in-context models have
been adapted to causal inference (Robertson et al., 2025), time-series forecasting (Hoo
et al., 2025), survival analysis (Seletkov et al., 2026), graph learning (Eremeev et al.,
2026), and reinforcement learning (Schiff et al., 2026). These extensions broaden the use
of pretrained models to a wider range of statistical problems. However, none of these
models can handle weighted regression or classification.

Methodology
Consider a task with observations (X, Y, W) drawn from a distribution P, where X ∈
X ⊆Rd is a vector of d covariates, Y is the response, taking values in R for regression or
[K] = {1, . . . , K} for classification, and W ≥0 denotes a sample weight.
In conventional unweighted learning, the objective is to minimize the population risk
EP{ℓ(f(X), Y )} over a class of prediction functions f, where ℓdenotes a loss function.
The resulting optimal prediction depends on the choice of loss. For regression, the squared-
error loss ℓ(a, b) = (a−b)2 for a, b ∈R yields the conditional mean EP(Y | X = x) as the
population risk minimizer. For multiclass classification, the 0–1 loss ℓ(a, b) = 1(a̸ = b)
for a, b ∈[K] yields the optimal classification rule arg maxk PP(Y = k | X = x).
In many applications, however, observations contribute unequally to the learning
objective. We therefore consider the weighted population risk EP{Wℓ(f(X), Y )}. Its
minimizer over f generally differs from its unweighted counterpart. Specifically, under
squared-error loss, the optimal prediction is the weighted conditional mean mP(x) =
EP(WY | X = x)/EP(W | X = x). For multiclass classification, minimizing the weighted
0–1 loss yields the optimal classification rule arg maxk p∗
k(x), where p∗
k(x) = EP[W1(Y =
k) | X = x]/EP[W | X = x].

These formulas show why the weights matter: changing them can change the optimal
prediction. A tabular foundation model that ignores the weights estimates the unweighted
target, such as EP(Y | X = x), rather than mP(x).
We therefore build a weighted
tabular foundation model that takes the weighted observations as context and estimates
the weighted conditional mean or classification rule through in-context learning. A single
model is pretrained across many synthetic datasets and then adapts to each new real
dataset through its weighted context, without further fitting.
We pretrain the model exclusively on synthetic missing-data tasks (Example 1), which
teach it how to use sample weights for regression and classification, and reserve causal
tasks (Example 2) for out-of-distribution evaluation of whether this ability transfers to

2.1.2
Causal tasks for out-of-distribution evaluation
Beyond missing-data analysis, weighted prediction has many other applications, as dis-
cussed in Section 1.
Among these, we use the causal tasks in Example 2 for out-of-
distribution evaluation.
Weighted regression.
We begin with CATE estimation through weighted regression.
Let A ∈A denote the assigned treatment, where A is a finite set of available treat-
ments. For each a ∈A, let R(a) denote the potential outcome under treatment a, and let
R = R(A) be the observed outcome. We assume that larger outcomes are preferred. De-
fine the conditional mean potential outcome µa(x) = E{R(a) | X = x} and the propensity
score πa(x) = P(A = a | X = x). Under consistency, conditional exchangeability, and
positivity, µa(x) = E(R | A = a, X = x) (Hernán and Robins, 2020). These conditions
hold in randomized experiments, where the propensity score is also known. For binary
treatments, take A = {−1, 1}. The CATE is τ(x) = µ1(x) −µ−1(x), whose sign deter-
mines which treatment yields the larger conditional mean outcome. The CATE can be
estimated through weighted regression without separately estimating the two conditional
mean potential outcomes (Tian et al., 2014). Namely, consider the weighted samples

We study the class F of single-head Transformers fθ(Dn, X) with L blocks. Aﬀine em-
beddings first map each context pair (Xi, Yi) and the query covariates X to dk-dimensional
vectors, called the initial token representations. Within each block, linear projections of
the token representations produce the query, key, and value vectors introduced in Sec-
tion 2.2, which we also take to have dimension dk. The block then applies the weighted
attention mechanism in Section 2.2, followed by an FFN with DFFN hidden layers of width
at most WFFN and GELU activations (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2016). The prediction is
clipped to [−BY , BY ]. Section S7 of the supplementary material gives the precise archi-
tecture we analyze theoretically. Its core components, including the weighted attention
and the GELU activation, are the same as in the WeightedPFN used in our experiments.
To keep the analysis tractable, as in previous theoretical studies of in-context learning
(Garg et al., 2022; Ching et al., 2026), we omit some components, including multiple
heads and layer normalization.
A task law P is a distribution of (X, Y, W) satisfying the bounds in (3.1). Let P be a

Theorem 3.1 shows that a pretrained Transformer can learn a new weighted regression

The first two terms describe what happens within a single task.
The first term,

estimator fitted directly to the task. The second term, e−L/Cs, is the computation error.
Our Transformer-based method does not fit the new task by updating its parameters.

The conditions on the Transformer architecture are mild. The token dimension dk
and the FFN width WFFN do not grow with n and depend only on d and s, and one
hidden layer per FFN, DFFN = 1, suﬀices. The number of attention blocks L grows only
logarithmically in n. The condition on the number of queries per task during pretraining,
m, is also mild. Since 2s/(2s+d) < 1, it requires fewer queries than context samples n. For
example, splitting each pretraining task into context and queries in a fixed proportion,
as in our implementation, satisfies it for all suﬀiciently large n. The condition on the
number of pretraining tasks is the most demanding: M must grow polynomially in n,
roughly n4+4s/(2s+d) up to logarithmic factors. Because pretraining tasks are generated
synthetically, this condition can be met by generating more tasks. The condition on the
optimization error Eopt is determined by the optimization algorithm.
4
Experiments
In this section, we first describe the implementation details for pretraining our weighted
regression and classification models on synthetic missing-data tasks. We then introduce
the competing methods and compare their performance with our models on two groups
of tasks based on real datasets. The first consists of missing-data tasks, while the second
consists of causal tasks used to assess out-of-distribution task generalization.
4.1
Implementation
We generate synthetic tasks with 128 to 1,024 observations and 4 to 16 covariates before
imposing missingness. The task generator samples the X+Y, Y-only, X-only, and MCAR
mechanisms of Section 2.1.1 with probabilities 0.4, 0.4, 0.1, and 0.1, respectively. Weight
construction uses oracle, perturbed, or TabPFN-estimated observation probabilities, as
detailed in Section S1.3 of the supplementary material. Each task supplies 70% of its
weighted samples as context and the remainder as queries. We initialize from the released
parameters of TabPFN-3.5 and use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with a learning
19


rate of 10−5, weight decay of 0.01, gradient-norm clipping at 1, and a batch of 16 tasks
per update. We use the checkpoint after 800 updates. Thus, WeightedPFN is fine-tuned
on 12,800 synthetic datasets. Fine-tuning takes about 0.5 hours on a single NVIDIA
H100 GPU for both weighted regression and weighted classification.
4.2
Comparison methods
Competing methods for weighted regression. We compare our WeightedPFN regressor with
the unweighted TabPFN-3.5 regressor and five baselines: weighted ridge linear regression
(Ridge), weighted support vector regression with a radial basis function kernel (SVR;
Smola and Schölkopf, 2004), weighted random forests (RF; Breiman, 2001), weighted
LightGBM (LightGBM; Ke et al., 2017), and a weighted multilayer perceptron (MLP;
Rumelhart et al., 1986). Each of the five baselines uses Wi as a sample weight in its
training loss. Hyperparameters are chosen by five-fold cross-validation within the context,
minimizing weighted held-out prediction error.
The tuned hyperparameters for these
methods are listed in Section S6.1 of the supplementary material.
Competing methods for weighted classification. We compare our WeightedPFN clas-
sifier with the unweighted TabPFN-3.5 classifier and five baselines: weighted logistic re-
gression (Logistic), a weighted support vector machine with a radial basis function kernel
(SVM; Cortes and Vapnik, 1995), weighted random forests (RF), weighted LightGBM
(LightGBM), and a weighted multilayer perceptron (MLP). Each of the five baselines
uses Wi as a sample weight in its training loss. Hyperparameters are chosen by five-
fold cross-validation within the context, maximizing weighted held-out accuracy. The
tuned hyperparameters for these methods are listed in Section S6.2 of the supplementary
material.
For both regression and classification, TabPFN-3.5 receives the same context covari-
ates and responses as WeightedPFN but does not use the sample weights. Both in-context
models make predictions without updating their parameters on the evaluation tasks.

4.4
Treatment selection
We use 78 publicly available datasets from randomized experiments to assess out-of-
distribution generalization of our WeightedPFN to causal tasks. Most of them are ran-
domized clinical trials from RCT_Bench (Shao et al., 2026). The datasets used in our
evaluation are listed in Section S3 of the supplementary material. Of the 78 datasets, 55
have two arms and 23 have three or more arms.
To evaluate weighted regression tasks, we form binary treatment pairs and apply
covariate-dependent sampling within each pair to induce covariate-dependent treatment
propensities. Without this step, balanced randomized experiments have constant propen-
sity scores of 0.5, so the CATE estimation task in Section 2.1.2 reduces to unweighted
regression and does not assess the model’s ability to use sample weights. We retain 148
treatment-pair tasks with at least 25 selected observations per arm. For each task, we
construct the four weighted regression samples described in Section 2.1.2, which combine
ordinary or residualized modified outcomes with IPW or overlap weighting. All these
constructions target the same CATE.
To evaluate weighted classification, we use the observed treatment assignments with-
out additional covariate-dependent sampling. The sample weights depend on outcome
information and can therefore vary even when treatment propensities are constant. We
construct weighted classification samples using DR and RWL for 163 binary treatment-
pair tasks. For OWL, we use all available arms jointly. Because multiple outcomes from
one dataset define separate tasks, this yields 84 tasks from the 78 datasets. All three
constructions target the optimal treatment rule. Further details on DR and RWL are
provided in Section S5 of the supplementary material.
For regression, our objective is to estimate the CATE. Since the true CATE is un-
known in real datasets, we cannot directly evaluate its estimation error. We therefore
construct a treatment rule from the sign of the estimated CATE and evaluate its policy
value. For classification, the model directly learns a treatment rule, which we evaluate
using the same criterion. In both cases, we estimate policy value on held-out evaluation

Discussion
In this paper, we propose a general pipeline that adapts attention-based tabular founda-
tion models to weighted regression and classification by combining synthetic missing-data
tasks, weighted attention, and a normalized weighted loss. Applied to TabPFN-3.5, it
yields WeightedPFN after approximately half an hour of fine-tuning on a single H100
GPU. Across real missing-data and out-of-distribution causal tasks, WeightedPFN com-
bines strong performance with runtimes close to those of linear baselines, without task-
specific fine-tuning. Because the pipeline is not specific to TabPFN-3.5, it can also be
applied to future attention-based tabular foundation models, carrying their advances over
to weighted statistical learning.
Our theory shows that, under regularity and task-coverage conditions, a suitably
pretrained weighted Transformer attains the nonparametric minimax rate for weighted
regression on new tasks without task-specific fitting. This result could be extended to
weighted classification and to Transformer architectures that more closely match practical
tabular foundation models.
A related direction is to establish theoretical conditions
under which pretraining on missing-data tasks supports generalization to other weighted
problems, such as those arising in causal inference. We leave these extensions to future
work.
