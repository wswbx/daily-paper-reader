Abstract
The development of tabular foundation models (TFMs) has accelerated in recent
years, outperforming traditional ML methods on many tabular benchmarks. A
key finding is that TFMs can be pretrained entirely on synthetic datasets, opening
opportunities to design data generators that encourage desirable model properties.
Prior work has mainly focused on crafting high-quality priors over generators to
improve overall pretraining performance. Our key insight is that parameterizing the
generator distribution enables an adversarial, distributional robustness perspective:
during training, we can adapt the generator to emphasize datasets that are particu-
larly challenging for the model, which we formalize by introducing an optimality
gap measure. Further, we develop a method to align the synthetic data genera-
tion with real-world datasets from a given domain, constraining the adversary to
generate realistic data. Together, these algorithms comprise the ROBUST AND
DOMAIN-ADAPTED TABULAR FOUNDATION MODELS (RAD-TFM) pipeline,
a model-agnostic adversarial training framework. Applied to the TabPFN V2
classifier, RAD-TFM improves performance across 6 diverse tabular benchmarks,
with up to a 10.8 percentage-point improvement in mean normalized AUC over the
strongest baseline, using fewer than 100k additional synthetic training datasets, less
than 0.1% of the original pretraining data. Applying the same pipeline to TabICL
V2 also improves performance across three major benchmarks, demonstrating the
generality of the framework. These results highlight a promising new direction for
targeted adversarial continued training of TFMs using synthetic data alone.

Introduction
Recent survey studies have challenged the deep learning (DL) community to improve upon traditional
methods for learning from tabular data, such as boosted trees methods, having found that DL
approaches still lagged behind in terms of performance [19]. In response, substantial progress has
been made to close this gap, resulting in a variety of strong deep learning approaches being proposed
over the past few years [35, 18, 20, 22, 7, 39, 42]. Among these new approaches, tabular foundation
models (TFMs) which rely on in-context learning (ICL) [22, 31, 42] have emerged as a promising
direction for classification and regression tasks with structured datasets. The benefits of this approach
are two-fold. In many cases, the performance of the TFMs improves upon strong traditional baselines
such as boosted trees [16, 14]. Additionally, this performance is achieved in a zero-shot framework,
enabling high-quality predictions on new datasets in milliseconds when GPU-accelerated. Further, as
we will explore in this work, since these TFMs are pretrained using synthetic data, there is significant
opportunity for improvement as the data generation process itself is expanded and improved.
Pretraining TFMs relies on generating a large amount of diverse synthetic datasets [22]. As we will
formally define in the following section, this generation process relies on constructing structural
causal models (SCMs) from which datasets can be sampled [28, 34]. The structure of these SCMs
is implicitly parameterized, giving significant control over the data generation process. All current

process to frame TFM training from an adversarial robustness perspective [24, 32]. Recent work has
explored a narrower version of this idea, focusing on adjusting the weights of a specific class of SCMs
in GAN-style training of TFMs [37]. In this work, we focus on a broad and highly flexible framework
for adversarially robust training of TFMs. Going one step further, we ground this adversarial training
process, constraining it to generating realistic data, yielding a robust and domain-adapted TFM.
Recent developments in TFMs, such as Real-TabPFN, have demonstrated the benefit of incorporating
real-world data into the training process of TFMs [17]. However, as we will show in this work,
finetuning on real datasets alone is often insufficient due to the lack of large-scale, high-quality tabular
datasets, especially for more specific domains such as genomics or physical sciences. In this work,
we will train TFMs on synthetic data, but we will constrain the data generation process to produce
datasets that are similar to real-world datasets from a given domain. Previous work that aims to align
a synthetic data generator with a tabular dataset typically take a GAN-based approach, focusing on
constructing a generator to closely mimic a single real dataset [9, 27, 38]. In this work, we will
expand beyond this one-to-one relationship, developing an algorithm that efficiently constructs a
distribution over SCM-based tabular dataset generators that, in aggregate, align with real-world data
across a large variety of meta-features capturing data schema, geometry, and learnability.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows: 1) We formalize distributionally-robust adversarial
training over the SCM parameter space, allowing the model to adapt to challenging regions (e.g.,
varying numbers of features, categorical feature ratios, nonlinearities). We introduce an optimality
gap objective and use it to target regions where the TFM underperforms relative to the best achievable
performance. 2) We formalize the notion of domain-adapted TFMs in our DRO framework and
introduce an efficient algorithm to construct a reference distribution over synthetic data generators
that closely matches the real-data distribution for a given domain. 3) We propose an efficient,
model-agnostic two-stage adversarial training algorithm for TFMs, which we call ROBUST AND
DOMAIN-ADAPTED TABULAR FOUNDATION MODELS (RAD-TFM). 4) We apply RAD-TFM to

evaluate it on a variety of tabular benchmarks, both general and application-specific.

Problem Definition
We begin by defining some notation, and then introduce our theoretical formulation for RAD-TFM.
Let Φ represent the space of hypothesis functions which define the relationship between features
x and outcome y. The outcome y can be discrete or continuous, but for this work, we focus on
classification tasks. Then a hypothesis ϕ ∈Φ is a specific function which we can treat as a data
generating mechanism. In the case of SCMs, Φ is the entire space of SCMs, and ϕ ∈Φ is a specific
instance of an SCM, from which a dataset D ∼p(ϕ) can be generated. In this work, we implement
SCMs as randomly initialized multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs), as introduced in [22]. Full details
of this process are provided in Appendix D. For example, we can sample the number of layers
l ∼p(l), the hidden size h ∼p(h), the activation function a ∼p(a), and the ratio of categorical
features rcat ∼p(rcat), among other dimensions. An SCM is generated by first sampling from
the joint distribution over these hyperparameters, each of which is parameterized. For example,
we might sample the ratio of categorical features rcat ∼TruncNorm(µrcat, a = 0, b = 1) from
the truncated normal distribution with some mean µrcat which parametrizes the distribution. Then,
assuming we have similar parameterizations for the distribution over each hyperparameter, we define
θ = (µl, µh, µrcat, . . . ) as the parameterization of the joint distribution over all the hyperparameters.
We define Θ as the space of the parameters, such that θ ∈Θ represents a specific parameterization.
The generators are then sampled from ϕ ∼p(Φ; θ).
We now define the original objective for the Prior-Fitted Network (PFN) learning task and provide our
adversarially robust formulation. Let gW be our predictive model parameterized by weight matrix
W. As a PFN, gW takes as input a sequence of labeled training samples and unlabeled test samples,
and predicts labels for the test samples. For a given data generator ϕ ∈Φ, the PFN loss is defined as
the cross entropy loss over the test samples,
LP F N(W; ϕ) = E({(xt,yt)}∪D)∼p(ϕ)[−log(gW(yt|xt, D)].

Since our goal is to learn a foundation model which performs well across all data generation schemes,
we want to introduce an adversary who can shift the distribution over data generators to identify
the regions over which gW yields the worst performance. However, there is a key subtlety in this
formulation. Maximizing the loss alone could lead the maximizer to select data generators which
produce datasets that are hard to learn for any model. Instead, we want to maximize the optimality
gap between the model’s current performance and the best achievable performance. Since we are
using the cross-entropy loss, we can frame this optimality gap in terms of the conditional entropy
of the data. For any data generator ϕ, let us denote the features as a random variable Zx and the
target as Zy such that (Zx, Zy) ∼p(ϕ). Then the Bayes’ optimal predictor is exactly f ∗= p(Zy|Zx)
which achieves the cross-entropy loss LCE(f ∗) = Hϕ(Zy|Zx), the conditional entropy of the target
Zy given the features Zx. It follows that for any fixed data generator, LP F N ≥H(Zy|Zx), and

3
Domain-Adapted Tabular Foundation Models
In our RAD-TFM methodology, the key to adapting a TFM to a specific domain is successfully
constructing a reference distribution P ∈∆Θ that connects a corpus of real-world datasets to a
corresponding distribution over dataset generators. We will then use this reference distribution in our
DRO formulation given in Equation 5 to adversarially generate datasets that challenge the TFM while
remaining similar to the real-world datasets observed in the given domain. However, tabular datasets
are structurally heterogeneous, with varying numbers and types of features, patterns, and targets. This
motivates the need for a shared latent space for all tabular datasets that encodes semantic meaning
through which we can project and compare datasets. To accomplish this, we rely on meta-feature
extraction, a well-studied topic in meta-learning [5], to efficiently summarize any given tabular
dataset into a fixed-size vector of statistical and information-theoretic properties. An illustration of
our domain-adaptation scheme is given in Figure 2. Full details are given in Appendix A.

Robust Tabular Foundation Models
In this section, we give an overview of our two-stage optimization algorithm, ROBUST AND DOMAIN-
ADAPTED TABULAR FOUNDATION MODELS (RAD-TFM), which is visualized in Figure 1. The
pseudocode and additional details are provided in Appendix B. We first decompose the problem
naturally into a maximization stage (parameter search) and a minimization stage (model training).
We assume access to a pretrained TFM, gW, with weights W which will be used as the input model
for our RAD-TFM algorithm. While not absolutely necessary, starting with a pretrained model
gives us a warm-start for the model weights. In addition, we specify a fixed set of baseline models
f1, . . . , fe which will be used to estimate the optimality gap. We begin with the maximization stage,
during which we freeze gW to maximize the optimality gap in its current state. Since the cardinality
of the parameter space is large in practice, we use a black-box optimization algorithm [1, 36, 3] to
efficiently search the space for parameters with large optimality gaps.

Experiments
Experiment Setup. We evaluate our RAD-TFM algorithm on broad benchmark classification
datasets from TabArena [14], TabPertNet [40], and PMLB [26], as well as three domain-specific
benchmarks derived from datasets available on OpenML [4]. We use the open-source model weights
from TabPFN V2 [23] to initialize our TFM, and further apply the same pipeline without architectural
modifications to the TabICL V2 classifier [30]. We provide a full description of the experiment setup
and results in Appendix G. For the RAD-TFM algorithm, we use ntrials = 100 and nds = 20
datasets to estimate the optimality gap for each trial. During the minimization stage, we use a
learning rate of lr = 1e-5 and a batch size of b = 64. We set ρ = 0.1 · ρmax, where ρmax is the
maximum KL-divergence given the learned reference distribution P. Depending on the task, we train
on 1,000–3,000 generated datasets per epoch and run the full max-min loop for e ∈[5, 30] epochs,
using at most 90k generated training datasets in any run. For baseline models, we use Random Forest,
CatBoost, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and MLPs. For the primary TabPFN benchmark tables, we
also finetune TabPFN-V2 on each evaluation dataset for 10 epochs and report held-out performance as
TabPFN (FT). In a separate compute-controlled comparison, we match fine-tuning to RAD-TFM by
training-dataset exposure, batch size, and number of optimizer updates, evaluating checkpoints every
500 training datasets (Appendix H). We evaluate the conventional baseline algorithms using their
respective default settings, and report TFM results without ensembling. All experiments were run on
a single node with one A100 GPU; further details of the compute environment and SCM parameter
space are provided in Appendices G and D. Following previous work, we report normalized ROC-
AUC as our primary metric, which scales raw ROC-AUC scores to [0,1], with the worst-performing
model receiving a score of 0, and the best a score of 1 [21, 17]. This provides consistency when
comparing performance across datasets, and measures how close each model comes to the best
possible model on average. For multi-class tasks, we use the One-versus-One ROC-AUC score. We
also report the mean rank and number of rank-1 wins for each model, based on the normalized AUC
scores.

6
Conclusion
We introduce RAD-TFM, a model-agnostic framework for robust, domain-adapted continued training
of TFMs. We demonstrate its ability to improve TabPFN-V2 using less than 0.1% of the original
pretraining data, across six different tabular benchmarks. We further improve the architecturally
distinct TabICL V2 classifier across three major benchmarks, demonstrating the generality of our
approach. We show that constraining the robust training process to generate synthetic data that
is similar to real-world data can be done efficiently, and that both domain adaptation and robust
parameter selection independently contribute to performance. Compute-matched comparisons further
distinguish the gains from training on robust, domain-adapted synthetic data from simply training for
longer on real datasets. The foreign-exchange experiments further highlight the role of the synthetic
generator family: extending its support to temporal data improves domain coverage and enables
further adaptation. Our framework can also be extended to other TFMs and regression tasks. The
RAD-TFM algorithm is highly-parallelizable, enabling large-scale training and efficient parameter
search. Together, these results demonstrate how robust optimization and flexible SCM-based synthetic
data generation can improve TFM performance across diverse domains.

In this Section, we provide a detailed overview of the domain-adaptation process introduced in
Section 3. We begin by defining the set of meta-features used to encode tabular datasets, then define
our surrogate reward for the Bayesian Optimization process to maximize partial coverage of the
real data distribution, and finally introduce our algorithm for constructing the global mixture over
synthetic data generators. Although the overall pipeline involves multiple steps, the meta-features we
define are inexpensive to compute, and we are able to solve the global mixture optimization efficiently
via the Sinkhorn algorithm. As a result, we found that for all of our experiments, we can recover the
approximate reference distribution over synthetic data generators in under 10 minutes, using a single
A100 GPU on standard hardware. Further, this process only needs to be run once for each domain,
since the same reference distribution can be re-used across many training runs of the RAD-TFM
algorithm. We summarize the pseudocode for our domain-adaptation pipline in Algorithms 1 and 2.

SCM Parameters
We summarize the MLP-based SCM parameter space used in our experiments in Table 6. In this
work, we instantiate SCMs as randomized MLPs, so the search space includes both architectural
parameters and dataset-level properties. The MLP-specific parameters are the hidden width µh,
number of layers µl, input width µxin, activation function µa, shuffled categorical ratio µpshuffle, node
noise scale µnoise, and layer sparsity µsparsity. These are combined with shared dataset parameters
controlling the number of observed features µzx, number of classes µnc, mean number of categories
µmean_cat, categorical feature ratio µpcat, missing-value ratio µpmissing, missingness mechanism
µmissingness_type, class-balance concentration µclass_balance, weight scaling µweight_scaling, and input
distribution µdist. Integer-valued parameters are sampled from truncated normal distributions over
the ranges shown in Table 6 and then rounded to the nearest integer, while continuous parameters are
sampled from truncated normals on their admissible intervals. Categorical parameters are sampled
with an ϵ-greedy scheme: with probability 1 −ϵ we select the reference value and with probability
ϵ we sample uniformly from the remaining choices. We also implemented Tree-based SCMs, as
in related literature, but found that they did not make a significant contribution to our RAD-TFM
pipeline, so we exclude them here for brevity [31].

F
Generator Coverage on Foreign-Exchange Data
Effect of the Generator Family. To investigate the limitations imposed by the synthetic generator
family, we consider a corpus of 64 foreign-exchange datasets from OpenML [4], derived from daily
candlestick price series. These datasets exhibit strong temporal dependencies that are not naturally
represented by the non-temporal SCMs used in our main experiments. With the original generators,
the entropy-regularized optimal transport alignment objective between the real and synthetic dataset
embeddings is approximately Sd = 2, compared with values below 0.2 on the other evaluated
domains. We also observe no empirical coverage (C = 0), where coverage is the fraction of reference
datasets whose nearest synthetic embedding is at least as close as a resampled real-data neighbor,
averaged over resampling trials.
To expand the generator family, we introduce a temporal SCM with controllable latent dimension,
autoregressive persistence, feature-noise ratio, maximum lag, prediction horizon, and target signal
strength. This extended parameter space reduces the alignment objective to approximately Sd = −0.6
and increases coverage to C = 0.05. Although coverage remains limited, the additional temporal
structure permits closer alignment with the reference domain. Table 8 reports the resulting mean
test AUC. Neither fine-tuning nor RAD-TFM with the original generators improves upon the frozen
backbone; in contrast, the temporal extension yields a modest improvement. The results illustrate that
reweighting generators cannot compensate for missing data-generating mechanisms, while expanding
22


the SCM family can extend the range of domains amenable to robust, domain-adapted continued
training.

to approximate the reference distribution for the given domain. We report our test results on N = 70
datasets from TabPertNet, N = 91 datasets from PMLB, N = 21 datasets from TabArena, N = 31
dataset for Healthcare, N = 11 datasets for Genomics, and N = 20 datasets for Engineering. The
original genomics datasets have O(10k) features, so we first preprocess these datasets by randomly
selecting a subset of d = 500 features and down-sampling. For TabArena, we use the same folds as
defined in the original work and corresponding leaderboard. For the OpenML datasets, we use the
same train/test splits as provided from the database. For TabPertNet, we use the same train/test split
used in the original paper. We report ROC-AUC for binary classification tasks and AUC one-vs-one
(OVO) for multi-class tasks.
Compute Resources. All experiments were run on a single 80GB A100 GPU together with up to
140 CPU cores during the robust parameter-search stage. Constructing the domain-adapted reference
distribution for a benchmark required approximately 10 minutes of GPU time, while each max-min
iteration of the RAD-TFM algorithm required approximately 1 GPU-hour; we used 5 epochs for
Genomics, 10 epochs for Engineering and Healthcare, and 30 epochs for TabPertNet, TabArena, and
PMLB. The CPU-intensive parameter-search stage used roughly 140 cores for about 6 hours per full
training run.

1. Claims
Question: Do the main claims made in the abstract and introduction accurately reflect the
paper’s contributions and scope?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The abstract and introduction state the paper’s theoretical, algorithmic, and
empirical contributions, and these are matched by the formal development in Sections 2–4
and the experiments in Section 5.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the abstract and introduction do not include the claims
made in the paper.
• The abstract and/or introduction should clearly state the claims made, including the
contributions made in the paper and important assumptions and limitations. A [No] or
[N/A] answer to this question will not be perceived well by the reviewers.
• The claims made should match theoretical and experimental results, and reflect how
much the results can be expected to generalize to other settings.
• It is fine to include aspirational goals as motivation as long as it is clear that these goals
are not attained by the paper.
2. Limitations
Question: Does the paper discuss the limitations of the work performed by the authors?
Answer: [Yes]
Justification: The paper discusses several concrete limitations, including limited statistical
power for small benchmarks, challenging domains where the current SCM space and meta-
features may be insufficient, and design restrictions such as fixed generated sample sizes;
see Section 5 and Appendices A and D.
Guidelines:
• The answer [N/A] means that the paper has no limitation while the answer [No] means
that the paper has limitations, but those are not discussed in the paper.
• The authors are encouraged to create a separate “Limitations” section in their paper.
• The paper should point out any strong assumptions and how robust the results are to
violations of these assumptions (e.g., independence assumptions, noiseless settings,
model well-specification, asymptotic approximations only holding locally). The authors
should reflect on how these assumptions might be violated in practice and what the
implications would be.
• The authors should reflect on the scope of the claims made, e.g., if the approach was
only tested on a few datasets or with a few runs. In general, empirical results often
depend on implicit assumptions, which should be articulated.
• The authors should reflect on the factors that influence the performance of the approach.
For example, a facial recognition algorithm may perform poorly when image resolution
is low or images are taken in low lighting. Or a speech-to-text system might not be
used reliably to provide closed captions for online lectures because it fails to handle
technical jargon.
• The authors should discuss the computational efficiency of the proposed algorithms
and how they scale with dataset size.
• If applicable, the authors should discuss possible limitations of their approach to
address problems of privacy and fairness.
• While the authors might fear that complete honesty about limitations might be used by
reviewers as grounds for rejection, a worse outcome might be that reviewers discover
limitations that aren’t acknowledged in the paper. The authors should use their best
judgment and recognize that individual actions in favor of transparency play an impor-
tant role in developing norms that preserve the integrity of the community. Reviewers
will be specifically instructed to not penalize honesty concerning limitations.

Guidelines:

(a) If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how

(b) If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe

(c) If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should
either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce
the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct
the dataset).

authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility.
In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in
