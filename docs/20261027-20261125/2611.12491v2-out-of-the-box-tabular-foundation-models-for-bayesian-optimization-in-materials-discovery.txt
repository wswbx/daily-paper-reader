Abstract
Bayesian optimization is widely used in active learning loops for materials sci-
ence. Gaussian processes serve as the de-facto surrogate model family, but require
task-dependent kernel choices and can lack the modeling capacity for complex
relationships. Tabular foundation models (TFMs) have recently emerged as the
state-of-the-art for predictive tasks on structured data, without requiring bespoke
kernels or hyperparameter tuning. We evaluate two TFMs, TabPFN and TabICL,
as out-of-the-box surrogate models on two standard materials science benchmarks,
comparing against a large field of baselines encompassing Gaussian processes,
random forests, and CatBoost. Our findings indicate that TFMs are highly competi-
tive surrogate models across the two benchmarks, without task-specific training
for active learning or GP kernel selection. Our results motivate developing TFMs
further as surrogates for active learning in materials discovery.

Introduction
Materials discovery is characterized by costly experimental pipelines, where rapid iteration cycles are
stifled by the cost of evaluating a candidate set of input parameters. As such, Bayesian optimization
(BO) is a natural paradigm to explore the space of experimental configurations, given past results
[15, 28, 9]. As running a full active learning campaign for each new candidate method is both
impractical and ineffective, methods are commonly evaluated and compared retrospectively in
completed learning campaigns [36, 26]. The default surrogate model for the BO loop in these settings
is a Gaussian process (GP) [26, 20, 37].
However, Müller et al. [30] have shown in-context learning to be a general and flexible method for
surrogate modeling, with strong performance in hyperparameter optimization. While this approach
relies on task-dependent priors provided by the user, Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs), trained
on a single task-independent prior, are promising candidates as general surrogates for BO. Indeed,
these models have become the state of the art for predictive tasks on structured data [12, 32],
by leveraging the principle of pretraining on large-scale synthetic datasets in place of per-dataset
fitting [21, 33, 17, 18, 47, 46]. TFMs thus combine native predictive distributions [25] with the
modeling capacity of neural network-based surrogates [40], without requiring campaign-specific
kernel selection.
In practice, GIT-BO [45] utilizes the TFM TabPFN in a high-dimensional continuous engineering
design setting, and Rogers and Ponnada [35] find an off-the-shelf TabPFN competitive with BO-
pretrained transformers on hyperparameter tuning. None of these settings is materials discovery, and
rankings need not carry across domains [44]. Hu et al. [22] provide first evidence that TFMs can
outperform GPs in small-budget chemistry campaigns, under their own protocol, against one GP,

Scope and contribution.
We ask whether off-the-shelf TFMs, with no training for BO or chemistry
and no tuning, work as drop-in surrogates under the published protocols of established materials
benchmarks; we propose no new method. We evaluate TabPFN and TabICL on 15 retrospective, pool-
based campaigns from two benchmarks [26, 20], against 56 arms: seven GP kernels, random forests,
CatBoost and both TFMs, each under five acquisition rules, plus random search. A TabPFN arm ranks
first on both benchmarks (Figure D.3). By avoiding campaign-specific GP kernel selection, tabular
foundation models offer a low-friction surrogate for small-data, closed-loop materials optimization.

Setup
We consider retrospective, pool-based active learning tasks and study how using TFMs as surrogate
models in a Bayesian optimization (BO) loop compares to other methods. A pool is the finite set
of candidate conditions a published study actually measured, together with its recorded outcomes.
An active learning algorithm explores that pool sequentially, optimizing for a given target: at each
round a surrogate is fitted to what has been queried so far while an acquisition function selects the
next candidate. What makes the task retrospective is that every outcome is already on record, so a
campaign can be replayed over many seeds at no experimental cost. Figure 1 illustrates this setup.
Benchmark datasets.
We use two benchmarks: the liang dataset containing five materials discov-
ery tasks (3-5 variables; 94-600 candidates) [26] and the (raw) olympus datasets with ten chemistry
and colorimetry tasks (3-6 variables; 66-1386 candidates) [20]. See Appendix A for all details of
the benchmark datasets. Following Liang et al. [26], for each run, we randomly sample two initial
candidates from the pool, acquire one more point per iteration and repeat until the pool is exhausted.
Different random seeds determine the initial starting candidate pair, as well as any algorithmic
randomness. Method rankings are sensitive to the initial design, budget and scoring rule [36, 4], so
we adopt each benchmark’s own seeds and scoring rule: 50 seeds and Top-5% on liang, 100 seeds
and best achieved value on olympus. We deviate in one respect. Häse et al. [20] fit emulators to their
raw data and have planners query those emulators, whereas we replay the recorded pools directly.
Selecting from a fixed candidate set is the setting we study, and an emulator smooths the design space,
which may favor surrogates whose assumptions match that smoothness [26].
Baselines.
We pair eleven surrogates with five acquisition rules and report the full combinatorial set.
As surrogate models we consider Gaussian processes with seven kernels [26, 39], since a GP’s kernel
choice is itself non-trivial and task-dependent [10], random forests [5], CatBoost [31] and tabular
foundation models (TabICL [33] and TabPFN-3 [18]). Every surrogate is run under the acquisition
rules greedy [7], expected improvement (EI) [29, 23], probability of improvement (PI) [24] and
UCB1, UCB2 [6, 41]. Additionally, we report the baseline of randomly choosing the next candidate
point. The full enumeration of all surrogate-acquisition pairs is given in Appendix B.

Features and predictive distributions.
All surrogates see the same features: the benchmark’s
raw input variables (3–6 numeric variables), with no descriptors or feature engineering. Targets are
negated for minimization tasks. GPs normalize inputs and standardize targets; the other surrogates
take raw values. Every surrogate is refitted on the queried rows at each round; for TFMs, these rows
form the context of one forward pass, with no weight updates. Acquisition uses each TFM’s native
predictive distribution, with no Gaussian approximation: EI, PI and UCB are computed exactly on
TabPFN’s bar distribution, and from TabICL’s predicted quantile function. UCBβ is the predictive
quantile at probability Φ(β), β ∈{1, 2}, which equals µ + βσ for a Gaussian. GPs, random forests
and CatBoost use the Gaussian forms on their predictive mean and standard deviation, and greedy
ranks by the mean (Table B.3, Appendix B.1).
Metrics.
Top-5% is the fraction of the pool’s top 5% candidates found. Given the different sizes
of pools, budgets are stated as a fraction of the pool. To aggregate results over settings within a
benchmark, we employ the Normalized Improvement (NI) metric (cf. Appendix C) [11], which
rescales every arm within a campaign and budget onto the range [0, 1]: the worst arm maps to 0 and
the best to 1. This metric is then averaged over tasks to obtain the final aggregate score.

Results
Figure 2 shows the best observed arm per surrogate family, selected by area under the NI curve within
each benchmark (Figure D.3) [19, 43]. Selection includes the acquisition rule for every family and
the kernel for GPs. These curves thus describe configurations selected on the evaluated results. All
56 arms appear in Figures D.4 and D.5.
Across both benchmarks, the TFMs are competitive with the other baselines over most of the budget
range. Seed-to-seed spread within a campaign is large (Figures D.1 and D.2). Following Shields et al.
[37], we test each campaign with a Welch t-test on performance AUC over seeds (Appendix D.4).
The TabPFN arm of Figure 2 is better than each other family’s arm on most campaigns and worse on
at most one (Table D.1). Both arms are selected post hoc, so selection bias remains.

Kernel choices for GPs.
The per-campaign results for liang in Figure 3 show considerable
heterogeneity across GP kernels, the design of which is an active concern in chemistry applications
[16] and a known source of variation in this benchmark family [36, 26]. The MLP arm exemplifies
this best: it performs best on some campaigns (AgNP and Perovskite), while doing worst on another
(AutoAM). On AutoAM and Perovskite, the spread across kernels is comparable to the gap to
random search. The best kernel differs across campaigns, making kernel choice a model selection
problem [10, 27]. Kernel misspecification can impair optimization [3]. TFMs avoid this choice, while
acquisition selection remains consequential.
Metric dependence.
Scoring the same runs on the alternative metric reorders the field. On
olympus, the TFMs stand out more under the benchmark’s own metric (best value) than under
Top-5%. Appendix D.2 shows both benchmarks under the alternative metric.
4
Discussion
We find that tabular foundation models are highly competitive surrogates for retrospective pool-based
active learning in materials discovery, despite being used entirely out-of-the-box and not being
trained for sequential optimization or chemistry. Across two established benchmarks with small,
low-dimensional pools [26, 20], they compete with or outperform carefully specified GP surrogates
while avoiding GP kernel selection. Evaluating further benchmarks [36, 37] is an important next step.
Limitations.
Our benchmarks are retrospective, with small pools and few variables. Replay
measures how well a method reorders an existing pool, not which data it would collect, so our results
do not establish superiority in live closed-loop campaigns. The pools are also shaped by earlier
policies: four of five liang campaigns were collected along the path of a BO-guided campaign [26],
potentially favoring BO-like policies. Autonomous laboratories [1, 14, 38, 13] may enable “live”
benchmarks.
As discussed in Section 3, the ranking and ultimately the interpretation of what constitutes a good
model depend crucially on the chosen evaluation protocol and metric. We follow the evaluation
schemes of the considered benchmarks in an attempt to minimize the effect of custom metrics, but
4


even among the two benchmarks in this work, underlying metrics differ. The development and
widespread adoption of unified benchmarks have been central to the success of TFM development
[12, 32], and we believe similar efforts for materials science could catalyze the development of active
learning methods as well.
Outlook.
TFMs can take any auxiliary information expressible as rows, such as prior measurements,
sibling campaigns or computed descriptors, directly into their context, whereas GPs typically need
purpose-built multi-task or transfer kernels [42]. Existing TFMs [18, 33] scale quadratically with
context size, against the cubic cost of GPs.
Our experiments use TFMs without task-specific pretraining. Encoding domain knowledge in their
synthetic training priors may improve performance further. Another direction is to adapt TFM training
to sequential optimization and acquisition. Our results motivate investigating both directions for
materials discovery.

Inputs.
Every surrogate receives the same matrix of the benchmark’s numeric input variables. On
liang, repeated measurements of one design point become one candidate whose target is the mean

candidate. Minimization targets are negated, so every surrogate maximizes.

E
Differences with Hu et al. [22]
Hu et al. [22] also study the performance of TFMs as surrogates for BO in materials science. Here
we provide more details on our differences with this paper.
Different evaluation datasets.
Hu et al. [22] assemble their own collection of ten datasets (copper
alloys, bulk metallic glasses, lattice thermal conductivity; 495–3,148 candidates) and score methods
on a custom metric, the number of extra evaluations to reach the global optimum, starting from
an initial set chosen as the bottom-K% of samples by property value. We instead evaluate on two
previously published benchmarks [26, 20] (15 campaigns, 66–1386 candidates), following each
benchmark’s own protocol, seeds, and metric, with a random two-point initial design.
More baselines.
Hu et al. [22] compare TabPFN against a single GP and a random forest. We
report the full cross product of eleven surrogates and five acquisition rules (55 arms, plus random
search), including seven GP kernels, CatBoost, and a second tabular foundation model (TabICL),
which lets us separate the effect of the surrogate family from that of the kernel or acquisition choice.
In addition, we always vary the random seed for all models compared, including TabPFN, whereas
Hu et al. [22] do not vary it for TabPFN.
Taking advantage of TabPFN’s native uncertainty quantification.
Hu et al. [22] use a Gaussian
approximation for the uncertainty of TabPFN, and introduce normalized and hybrid acquisition
functions, in particular pairing TabPFN’s mean with a GP’s variance, to compensate for perceived
weaknesses of TabPFN’s uncertainty estimates. We use TabPFN and TabICL strictly out-of-the-box,
integrating standard acquisition rules directly against their native predictive distributions.
Differences in conclusions.
While we share the overall conclusion that TFMs are strong surrogates,
Hu et al. [22] report a minimum-data threshold of roughly 10–20% of the pool before TabPFN
overtakes a GP. We do not observe this: in our setting the best TabPFN arm is already ahead of the
best GP arm at the smallest budget we report (5% of the pool), on both benchmarks (Figure 2).
