Subsurface schematisation from sparse CPT data
using the tabular foundation model TabPFN

Abstract
Subsurface schematisation supports geotechnical decisions but remains challenging because sparse site-
investigation data leave substantial gaps in information about ground conditions. This study explores
TabPFN, a foundation model pretrained on general synthetic tabular data, for this task. Using only
grid coordinates and soil behaviour type index observations from sparse cone penetration tests (CPTs),
we first reconstruct two-dimensional sections without geotechnical fine-tuning or predefined layer
boundaries. We evaluate the approach on synthetic sections and at two field sites in the Netherlands,
comparing it with SchemaGAN, a conditional generative adversarial network trained on synthetic
geotechnical sections, and with reference interpolation methods. In the baseline comparisons, TabPFN
achieved mean errors close to SchemaGAN’s on synthetic sections and lower mean errors at both field
sites. On synthetic sections, TabPFN’s mean absolute error decreased at each tested increase in CPT
count, and, under the baseline input setting, its pointwise prediction intervals had mean coverage close
to nominal levels. Subsequent fine-tuning on the synthetic sections used to train SchemaGAN improved
synthetic test accuracy but increased mean errors at both field sites. These findings indicate that
general-purpose tabular foundation models offer a promising route for updating subsurface predictions
from sparse and evolving site-investigation data without retraining.

1. Introduction
Geotechnical engineers need to characterise subsurface stratigraphy and spatially varying ground
properties to inform design and construction [1]. Phoon and Kulhawy [2] described inherent soil
variability in terms of its magnitude and spatial scale and distinguished it from measurement error and
transformation uncertainty. Otake and Honjo [3] emphasised the need to examine how uncertainty in
ground conditions propagates to predictions of structural performance. To support such assessments,
engineers require spatial predictions of ground conditions between investigation locations, together
with the uncertainty of these predictions. Site-investigation data are often multivariate, sparse and
incomplete, making it difficult to estimate spatial correlation structures or assess assumptions such
as stationarity [4]. Cone penetration tests (CPTs), for example, provide detailed vertical profiles but
only at a limited number of locations, leaving layer geometry and within-layer variability between
profiles to be inferred. Engineers therefore draw on assumptions about spatial dependence, geological
knowledge and data from other sites to interpret ground conditions between observations.

Researchers have addressed the sparsity of site observations by modelling spatial dependence
and incorporating data from other sites. Geostatistical methods such as Kriging represent spatial
dependence through a specified covariance model, with parameters typically estimated from the
available observations. Yoshida et al. [5] used Gaussian process regression to estimate trend and
random components of soil spatial variability. Zhao and Wang [6] combined Bayesian interpolation of
a multilayer soil-property profile with clustering of the interpolated profile to identify stratification
from sparse measurements. For multivariate soil-property estimation, Ching et al. [7] developed a
hierarchical Bayesian model that combines site observations with information from a database of
multiple sites while accounting for differences among sites. Using this framework, Saito et al. [8]
compared local and global clay databases for soil-property estimation and argued that the definition of
a site in such databases should account for the complexity of vertical stratigraphy. Engineers applying
spatial models need to assess the suitability of the assumed spatial correlation structure, while those
using data from other sites need to evaluate the relevance of that information to the target site [4].
Hu and Wang [9] combined random-field modelling with Monte Carlo simulation to infer soil
classes and stratification in two-dimensional sections from limited CPT soundings, accounting for
both interpolation and classification-model uncertainty. Shi and Wang [10] used iterative convolution
XGBoost to infer geological cross-sections from limited boreholes and a training section representing
similar geological conditions. Other approaches use deep-learning models to learn spatial patterns
from collections of training sections, so the amount and representativeness of the training data become
central considerations at a new site. Campos Montero et al. [11] introduced SchemaGAN, a conditional
generative adversarial network trained on synthetic geotechnical sections for subsurface schematisation,
and evaluated it on synthetic sections and at two field sites. The authors noted that its performance
depends on the layering patterns and complexity represented in the synthetic training database.
Foundation models rely on pretraining, using broad data distributions to support a range of
downstream tasks [12]. The Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network (TabPFN) is a Transformer-based
foundation model for tabular prediction that, like SchemaGAN, is pretrained on synthetic data, but on
general tabular prediction tasks rather than geotechnical sections [13, 14]. We use TabPFN-3, which is
pretrained on synthetic tabular datasets generated from a prior that includes spatial relationships [14].
At inference, the model returns predictive distributions for query targets conditioned on the observed
feature–target pairs supplied as context, without updating its pretrained weights [14]. This use of
in-context learning, referred to here as in-context inference, allows the same pretrained regressor to be
used with different features and targets. Civil-engineering applications of TabPFN include estimation
of pavement roughness [15] and prediction of pavement condition states [16]. Geotechnical applications
include estimation of rock properties and horizontal stresses [17], as well as spatial prediction of
undrained shear strength and imputation of missing mechanical parameters [18]. Related work has
explored the interpretability of TabPFN predictions through embeddings and feature attribution, with
a view to supporting future geotechnical decision-making [19]. In digital soil mapping, Schmidinger et
al. [20] evaluated TabPFN by augmenting environmental covariates with spatial features derived from
ordinary Kriging, assessing both point and probabilistic predictions. In an extended benchmark, they
also evaluated geographic coordinates as additional features alongside those covariates.
Building on previous TabPFN applications to property prediction and imputation, we examine
whether general tabular pretraining can also support subsurface schematisation.
Following the
terminology of Campos Montero et al. [11], we use this term for the reconstruction of two-dimensional
subsurface sections from sparse CPT profiles. This study focuses on reconstructing a continuous
field of the soil behaviour type index Ic; delineating soil units and assigning engineering properties
are subsequent steps beyond its scope. For this reconstruction, we deliberately adopt a minimal
input representation based on grid coordinates and observed Ic values alone, although geological
interpretations, borehole logs and geophysical measurements could be supplied as additional features
or context observations. For TabPFN, horizontal and vertical grid coordinates are the only features,
observed Ic values are the context targets, and the coordinates of all grid locations in each section
are the queries. No geological labels or predefined layer boundaries are supplied, and no covariance
model is explicitly prescribed. If effective for this task, TabPFN could offer an additional option for
subsurface schematisation alongside conventional interpolation and task-specific deep learning. By
2


adding new CPT observations to the context, we can update predictions and their uncertainty without
updating the pretrained weights, potentially supporting sequential site investigation.
Our contribution is a comparative evaluation of two-dimensional Ic reconstruction from sparse
CPT profiles, using grid coordinates as TabPFN’s only features and the released SchemaGAN model
as the principal comparator. We include Kriging and nearest-neighbour interpolation as reference
methods. From the SchemaGAN study, we use the synthetic sections, generated using GeoSyn [21],
and the field CPT data [11, 22], with input observations and evaluation pixels matched across methods
in the accuracy and prediction-interval comparisons. The comparison evaluates complete prediction
procedures with different priors, including SchemaGAN’s task-specific geotechnical prior and TabPFN’s
general tabular prior. Because the two models differ in their input representations and inference
procedures, their relative errors do not isolate the effect of either the prior or the network architecture.
With both neural models’ released pretrained weights fixed, we first assess accuracy on synthetic
sections and at held-out CPT locations in the field sections. On synthetic sections, we extend this
assessment by varying the number of input CPTs and evaluating pointwise prediction intervals, an
aspect not assessed in the original SchemaGAN study. At each field site, we examine how held-out
errors vary across random input placements in the two sections used for comparison. To illustrate
flexibility in spatial extent and context size, we reconstruct the two sections at each site jointly with
TabPFN using their combined input CPTs. In a separate experiment, we test whether updating
TabPFN’s weights through fine-tuning on the synthetic sections used to train SchemaGAN improves
prediction on both datasets. As an initial proof of concept, this study examines the potential of
general tabular pretraining for subsurface schematisation using one synthetic dataset and two field sites.
Finally, we discuss these results in terms of transfer from general tabular pretraining, sequential site
investigation, and future ways to incorporate geotechnical information through context or fine-tuning.
2. Methods
2.1. Problem formulation
We formulate subsurface schematisation as the reconstruction of the soil behaviour type index Ic
over a two-dimensional vertical section from sparse CPT observations. Each section is represented on
a grid of m pixels. Let sj = (xj, zj) denote the horizontal and vertical grid coordinates of pixel j, and
let yj = Ic,j denote its target value, for j = 1, . . . , m. The CPTs supplied as input occupy a limited
number of grid columns (the input columns) and may cover different depth ranges; we refer to one
such configuration as an input placement. For a given input placement, let O ⊆{1, . . . , m} denote the
index set of the valid input pixels, that is, the pixels in the input columns that hold an observed Ic
value. We define the context set C and the query set Q as
C = {(si, yi) : i ∈O},
Q = {sj : j = 1, . . . , m}.
(1)
The context contains n = |O| = |C| rows, each holding the coordinates and observed Ic value of one
valid input pixel. The query set Q covers every grid location, including those represented in C. Given
C, each method returns a point prediction ˆyj of yj at every sj ∈Q. The pixels used to evaluate the
predictions are specified for each experiment in Section 2.5.
Figure 1 illustrates the subsurface schematisation task and the input representations used by
SchemaGAN and TabPFN. SchemaGAN receives the values in C as an image, with each value placed
at its grid location and the unobserved pixels, whose indices are outside O, set to zero (Figure 1b).
TabPFN receives the same C as tabular rows and predicts Ic at the locations in Q (Figure 1c). The
datasets and preprocessing are described in Section 2.2, the prediction methods in Sections 2.3 and 2.4,
and the experimental design and evaluation in Section 2.5.

2.3. Spatial prediction with TabPFN
TabPFN is a Transformer-based foundation model for tabular prediction [13]. We use TabPFN-3,
pretrained exclusively on synthetic tabular datasets generated from a structural causal model prior,
with a reported training scale exceeding eight trillion tokens. The prior covers diverse relationships
among variables, including spatial relationships, but the TabPFN-3 report describes no pretraining
task specific to geotechnical sections [14]. During pretraining, the model learns to predict targets for
query rows from context rows with known features and targets. At inference, TabPFN predicts targets
from the supplied context without updating its weights.
For each grid and input placement, we supply the grid coordinates (x, z) as the only features
and Ic as the context target (Figure 1c). Each valid input pixel contributes one context row, giving
n ≤32K rows for K input columns (at most 192 for six input columns). We use zero-based column
and row indices as x and z, with x increasing to the right and z increasing downwards from the
top-left pixel. For a 32 × 512 section, x ∈{0, . . . , 511} and z ∈{0, . . . , 31}, giving m = 16,384 query
locations. For reconstruction, we query all grid locations in Q and score predictions on the pixels
specified in Section 2.5. In all experiments, including fine-tuning, we use these grid indices without
supplying physical grid spacings or section aspect ratios, and we do not assign physical dimensions to
the synthetic sections.
We use the released TabPFN-3 default regression weights, identified by the checkpoint tabpfn-v3-
regressor-v3_default.ckpt, except when evaluating the fine-tuned models. For reconstruction, we
use the TabPFN package’s inference-time ensemble of eight members that share the same network
weights, with inference seed 0 (tabpfn version 8.5.0). We retain TabPFN’s default preprocessing,
including feature-wise standardisation. Each member applies one of the four combinations of feature and
target preprocessing supplied with the released model and a random permutation of the preprocessed
features. The predictive distribution of each member is computed with the package’s default softmax
temperature of 0.9, and the eight distributions are averaged to obtain pθ(yj | sj, C) in the original Ic
units, where θ denotes the model weights.

2.4. Comparison methods
We focus on comparing TabPFN and SchemaGAN, which have different pretraining tasks. Schema-
GAN incorporates a task-specific prior learned from synthetic geotechnical sections [11], whereas
TabPFN incorporates a general tabular prior [14]. Both models receive the observations in C, with
spatial positions encoded by pixel locations in the input image for SchemaGAN and supplied as
grid-coordinate features for TabPFN. Their pretraining data, input representations and inference
procedures differ, so the comparison evaluates the complete prediction procedures and does not isolate
the effect of either the prior or the network architecture. We include Kriging and Nearest Neighbour,
the two interpolation methods compared with SchemaGAN at the field sites in the original study [11].
SchemaGAN is a conditional generative adversarial network based on pix2pix [23] that reconstructs
a complete Ic section from a sparse CPT-like input image [11]. Its modified U-Net generator was
trained against a patch-based discriminator using adversarial and L1 reconstruction losses. The
released training implementation uses six input columns per section [24]. We retain the released
pretrained weights [22] and 32 × 512 image dimensions throughout the experiments. Following the
released implementation [24], we apply the same linear normalisation used during pretraining to
the input image after the unobserved pixels have been set to zero, and map model outputs back to
the original Ic units. In the released generator, dropout remains active during inference and batch
normalisation uses the statistics of the current batch. As in the released evaluation code, each input
image is processed in a separate forward pass with batch size 1.
Unless stated otherwise, we use 100 SchemaGAN reconstructions per input placement, generated
using Monte Carlo dropout [25] with inference seeds 0–99. We score each reconstruction separately
and average the resulting errors to reduce dependence on a single dropout realisation (Section 2.5.2).
To examine how the choice of point prediction affects the comparison, we also evaluate the predictive
medians of both models using MAE and their predictive means using RMSE. For SchemaGAN, we
use the pixelwise sample median and mean of these reconstructions. We assess these summaries
for pointwise accuracy; they need not preserve the spatial structure of an individual generated
reconstruction. We also use the same generated reconstructions to construct pointwise prediction
intervals, which were not evaluated in the original study [11]. The released SchemaGAN model was
trained for reconstruction, and we apply no calibration procedure targeting nominal coverage to the
intervals constructed here. At nominal level 100(1 −α)%, we use a normal approximation at each
pixel to define the interval as the sample mean plus or minus Φ−1(1 −α/2) times the sample standard
deviation across these reconstructions, where Φ−1 is the standard normal quantile function.
Kriging and Nearest Neighbour follow the implementations released with the original study [11, 24],
using the same grid coordinates as TabPFN. The Kriging implementation uses Gaussian process
regression with a covariance given by the sum of an anisotropic radial basis function kernel and a
white-noise kernel. For each fit, we centre and scale the input Ic values using their mean and standard
deviation and transform predictions back to Ic units; the Gaussian process has no spatial trend term.
The kernel parameters are fitted separately for each input placement by maximising the log marginal
likelihood with the L-BFGS-B optimiser and ten random restarts. The two length scales and the
noise variance are bounded between 10−5 and 105, and the seed of the random restarts is fixed to the
zero-based index assigned to each combination of section and input placement. We use the posterior
mean for point prediction. We construct central pointwise prediction intervals from the Gaussian
posterior predictive distribution, including the white-noise contribution in the predictive variance. The
median fitted horizontal and vertical length scales were 136 and 2.5 pixels on the synthetic sections,
67 and 1.6 pixels at Delft–Schiedam and 54 and 1.5 pixels at Groningen–Delfzijl. Of the 8,000 fits,
72 produced warnings, including one L-BFGS-B convergence warning; all other warnings concerned
parameters close to their bounds, and all predictions were retained. Comparisons with Kriging refer to
this particular covariance and fitting procedure. Nearest Neighbour assigns each pixel the value of the
nearest observed pixel, using Euclidean distance in grid coordinates, and provides point predictions
only.

2.5. Experimental design and evaluation
For each input placement in the accuracy and prediction-interval comparisons, the methods being
compared received the same input observations C and were evaluated on the same pixels. After
inference, predictions were neither clipped to a prescribed Ic range nor replaced with observed values
at input pixels.
2.5.1. Experimental setup
The baseline accuracy comparisons on both datasets included all four methods. For each synthetic
section, the baseline input was drawn by the released sampling procedure [24] and comprised K = 6
randomly selected input columns subject to its minimum-spacing constraint, with randomly truncated
depths. These inputs covered approximately 1% of the grid pixels on average. We evaluated prediction
errors over all m grid pixels in Q, including the input pixels [24] (Section 3.1). With the same
inputs, we analysed TabPFN and SchemaGAN errors at unobserved pixels by Euclidean distance
in grid coordinates to the nearest observed pixel (Section 3.2). Using the baseline six-CPT inputs,
we evaluated prediction intervals from TabPFN, SchemaGAN and Kriging over unobserved pixels at
nominal levels of 50, 80, 90 and 95%, without additional calibration (Section 3.3).
To assess how the released models respond to changes in CPT count without retraining, we
evaluated TabPFN and SchemaGAN with K ∈{3, 6, 12, 24, 48} input columns (Section 3.2). For
SchemaGAN, counts other than six differ from its released training configuration. We used each
synthetic section’s baseline input for K = 6 and a random subset of three of its columns for K = 3.
Starting from the six-column input, we successively added randomly selected unused columns to obtain
the 12-, 24- and 48-column inputs, without a minimum-spacing constraint. Existing observations were
retained, and added columns used the same random depth truncation as the baseline inputs. In each
section, predictions were scored on the pixels unobserved in all five input placements.
For the baseline field accuracy comparisons (Section 3.4), we drew 1,000 input placements per
section, each comprising K = 6 measured columns. Following the original study’s procedure for
selecting field inputs [11, 24], we drew columns sequentially at random subject to its minimum-spacing
constraint, retaining repeated six-column combinations as separate input placements. All remaining
measured columns were held out for evaluation regardless of their distance from the input columns.
For both datasets, the comparison of predictive medians and means (Section 2.4) used the same inputs
and evaluation pixels as the baseline comparisons.
For the field reconstruction examples in Section 3.5, we reconstructed each site’s two 32 × 512
sections independently with SchemaGAN and TabPFN, using six input CPTs per section. For joint
reconstruction, we placed the two original section grids side by side without rescaling to form a
32 × 1,024 grid, assuming that equal row indices represented the same depth interval. TabPFN
reconstructed this joint grid using the combined observations from the 12 input CPTs. The split and
joint TabPFN reconstructions used the same model weights and inference settings. No boundary
smoothing was applied to any split or joint reconstruction.
The preceding experiments used TabPFN without training it specifically on geotechnical sections.
To assess the effect of such training (Section 3.6), we fine-tuned all TabPFN weights θ on the same
16,000 synthetic training sections used to train SchemaGAN. At each training step, we used C as
context and sampled prediction targets at unobserved locations. The loss was the sum of the continuous
ranked probability score (CRPS) and the mean squared error (MSE) of the predictive mean, both
computed in TabPFN’s internal target space. We trained for five epochs with optimisation seed
0. We selected the checkpoint with the lowest mean MAE over unobserved pixels, using only the
original study’s 4,000 synthetic validation sections [11]. The selected checkpoint was evaluated on the
synthetic test and field sections using the inference settings in Section 2.3. Training details are given
in Appendix A. To characterise how the synthetic training sections differ from the field sections, we
also compared three descriptors of Ic: its mean, the mean absolute difference between adjacent depth
rows, and the mean absolute difference between neighbouring measured columns at the same depth
row. For each field section, we calculated these descriptors at its CPT locations and depth rows and
at the same grid positions in each of the 16,000 synthetic training sections.

2.5.2. Metrics and statistical comparison
We assessed each method’s point predictions ˆyj against target values yj using MAE and RMSE in
Ic. Following the original evaluation code [11, 24], field errors in each held-out column were averaged
over all 32 rows, with zero residuals assigned below the CPT’s termination depth. Synthetic errors
were calculated per section; field errors were calculated per held-out column and averaged equally
across columns within each input placement. For field RMSE, the square root was taken within each
held-out column before averaging across columns. Unless stated otherwise, SchemaGAN errors were
averaged across the 100 reconstructions for each input placement before calculating the statistical
summaries.
The accuracy tables in Sections 3.1 and 3.4 report means and sample standard deviations across the
4,000 synthetic sections or the 2,000 input placements per field site. We also report the percentage of
synthetic sections or field input placements in which each method had lower error than SchemaGAN. For
these tables, we paired errors by synthetic section or by input placement within each field section and
calculated mean differences (method minus SchemaGAN) with normal-approximation 95% confidence
intervals. Field standard errors were based on variation in paired differences within each section, with
equal weight given to the two section means. Field confidence intervals quantify input-placement
uncertainty conditional on the two observed sections and inference seeds. For the CPT-count and
distance curves, pointwise 95% confidence intervals used the same normal approximation, with standard
errors estimated across contributing sections. In all box plots, boxes span the first to third quartiles
and the central lines indicate medians. To complement MAE and RMSE, we calculated ratios of the
mean and median absolute Ic differences in each reconstructed section to the corresponding values in its
target section, using all pixel pairs separated vertically by 1, 2, 4 or 8 rows, or horizontally by 1, 2, 4, 8,
16, 32, 64 or 128 columns. Ratios below one indicate smaller mean or median absolute differences than
in the target section at the same separation. Using the baseline six-CPT reconstructions, we calculated
ratios separately for each section and separation and report medians across the 4,000 synthetic test
sections. For SchemaGAN, we first averaged the ratios from 100 individually evaluated reconstructions
within each section.

4. Discussion
4.1. Transfer from general tabular pretraining
Building on applications of TabPFN to soil-property prediction and digital soil mapping [18, 20],
we evaluated its ability to reconstruct two-dimensional Ic sections from sparse CPT observations
under varying observation conditions, using grid coordinates as the only features. Our results provide
initial evidence that general tabular pretraining can support this reconstruction without geotechnical
fine-tuning. With its pretrained weights held fixed, TabPFN recovered the main layer geometry in a
representative synthetic section and achieved mean errors close to those of SchemaGAN across the
synthetic test sections (Section 3.1). In the baseline field comparison, it achieved the lowest site-level
mean MAE and RMSE among the four methods at both sites (Section 3.4).
Both Kriging and TabPFN combine prior assumptions with the observed data: Kriging uses a
specified covariance model, whereas TabPFN draws on its general tabular prior. This prior covers diverse
relationships among variables, including spatial relationships [14]. Subsurface patterns may share
statistical regularities with the synthetic pretraining data, allowing TabPFN to use learned relationships
to represent local smoothness, larger-scale trends and interactions between the coordinate variables.
However, we did not analyse the model’s internal representations or isolate the contributions of these
learned relationships. The reconstructions therefore do not establish that the model has geological
understanding or recovers the processes that formed the ground. Future work could investigate the
internal representations underlying these spatial predictions through embedding analyses, as explored
in previous geotechnical applications of TabPFN [19].
TabPFN and SchemaGAN differ in their pretraining data, input representations and inference
procedures, so their relative errors do not isolate the effect of either the prior or the network architecture

spatial differences overall than those in the target sections (Table B.1). TabPFN’s preservation of
layer continuity and SchemaGAN’s recovery of a thin inclined layer in different examples illustrate
why local layer geometry should be assessed alongside mean errors (Figure 4b,c). Overall, these results
suggest that, without geotechnical fine-tuning, TabPFN can provide initial section reconstructions for
examining the main layer geometry between sparse CPTs.
4.2. In-context inference and investigation updates
With in-context inference, we can incorporate additional CPT observations and extend the prediction
domain without updating TabPFN’s weights. In the synthetic experiments, TabPFN’s mean MAE
decreased at every tested increase in CPT count from 3 to 48, indicating that it benefited from
additional observations supplied as context (Section 3.2). SchemaGAN’s response to additional CPTs
should be interpreted in relation to its training configuration: its released training implementation uses
six input columns per section. The released model accepted other CPT counts in our experiments, but
its mean MAE increased above six CPTs, possibly reflecting changes in observation density and column
spacing relative to this configuration. Retraining SchemaGAN on larger datasets with more varied
layer geometries and mixed CPT counts could change this response and the comparison with TabPFN.
For larger prediction domains, SchemaGAN can combine predictions from overlapping sections [27].
TabPFN instead combined the input CPT observations from each site’s two sections into a single
context, allowing these observations to inform predictions throughout the joint grid (Section 3.5).
These results suggest that a model pretrained on general tabular data can provide an initial section
reconstruction with default preprocessing, without requiring a representative geotechnical training
dataset. During sequential site investigation, CPT observations from each stage could then be added to

4.3. Fine-tuning and geotechnical training data
Under the training and checkpoint-selection procedure used here, fine-tuning improved synthetic test
accuracy but increased mean MAE and RMSE at both field sites (Section 3.6). In the Delft–Schiedam
example, fine-tuning increased underestimation to the left of all input CPTs while predictions between
them changed less (Figure 11b). In the descriptor comparison, between-CPT differences in all four
field sections exceeded the 99.9th percentile of the corresponding synthetic training distributions,
while vertical differences exceeded the synthetic 95th percentile only in the Delft–Schiedam sections
(Figure 12). One possible explanation for the field deterioration is that fine-tuning adapted the model
more closely to the layer geometries and within-layer variability represented in the synthetic training
data, reducing its ability to generalise to field conditions with different characteristics. We compared
descriptors calculated on the section grids, so the observed differences may reflect how the field CPT
data were preprocessed and averaged into grid rows (Section 2.2). The present experiments do not
establish the cause of the field deterioration or rule out overfitting to individual training sections. We
did not separately assess the effects of the training distribution, optimisation hyperparameters and
checkpoint selection.
Future comparisons across different ground conditions and synthetic data-generation procedures
should investigate the causes of the field deterioration and examine whether site information is better
introduced through context or weight updates. Training sets could vary layer geometries, within-layer
variability and observation patterns, with generation parameters informed by field CPTs. Context
selection could use observations from similar sites, drawing on the concept of site recognition in
geotechnical engineering [4]. Site characteristics, such as depositional history or stratigraphic structure,
could guide the selection of context observations or synthetic training sections, and could also be
included as features when C combines observations from multiple sites. Comparisons of context
selection and fine-tuning at sites not used to design these approaches, using the same available field
information, would test whether each approach improves field prediction and which is preferable when
site information is limited.
4.4. Limitations and future validation
Across the field accuracy experiments, we evaluated two fixed sections at each of two sites in the
Netherlands, using random input placements to assess sensitivity to observation selection. These
placements do not provide independent tests across geological settings, and accuracy at locations
without CPT measurements remains unverified. We have also not tested how field accuracy changes
20


with CPT count or how TabPFN’s predictions vary with its inference seed. Further validation should
include independent sites with different depositional histories, stratigraphic structures and investigation
patterns. Regarding spatial representation, the field targets are depth-averaged Ic values, so the
results do not establish reconstruction of variations finer than the grid intervals. Grid indices also omit
physical spacings and aspect ratios (Section 2.3). For the joint reconstructions, we placed the two
section grids at each site side by side without rescaling and assumed that equal row indices represented
the same depth interval (Section 2.5.1). The illustrated comparisons cover one input placement per
site; they do not establish an accuracy benefit of the combined context across input placements or
assess sensitivity to the assumed depth correspondence (Section 3.5). More generally, sensitivity
to grid resolution and the resulting changes in observation aggregation remains untested. Further
work should examine how these factors and site geometry affect reconstruction accuracy, and how
coordinate representations and internal preprocessing influence TabPFN’s representation of spatial
anisotropy. For the difference ratios (Table B.1), we evaluated TabPFN’s pixelwise predictive medians
and SchemaGAN’s individual generated reconstructions; using the pixelwise median of SchemaGAN’s
100 reconstructions instead changed the across-section medians of both ratio types by less than 0.03 at
every tested separation and preserved the orderings of these ratios reported in Section 3.1. Neither the
difference ratios, which do not distinguish within-layer changes from layer transitions, nor MAE and
RMSE directly measure boundary locations or layer connectivity. Dedicated evaluation of stratigraphic
geometry and engineering-property assignments is therefore needed to assess the engineering usefulness
of the reconstructions.
We evaluated prediction intervals only on synthetic sections with six input CPTs; field point-
prediction accuracy does not establish interval calibration at the field sites. Although TabPFN’s
mean pointwise coverage was close to nominal levels on these sections, this average does not establish
calibration at every location or simultaneous coverage of an entire section. SchemaGAN’s undercoverage
concerns the narrow intervals obtained here from Monte Carlo dropout with a pixelwise normal
approximation; we did not evaluate other uncertainty formulations.
These narrow intervals are
consistent with the limited output variation under dropout reported for pix2pix [23], on which
SchemaGAN is based. Future work should test interval calibration at other CPT counts and at
held-out field CPTs, including at independent sites. To support engineering decisions, future studies
should also develop joint representations of uncertainty that account for spatial dependence. These
representations should be used to propagate uncertainty in reconstructed ground conditions through
geotechnical analyses, including uncertainty in any conversion from Ic to engineering properties. Case
studies of sequential investigation (Section 4.2) could then evaluate whether additional observations
improve predictions of engineering responses and decisions about safety, serviceability and cost. A
longer-term objective is to generate spatially coherent alternative ground models that represent
uncertainty in layer continuity, boundary geometry and material distribution.
5. Conclusion
This study provides initial evidence that TabPFN, pretrained on general synthetic tabular data, can
reconstruct two-dimensional Ic sections from grid coordinates and sparse CPT observations without
geotechnical fine-tuning. In the baseline comparison, TabPFN had mean prediction errors close to
those of SchemaGAN on synthetic sections and the lowest site-level mean MAE and RMSE among the
four methods at held-out CPT locations at both field sites. With fixed pretrained weights, TabPFN’s
mean MAE decreased at each tested increase in CPT count on the synthetic sections, and the model
reconstructed the two field-section grids at each site jointly from their combined input CPTs. Without
additional calibration, its pointwise prediction intervals achieved mean coverage close to the nominal
levels on synthetic sections with six input CPTs, with narrower mean widths than those of the Kriging
implementation compared here. Under the training and checkpoint-selection procedure used here,
fine-tuning on synthetic sections improved synthetic test accuracy but increased mean MAE and
RMSE at both field sites.
Future research should investigate how similarity in ground conditions can guide the selection of
additional context observations from other sites, and whether synthetic training data informed by
21


field CPTs can improve field prediction after fine-tuning. Validation at independent field sites and in
sequential investigations should assess prediction accuracy and interval calibration across different
ground and observation conditions. Further studies should account for the spatial dependence of
prediction errors when propagating uncertainty through geotechnical analyses to assess the implications
for investigation and design decisions. More broadly, the results from adding CPT observations and
extending the prediction domain with fixed pretrained TabPFN weights indicate that general-purpose
tabular foundation models offer a promising route for updating subsurface predictions from sparse and
evolving site-investigation data.

Appendix A. Inference and fine-tuning settings
TabPFN inference (float32 precision) and both fine-tuning runs used PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA
12.8, and SchemaGAN inference used TensorFlow 2.15.1. Each fine-tuning step used one section and
2,048 query locations sampled uniformly without replacement from its unobserved pixels. Training
used one ensemble member with a softmax temperature of 0.9, cycling through the four preprocessing
combinations in Section 2.3. Preprocessing was fitted only to the context observations; context and
query targets were standardised using the mean and standard deviation of the context targets before
the selected target transformation. We used AdamW with a peak learning rate of 10−5 and weight
decay of 0.01, with 500 steps of linear warmup followed by cosine decay. The gradient norm was
clipped at 1.0, and automatic mixed precision was used.
The training and validation inputs were drawn once per section using the baseline sampling procedure
(Section 2.5.1). Because each split’s inputs were sampled with seed 20232023 in lexicographic file-path
order, placements coincided at corresponding positions in the validation and test splits and the first
4,000 training sections, although the target sections differed. We therefore also evaluated TabPFN
before and after fine-tuning on the same 4,000 synthetic test sections with fresh six-column inputs
drawn by the same procedure (seed 20261022), none of which repeated a training, validation or baseline
test placement. For optimisation seed 0, fine-tuning reduced mean MAE and RMSE by 22.6% and
16.5% with fresh inputs, compared with 22.1% and 16.0% with the baseline inputs. To keep the
evaluation pixels fixed across input placements, we also scored only pixels unobserved under both
placements; the improvements from fine-tuning persisted.
We repeated fine-tuning with optimisation seed 1, keeping the training and validation data, input
placements, initial weights and hyperparameters fixed (Table A.1). Both runs were validated after each
epoch using the inference settings in Section 2.3. The mean validation MAE in Ic over unobserved
pixels decreased at every epoch, from 0.1271 before fine-tuning to 0.0981 and 0.0983 for seeds 0 and 1,
respectively; both runs selected epoch 5. For seed 1, mean training CRPS in the internal target space
decreased from 0.1482 at epoch 1 to 0.1243 at epoch 5. Mean training MSE decreased from 0.3180
to 0.2298 by epoch 4 before increasing to 0.3396 at epoch 5. The seed 1 run, including validation,
took 18.1 hours on one NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU and one CPU thread, with a peak
allocated GPU memory of 3.21 GiB.
