Abstract.
We introduce Kumo Tabular, a family of open tabular foundation models for classification and regression,
in three sizes: Kumo Tabular-S (27M parameters), Kumo Tabular-M (61M), and Kumo Tabular-L (214M).
Pretrained only on artificial tables from the Kumo prior, they can make predictions on new tables without
any training. In our approach, a table encoder compresses each row into an embedding, and an in-context
learning transformer predicts the unlabeled rows from the labeled ones. Kumo Tabular introduces architectural
innovations that improve both training stability and predictive performance, enabling the model to scale up
to 150M cells on modern GPUs. Kumo Tabular-L ranks first on eight benchmarks: TabArena, TALENT,
BeyondArena, ScoringBench, STRABLE, MulTaBench, RamanBench, and TabBench-Bio.
These cover
probabilistic regression, tables with over 1M rows or 100K columns, and tables with strings, text, or images.
Accuracy improves with model size in all tested comparisons. All three sizes lie on the accuracy–speed
frontiers of TabArena and BeyondArena. Kumo Tabular-L predicts faster than the runners-up, LimiX-2 on
TabArena (36×) and TabPFN-3.5 on BeyondArena (1.7×). Weights and pretraining code are released under
OpenMDW-1.1 and Apache 2.0, respectively.

1. Introduction
Tables are among the most common forms of data in industry and science, from bank transactions, credit
histories, and online orders to factory sensor logs, patient records, census data, and laboratory measure-
ments (Benjelloun et al., 2020; Shwartz-Ziv and Armon, 2022; Borisov et al., 2024; van Breugel and van der
Schaar, 2024).
For two decades, the strongest predictors on such data were gradient-boosted decision
trees (Friedman, 2001; Chen and Guestrin, 2016; Ke et al., 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018), tuned for each
task and often ensembled by AutoML systems (Erickson et al., 2020). Tabular foundation models (TFMs) have
changed this picture. They can make predictions on a new table without training or tuning on it. Pretrained
on a large number of predictive tasks, they read the labeled train rows of the table as context and predict
the labels of its test rows in one forward pass (Müller et al., 2022; Hollmann et al., 2023). The strongest
TFMs now rank well ahead of tuned and ensembled gradient-boosted trees and deep tabular networks on
TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025) and BeyondArena (Purucker et al., 2026), two broad benchmarks of real-world
tabular datasets (Figure 1).
TabPFN established the TFM paradigm but handled only relatively small classification tables (Hollmann et al.,
2023). Later models extended it to regression and to tables with hundreds of thousands of rows (Hollmann
et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025), learned from richer artificial priors and from real tables (Ma et al., 2025; Zhang
et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026), and began to exploit column names with semantics-aware designs (Spinaci et al.,
2025). TabICLv2 showed that a fully open model can reach the state of the art (Qu et al., 2026), launching
a new wave of innovation. Several models have since pushed accuracy on standard i.i.d. benchmarks even
further (Grinsztajn et al., 2026; Kong et al., 2026; Eo et al., 2026; Tao et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2026; Zhang
et al., 2026; Jäger et al., 2026).
Here, we present Kumo Tabular, a family of fully open TFMs for classification and regression that combine
frontier performance with computational efficiency. It comes in three sizes, Kumo Tabular-S, Kumo Tabular-M,

2. Model Architecture
Kumo Tabular predicts the labels of the unlabeled test rows of a table from its labeled training rows in a
single forward pass. It processes a table at two granularities: a table encoder builds a fixed-size embedding for
every row by attending within columns and rows of a table, and an in-context learning (ICL) transformer
then reasons across rows, allowing test rows to learn from labeled training examples (cf. Figure 3):
Cell Embedding.
Each cell is represented by a token together with two neighboring cells in its row,
following the feature grouping design of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026). As in Kong et al. (2026), values are
expanded into sine and cosine features at learned frequencies and projected based on column type (numerical
vs. categorical) into 𝑑-dimensional embeddings. Missing values are represented by learned embeddings added
to their imputed numerical representations. For training rows, the token also holds an embedding of the row’s
label.

Table Encoder.
In most two-stage tabular in-context learning architectures, the encoder first runs several
column attention layers (attention between cells in the same column), followed by several row attention
layers (attention between cells in the same row), which prevents the column attention layers from accessing
information across columns. Instead, we alternate column and row attention four times in Kumo Tabular-S
and six times in Kumo Tabular-M and Kumo Tabular-L. Interleaving has a similar runtime to staged column
and row attention, but it makes memory optimizations such as row chunking more expensive (Grinsztajn
et al., 2026). Column attention runs through learned inducing points (Lee et al., 2019), so its cost grows
linearly with the number of rows. These points attend only to the column’s training cells, while every cell
attends to the inducing points. Row attention runs over the cell tokens of a row, which it tells apart by rotary
position embeddings (Su et al., 2024). Four learned readout tokens join each row in row attention but skip
column attention. They serve as readout to obtain the final fixed-size row embeddings.
ICL Transformer.
A separate transformer processes the obtained row embeddings. Label embeddings
are added to the training row embeddings before the first layer. Train rows attend only to one another,
so their keys and values can be computed once and cached. To shrink this cache, we use Test-GQA in
Kumo Tabular-M and Kumo Tabular-L. Test rows thus only read the first two key/value heads of each layer,
making the cache 4-fold smaller per train row in Kumo Tabular-M and 8-fold smaller in Kumo Tabular-L.
After the final layer, a two-layer MLP maps each test row to ten class logits or to 999 quantiles of the target.
At inference, tasks with more than ten classes are split into ten-class subtasks.
Model Development.
We build on the open design of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026). We progressively modify
the architecture to improve training stability, efficiency, and predictive performance, then scale up the model.
TabICLv2 introduced query-aware scalable softmax (QASSMax), which scales logits with the logarithm of the
number of keys to counter attention dilution as tables grow. We revised attention normalization and scaling
after observing instabilities in longer training runs and larger models. We also apply length-dependent scaling
to row attention to retain sharp attention on wider tables. We interleave column and row attention to allow
interactions across columns throughout the encoder, refine the transformer blocks, and add missing-value-aware
embeddings to support our expanded prior. With the resulting efficiency gains, we increase the encoder
capacity and scale the ICL transformer to obtain the three model sizes below. We evaluate this progression
through an additive ablation study in Section 7 and report our development observations in Section B.

3. Pretraining Data
A TFM trained on tables drawn from a prior learns to approximate the posterior predictive distribution under
that prior (Müller et al., 2022), so the prior must produce both the varied dependencies and the imperfections
of real tables. The Kumo prior builds on the causal generator of TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026): each table comes
from its own random structural causal model (SCM; Pearl, 2009) and is generated in six steps (cf. Figure 4).
It adds mechanisms that model properties of real tables that random causal models alone do not reliably
reproduce: noisy measurements, groups of correlated features, coarse or code-like columns, targets derived
from a subset of features, skewed targets, and missing values. Section A provides a detailed specification of
our changes and newly added components.
1. Hyperparameters.
Each table draws its own hyperparameters: its shape, column types, and individual
parameters for subsequent generation steps. Within a table, these draws follow per-SCM preferences, so its
nodes tend to use related mechanisms and hyperparameter settings.
2. Causal Graph.
Features and target are assigned to random nodes of a random directed acyclic graph,
sparse in some tables and dense in others. A feature can thus be a cause or an effect of the target, and nodes
without a column stay hidden.
3. Propagation.
Values flow through the graph in topological order: roots initialize random points, and
every other node combines its parents’ outputs using random functions and a merging method. The functions
come from eight families, such as small neural networks, tree ensembles, and Gaussian processes.
4. Readout.
Each node holds a vector of hidden channels, and converters read each column and the
target out of the channels of their corresponding node. Converters can also update these channels with
the generated column or target values before passing them to child nodes. A new distributional numerical
converter samples numerical features and regression targets from distributions whose parameters depend on
the node channels. Three channels control the location, noise scale, and probability of belonging to a shifted
subgroup. The sampled values can then be transformed or rounded to counts. This produces numerical
columns representing noisy measurements or counts, with noise levels and subgroup probabilities that vary
across rows. For categorical features and classification targets, the converter maps node channels to a score
for each category, then either samples from the resulting softmax distribution or deterministically selects the
highest-scoring category.

5. Post-Processing.
As in TabICLv2, outliers are clipped, columns are standardized, class codes are
permuted, and rows and columns are shuffled. Three new kinds of transformations apply at random:
• Correlated feature blocks: Real tables often hold groups of related columns, such as the items of a
questionnaire. In 30% of the tables, we introduce correlations within randomly selected blocks of feature
columns. For each block, we draw one shared random value per row and blend it into the numerical
columns (Synthefy et al., 2026). For categorical columns, we use this shared value together with the existing
categories to reorder category assignments across rows, preserving each category’s frequency.
• Realism augmentations: Different augmentations can modify a fraction of generated tables: target
relabeling makes targets depend on only a few features; feature coarsening allows identical feature rows to
have different targets; high-cardinality codes introduce more than 200 categories to mimic identifiers; and,
from stage 3 onward, heavy-tailed regression targets mimic quantities such as prices.
• Missing values: Missing values can reflect how data are collected. A failed source leaves whole rows
incomplete, a lab value may be recorded only for patients with a particular diagnosis, and an instrument
may not report values outside its measurement range. In about a quarter of generated tables, we introduce
missing values through one or more patterns (Synthefy et al., 2026): missingness across rows, dependence
on other features, values outside specified ranges, nearly empty columns, blocks of missing values governed
by a shared hidden factor for each row, or independent random missingness.
6. Learnability Filter.
A table is redrawn with the same shape if a small ensemble of extremely randomized
trees (Geurts et al., 2006) cannot reliably beat predicting the mean. Additionally, the filter flags too-easy
tables (Pfeiffer et al., 2026), which a tree ensemble predicts with at most 5% of the error of predicting the
mean.

Training Setup.
Training cost grows with the number of rows, so stages move from many short tables to
fewer, longer ones. The first stage trains on tables of 1,024 rows. It takes over 90% of the steps but at most
62% of the GPU-hours. The second stage draws 400 to 10,240 rows per table, while the third stage draws
200 to 60K rows, with fewer features on its longest tables to bound memory. In stages 2 and 3 we also lower
the learning rate and add the realism augmentations described in Section 3. Each stage starts from the final
weights of the previous stage. The three model sizes differ in the number of steps, the batch size, and the
feature cap on the long tables: Kumo Tabular-L takes twice as many training steps as the smaller variants
in stage 1 and 1.5 times as many in later stages, with fewer features in its longest tables. Training a single
checkpoint took about 800, 3,600, and 11,600 GPU-hours for the three sizes of Kumo Tabular (Table 2).
Objectives.
Classifiers minimize the cross-entropy of the test labels over classes present in the context up
to at most ten classes. Regressors minimize the pinball loss (Koenker and Bassett, 1978), averaged over 999
quantile levels, on targets standardized using the mean and standard deviation of context targets.
Optimization.
Most weight matrices are optimized with Muon (Jordan et al., 2024), while the remaining
parameters are optimized with AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019). We apply decoupled weight decay of
0.01 to most parameters, which pulls the temperature coefficients 𝛼ℎtoward zero. Classifiers train in BF16
throughout; regressors use FP16 with dynamic loss scaling (Micikevicius et al., 2018) in the first stage and
BF16 in later stages. BF16 caused the first ICL layer’s mean temperature coefficient to grow to about 30
times its FP16 value, degrading prediction quality in the first stage. In stages 2 and 3, regression checkpoints
trained with BF16 had lower RMSE than their FP16 counterparts on most development datasets.
5. Inference
Kumo Tabular predicts on new tables without further training. Each forward pass uses the table’s training
rows to predict its test rows. The inference stack adds preprocessing, ensembling, and key/value caching:
Preprocessing.
We identify each column as numerical or categorical and fit all preprocessing steps on
training rows only. Categorical values are integer-encoded in lexicographic order and treated as numerical
values downstream. As in pretraining, all input features are standardized and clipped.
Estimators.
Preprocessing affects the computation of a forward pass: (1) the scale of a feature changes
its sine and cosine representation, (2) the column order sets the rotary positions and feature groups of a
token, and (3) the encoding of the classes sets the label embeddings. We therefore average 𝐸estimators
that each make these choices differently, with 𝐸= 8 for Kumo Tabular-S and Kumo Tabular-M and 𝐸= 16
for Kumo Tabular-L. For feature scaling, the estimators take turns among four monotone maps, namely the
identity, the Yeo–Johnson transform (Yeo and Johnson, 2000), the squashing scaler (Holzmüller et al., 2024),
and a rank-Gaussian map. Estimators may also flip the signs of numerical features and regression targets at
random. For the column order, each estimator orders the columns by its own row of a Latin square and keeps
the first 500. For the classes, each estimator shifts the class codes cyclically. Additionally, on datasets with
more than 200K rows, each estimator subsamples its training set to 200K rows.
Averaging.
For classification, SDM averages the estimators’ logits and then applies the softmax. Unlike
other models, we set our default softmax temperature to 1. With more than ten classes, each estimator first
splits the task into ten-class subtasks with error-correcting output codes (Dietterich and Bakiri, 1995). For
regression, each estimator predicts 999 quantiles. At each quantile level, SDM drops the highest and lowest
20% of the estimators and averages the rest.
Cache.
Training rows never attend to test rows, so keys and values of each transformer block can be cached
for fast batch inference. With several estimators, we keep the caches in pinned host memory and copy the
next estimator’s cache to the GPU while the current one runs.

6. Benchmark Results
We evaluate Kumo Tabular on eight tabular benchmarks, covering broad suites of real-world tabular
datasets (TALENT, TabArena, and BeyondArena; Ye et al., 2024; Erickson et al., 2025; Purucker et al., 2026),
probabilistic regression (ScoringBench; Landsgesell et al., 2026), tables with strings, text, or images (STRABLE
and MulTaBench; Blayer et al., 2026; Arazi et al., 2026a), and wide scientific tables (RamanBench and
TabBench-Bio; Koddenbrock et al., 2026; Kreuer et al., 2026).
Kumo Tabular-L ranks first on all benchmarks (cf. Figure 2), with predictive performance improving across
model sizes on each of the benchmarks (Figure 5). Its lowest ranks occur on grouped splits and tables with
more than 100K training rows, identifying two limitations beyond the pretraining distribution.
Protocol.
Kumo Tabular runs with the released weights without per-dataset tuning. Each benchmark
determines the data splits and evaluation metrics, summarized with the leaderboard snapshots in Table 3.
Unless noted otherwise, we use FP16 autocast with 8 estimators for Kumo Tabular-S and Kumo Tabular-M
and 16 for Kumo Tabular-L.2 Six leaderboards rank by Elo, a rating fitted to pairwise comparisons between
entries on each split or target (Bradley and Terry, 1952; Elo, 1978). ScoringBench ranks by mean rank
in the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), and STRABLE by the median score over table-folds,
𝑅2 for regression and ROC AUC for classification. Each Elo ranking is fitted separately, so ratings are
comparable only within that ranking. TabArena, BeyondArena, and RamanBench replace missing results with
RandomForest scores. MulTaBench does not impute missing results. We report median runtimes per 1,000
rows on each benchmark’s hardware. Comparisons on matched hardware are identified explicitly, since entries
may use different devices. For Kumo Tabular, fit time includes processing the train rows, and prediction time
covers the test rows.
Uncertainty.
The statistical evidence for a lead varies across benchmarks and tests. The first-place rankings
are point estimates, and on all six Elo leaderboards, the 95% confidence interval of Kumo Tabular-L overlaps
that of the highest-ranked entry outside the Kumo Tabular family. Against that entry, paired comparisons
favor Kumo Tabular-L on TALENT, ScoringBench, STRABLE, and TabBench-Bio under exact two-sided sign
tests (unadjusted 𝑝< 0.05, Table 4). Against TabPFN-3.5, a strong competitor across all eight benchmarks,
Kumo Tabular-L wins more comparisons than it loses on every benchmark. All five benchmarks with paired
results by dataset, table, or target favor Kumo Tabular-L under the same exact two-sided sign test (unadjusted
𝑝< 10−3). For the remaining three, the reported win rates are 74% on TabArena, 67% on BeyondArena, and
71% on RamanBench.

7. Ablation Study
We conduct an ablation study on the changes that distinguish Kumo Tabular from TabICLv2. Figure 6
presents the results. We start from our reimplementation of the TabICLv2 model and prior, trained with
our optimization setup (Section 4) and a peak learning rate of 3 × 10−4. This setting is the same across
the ablations, and we do not expect it to alter our findings. Because we observed instabilities in longer
development runs, we train the TabICLv2 reimplementation in FP32.
Architecture.
Our first change targets training stability: it adds query-key normalization and replaces
QASSMax with SSMax (Nakanishi, 2025), applying weight decay to the attention scales. This makes the runs
stable and allows us to perform the rest of the ablations in BF16, which also reduces the training time of the
100K-step runs from 6.95 to 5.08 hours. Next, we change the layout of the encoder from 3 column-attention
layers followed by 3 row-attention layers to our interleaved variant. This represents the most significant
architectural improvement. We attribute this to the model being better equipped to handle interactions
between columns. Next, we slightly adjust the transformer blocks by changing the normalization to RMSNorm
and zero-initializing residual connections. We also reduce the number of attention heads in the encoder to 4
and double their size to increase training efficiency. Finally, we introduce bounded QASSMax along the row
attention axis.
Prior.
We next measure the effect of the prior. To account for the missing values added to the data, we
also switch to our missing-value-aware embeddings. The prior change marginally improves performance. Our
implementation also speeds up the training across all three stages (from 4.95 to 4.51 hours for the 100K-step
runs). During development, we observed that the prior changes matter more with longer training. Some of
them only start in stage 2, which brings the largest improvement in our ablation study.
Scaling.
Next, we look into scaling up the model. With the efficiency gains from our architecture and
prior changes, we invest more parameters into the encoder, which we identified during development as a key
bottleneck in two-stage ICL architectures. We first increase the layer count from three row and column layers

TabArena and BeyondArena, stage 2 brings most of the gain in Elo: at every size, stage 2 adds 65 to 170
points, and stage 3 adds at most 23 (cf. Figure 7). On BeyondArena, each stage improves Kumo Tabular-L
mainly on datasets with more training rows than the tables of the stage before: its stage-1 checkpoint keeps
pace with the released model up to about 1K training rows, and its stage-2 checkpoint up to about 10K.

9. Limitations
Generalization beyond the pretraining distribution remains uneven. Pretraining uses i.i.d. tables with at
most 60K rows and therefore does not cover grouped or temporal dependencies, or longer contexts. On
BeyondArena’s grouped splits, Kumo Tabular-L ranks fourth, behind tuned and ensembled RealMLP, TabM,
and LightGBM. On tables with more than 100K training rows, it ranks ninth, behind seven tuned or tuned
and ensembled networks and tree ensembles, together with TabPFN-3.5. The gap on temporal splits is smaller,
with Kumo Tabular-L ranking third and trailing the leader, TabPFN-3.5, by 3 Elo. The strongest limitations
in this evaluation thus concern generalization to unseen groups and very large contexts.
Large tables also come with large runtime and memory requirements, especially when subsampling is disabled.
Additionally, Kumo Tabular can only process single tables, and handles semantic information like text and
images through separate encoders. For distributional regression, Kumo Tabular excels in quantile-based
metrics like CRPS but trails density-native methods on ScoringBench rules that score the predicted density
itself. We used subsets of the TabArena, BeyondArena, and TALENT benchmarks for validation during
development of Kumo Tabular, but as Kumo Tabular also tops five other benchmarks, benchmark overfitting
does not seem to be a problem.

10. Conclusion
Kumo Tabular combines strong predictive performance with efficient inference in a fully open family of tabular
foundation models pretrained entirely on synthetic data. Kumo Tabular-L ranks first on the eight evaluated
benchmarks under their primary metrics, and all three model sizes lie on the Pareto frontiers of accuracy
and prediction time on TabArena and BeyondArena. The remaining gaps on grouped splits and very large
tables motivate further study of generalization beyond the pretraining distribution. By releasing the model
weights, synthetic prior, and pretraining and inference code, we provide the community with an experimental
foundation for developing more accurate, efficient, and broadly applicable tabular models.

TabICLv2 introduced converters as mechanisms to extract columns of different data types from graph
nodes. While its categorical converter contains different sub-mechanisms to extract categoricals, its numerical

et al., 2026; Böhm et al., 2026; Alberge et al., 2026). We bring a flexible family of noise distributions to
tabular foundation models for classification and regression.

A.3. Correlated Feature Blocks
Correlated feature blocks are introduced in 30% of tables (Figure A.2). Between 2 and 𝑚columns are selected
and divided into 1 to 4 blocks, where 𝑚is the number of features. A strength 𝜌∈(0.2, 0.7) and one shared
factor 𝑧per row are sampled for each block containing at least two columns. Numerical values are transformed
as 𝑥↦→(1 −𝜌) 𝑥+ 𝜌𝑧(Synthefy et al., 2026). Categorical labels are reordered to follow 𝑧, preserving the count
of each category. The transformation is applied before standardization. The shared factor has a stronger
effect on columns with low variance, which can closely follow 𝑧after standardization (Figure A.2b, 𝜌= 0.44).
Its effect is weaker for columns with high variance.

• Argmax classes. Argmax selection is used with probability 0.15 in softmax converters for categorical

201 rows (Figure A.3d). Each column is split into more than 200 categories with power-law category sizes

A.5. Missing Value Patterns
Missing values are introduced after target generation and before the split into train and test sets. Each selected
mechanism samples the amount of missingness and the affected rows or columns. Near-empty missingness is
selected first and creates columns with high missingness (Figure A.4g). If selected, structured missingness
(Figure A.4a–e) is skipped for the entire table. Random and latent-factor masking are selected independently
(Figure A.4f,h). Structured and random missingness are used in every stage, while near-empty and latent-factor
masking are added in stages 2 and 3. At least one missingness mechanism is selected for about 21% of
generated tables in stage 1 and 27% in stages 2 and 3. The target is not masked. Missing feature values are
passed to the network as NaN.
• Structured missingness (probability 0.2, in tables without near-empty columns). One of four masks is
applied with probability 1/2. The same rows are masked in most columns under row dropout (Figure A.4a)
or in a few columns under block masking (Figure A.4b). A few columns are masked in rows from one target
tail or one class under target-dependent masking (Figure A.4c). Values are removed from a few categorical
columns under categorical masking (Figure A.4d). Subsequent censoring is based on the upper or lower tail
of a column’s own values or another column’s values (Figure A.4e). Censoring is applied to a few columns
with probability 1 if no cells are missing yet, or with probability 1/2 otherwise.
• Completely at random (probability 0.01). A probability between 1% and 8% is sampled once per table.
Each feature cell is masked independently with this probability (Figure A.4f).
• Near-empty column families (probability 0.05). Observations are restricted to 1–10% of rows for
50–80% of columns. The same observed rows are used for columns in the same family (Figure A.4g).
• Latent driver (probability 0.03). A score is computed for each row as a randomly weighted sum of a
few columns, then standardized and perturbed with Gaussian noise. Rows with the highest scores form a
group in which selected other columns are more likely to be missing (Figure A.4h).

A.6. Data Quality Checks and the Learnability Filter
Table generation combines data quality checks with a learnability filter to select tables for training. First,
constant columns are removed. Classification tables are then checked for matching classes across the train
and test sets, while regression tables are checked for sufficient target variation. Tables that pass these checks
are also evaluated by the learnability filter during the first five generation attempts. If generation fails or a
table is rejected, another table is drawn with the same sampled shape. Generation continues until a table is
accepted or 100 attempts have been made.
The learnability filter uses a predictive model consisting of 25 extremely randomized trees (Geurts et al.,
2006) with a maximum depth of 6, following TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026, Appendix E.14). Out-of-bag error is
compared with the error of predicting the mean, using one-hot labels for classification. Tables on which the
trees do not reliably beat this baseline are rejected in every stage. Tables with at most 5% of the baseline
error are considered too easy. These tables are rejected in stage 1; half are retained in stages 2 and 3 to
include some nearly deterministic tasks. The fraction of tables classified as unlearnable is 27–37% for tables
with 1,024 rows. The too-easy rate is 7–8% for classification and 12% for regression.
B. Training Stability
During development we encountered excessive growth in the attention scales, the learned factors that multiply
the attention logits, in longer runs and larger models. We added query–key normalization (QK-norm),
which helped stabilize training by removing the direct dependence of logit scale on query and key projection
magnitudes. However, QK-norm with learned affine gains still allowed the unbounded attention scales to grow.
As the query and key gains multiply with the scales, their growth could compound even after normalization.
Figure B.1 illustrates the behavior we encountered. Without QK-norm, scale growth was followed by abrupt
increases in gradient norm and training loss. With QK-norm, the learned gains and separate per-channel
query scale continued growing in the first ICL layer, while the training loss remained nearly constant and the
gradient norm rose only later. Monitoring these parameters and gradient norms exposed changes that were
not apparent from the loss alone.

Attention Parameterization.
These observations led us to remove the learned QK-norm gains and the
separate per-channel query scale. In the released model, QK-norm has no learned gains, and length-dependent
scaling has one learned coefficient 𝛼ℎper head. We apply weight decay to this coefficient to discourage
unchecked growth. Heads can still learn different attention scales, and the length dependence helps maintain
concentrated attention as contexts grow. This combination was stable in our training runs and enabled us to
train with mixed precision using FP16 and BF16.
Possible Feedback Mechanism.
Some ICL heads may benefit from concentrating attention on a few
relevant training rows, providing a reason for attention scales to grow even in stable runs. We suspected
that this concentration could also lead to a feedback loop through attention output magnitude. When diffuse
attention averages value vectors that partially cancel, concentrating the weights can increase the output
norm. In a pre-norm block, that output is added to the residual stream without further normalization. If
the larger contribution reduces the loss, optimization may favor still larger attention scales, reinforcing the
concentration and output growth. We hypothesized that cutting this feedback loop would restore stability.
Our parameterization does so simply, without sacrificing performance.
We also tested normalizing the attention output before the residual addition to limit its magnitude indepen-
dently of attention concentration. This stabilized development runs but lowered accuracy in short ablations,
so we did not retain it in the released model.
