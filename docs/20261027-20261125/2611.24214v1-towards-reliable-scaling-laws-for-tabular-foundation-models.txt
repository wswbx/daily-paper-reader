Abstract
Scaling laws are widely used to predict foundation model performance and guide compute
allocation. However, tabular foundation models (TFMs) differ from conventional founda-
tion models in that they are pretrained on virtually unlimited synthetic data from config-
urable distributions, while ultimately being evaluated on real-world datasets. We investi-
gate whether scaling laws for TabPFNv2-style models reliably extrapolate to larger compute
budgets and generalize across evaluation settings. Our analysis reveals differences between
synthetic and real-world scaling behavior and shows that accurately fitting a scaling law
does not necessarily yield reliable prescriptions on unseen datasets. In particular, select-
ing configurations using too few evaluation datasets leads to systematically over-optimistic
performance estimates. We further demonstrate that compute-optimal scaling requires ex-
panding the range of synthetic pretraining dataset sizes alongside model size and training
compute. Increasing the maximum numbers of rows and features sampled during pretraining
improves performance and produces more predictable scaling trends. These insights guide
the training of more efficient TabPFNv2-like models, using the open-source training pipeline
of nanoTabPFN.

Introduction
Tabular data underpins predictive tasks across diverse domains, including healthcare, finance, and scientific
discovery (Borisov et al., 2022; Prior Labs, 2026). Unlike conventional models that require dataset-specific
training, tabular foundation models (TFMs) achieve state-of-the-art performance on small- to medium-sized
datasets without dataset-specific parameter updates (Erickson et al., 2026; Purucker et al., 2026; Jäger et al.,
2026). Models such as TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2025; Jäger et al., 2026), TabICL (Qu et al., 2025; 2026),
LimiX (Zhang et al., 2025a), and Mitra (Zhang et al., 2025b) learn to perform in-context prediction by
pretraining on synthetic datasets sampled from task priors.
The success of TFMs has motivated extensions to time-series forecasting (Hoo et al., 2026), causal effect esti-
mation (Robertson et al., 2025), survival prediction (Böhm et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2026), and hyperparameter
optimization (Müller et al., 2023; Rakotoarison et al., 2024). These applications motivate experimentation
with task-specific priors and model architectures, increasingly facilitated by open-source implementations
such as TabICL (Qu et al., 2026) and TFM-Playground (Pfefferle et al., 2026). However, while such exper-
imentation is feasible at small scales, evaluating promising configurations at competitive compute budgets
remains expensive, especially as models scale to hundreds of millions of parameters (Kong et al., 2026). Scal-
ing laws, widely used to predict performance and guide compute allocation in language modeling (Kaplan
et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), offer a framework for bridging this gap.
Previous studies have investigated scaling relationships for TFMs (Zhang et al., 2025a; Ma et al., 2025;
Zhang et al., 2025b), but accurately fitting observed performance does not guarantee reliable extrapolation
to larger compute budgets. In particular, unconstrained scaling-law formulations can yield predictions outside

the valid range of evaluation metrics, such as ROC AUC (Zhang et al., 2025a). Selecting compute-optimal
configurations on too few evaluation datasets can further yield over-optimistic performance estimates even
with good scaling-law fits. Finally, the synthetic prior design and sampling contain many degrees of freedom
that yield vastly different data mixtures while remaining finely controllable. This makes the prior itself
a scaling axis for the compute-optimal performance of TFMs. We investigate these challenges using over
6,000 training runs spanning model sizes, compute budgets, synthetic prior configurations, and training
hyperparameters. Our main contributions are:
• A systematic approach to study tabular scaling. We conduct a comprehensive scaling analysis
of TabPFNv2-style models, jointly exploring model scale, training hyperparameters, and synthetic
prior configurations. We derive scaling laws and prescriptions for these dimensions, considering both
synthetic validation and real-world performance (Section 4.1).
• Compute-optimal scaling of synthetic prior support. To our knowledge, we provide the first
systematic investigation where we show that compute-optimal configurations favor increasing the
maximum numbers of sampled rows and features (Section 4.2).
• Robustness across evaluation settings. We compare scaling trends across synthetic and real-
world evaluation data and metrics. We further demonstrate that accurate scaling-law fits can conceal
substantial evaluation-set selection bias when configurations are selected on too few datasets, and
we quantify how this bias decreases as the number of evaluation datasets grows (Section 4.3).

Related Work
Scaling Laws
Scaling laws characterize how loss varies with model size, training data, and compute,
typically through parametric power-law fits (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Sorscher et al., 2022;
Alabdulmohsin et al., 2023; Caballero et al., 2023). They are commonly estimated from scaling ladders of
smaller training runs to predict performance and compute-optimal configurations at larger scales (Bhagia
et al., 2025).
However, scaling-law estimates are sensitive to experimental design, compute accounting,
hyperparameter tuning, and fitting choices, potentially undermining extrapolation reliability (Viering &
Loog, 2023; Mohr et al., 2022; Porian et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025). Recent work investigates reliable scaling-law
estimation (Choshen et al., 2025), how to select informative small-scale experiments (Lourie et al., 2026), and
benchmarks for comparing scaling-law methodologies across language and vision-language models (Sermaxhaj
et al., 2026). The cost of tuning scaling ladders (Jha et al., 2026) has also motivated modeling how optimal
hyperparameters vary with scale (Yang et al., 2021; Everett et al., 2024; Bjorck et al., 2025; Ajroldi et al.,
2026).
Scaling Laws for Tabular Foundation Models
Recent work has investigated how tabular founda-
tion models scale with model size, pretraining data, and compute. TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025) establishes
power-law relationships between model size and real-world pretraining data, while LimiX (Zhang et al.,
2025a) studies scaling on synthetic data. Its successor, LimiX-2 (Zhang et al., 2026), fits downstream perfor-
mance scaling laws across multiple benchmarks and extrapolates to larger models. MegaTabICL (Kaushal
et al., 2026) investigates compute-optimal model sizing and training duration. Beyond these scaling stud-
ies, TabFM (Kong et al., 2026) demonstrates strong downstream performance through large-scale synthetic
pretraining, while Mitra (Zhang et al., 2025b) and TabPFN-Wide (Kolberg et al., 2025) highlight the im-
portance of synthetic prior design. While prior work shows consistent scaling trends, it has not sufficiently
evaluated predictive reliability beyond the fitted range. We assess extrapolation errors and transferability to
unseen evaluation datasets, while investigating synthetic prior support as an additional scaling dimension.

A Reliable Scaling Analysis for Tabular Foundation Models
We describe our scaling analysis, experimental setup, and fitting procedure, focusing on deriving compute-
dependent scaling prescriptions and validating their predictive reliability at unseen scales.
Design of the Scaling Analysis
We explore a search space spanning eight architectural, training, and
synthetic prior parameters, including the maximum number of rows and features sampled during pretraining
(see Table 1). We investigate the trade-off between model size and training data volume, fit scaling laws to the
empirical compute-optimal performance frontier, and derive power-law scaling prescriptions for individual
parameters. To assess their reliability, we apply these prescriptions at unseen compute scales and compare
the resulting performance with scaling-law predictions.
We consider both synthetic validation loss and
real-world evaluation metrics, including NLL and ROC AUC.
Tabular Foundation Model Pipeline
We use TFM-Playground (Pfefferle et al., 2026) to train nano-
TabPFN (Pfefferle et al., 2025), a simplified implementation of the TabPFNv2 architecture (Hollmann et al.,
2025), on classification tasks sampled from the TabICLv1 MLP prior (Qu et al., 2025). We chose this archi-
tecture because, at the outset of our study, it was among the few strong TFM architectures with an openly
available training pipeline, enabling systematic experimentation with model configurations and synthetic
priors. We evaluate synthetic validation performance on two fixed, held-out sets of small and medium-sized
datasets, combining their losses into a single validation score. We additionally evaluate downstream perfor-
mance on TabArena (Erickson et al., 2026), restricting the benchmark to datasets with at most 500 features
and 10,000 samples, consistent with the TabPFNv2 evaluation setting. To efficiently obtain scaling measure-
ments at multiple compute budgets, we train with a constant learning rate and save intermediate checkpoints
at geometrically spaced FLOP intervals. We provide further experimental details in Appendix A.
Fitting Procedure
For each evaluation metric, we construct the Pareto frontier over training configu-
rations and intermediate checkpoints to estimate the best observed performance achievable within a given
compute budget, excluding configurations dominated by alternatives requiring equal or less compute. For
metrics to be maximized and bounded above by 1, such as accuracy and ROC AUC, we transform the
objective y into L = 1 −y. We model the resulting loss L as a function of training compute C using,
L(C) = AC−α + L0,
(1)
where A > 0 is a scaling coefficient, α > 0 is the scaling exponent, and L0 > 0 is the asymptotic loss. For
transformed metrics, this formulation ensures that predictions increase monotonically with compute but never
exceed the metric’s upper bound avoiding invalid extrapolations and improving robustness. Optimization
details, initialization settings and the fitting procedure for scaling parameters are provided in Appendix B.

Scaling the Support of the Training Prior

itself exposes many degrees of freedom that can be configured for performance scaling. For this experiment,
we look only at the role of the size of the synthetic datasets sampled for TFM pretraining, under a fixed
prior family. In Figure 3, we extract Pareto fronts under different data prior conditions from our entire
scaling grid collection. For the narrow prior, we restrict the sampling range of dataset sizes (i.e., number
of rows and columns) to a small range. For the wide prior, we sample datasets from a wide range (narrow
⊂wide). The scaled prior is the global Pareto front when no conditioning on dataset size is applied. All

neither the full wide range nor the narrow restricted range matches the global selection represented by the
scaled prior. The scaled prior follows Eq. 1 across the full compute range, for any metric chosen. For ROC

Figure 7 for more details on the custom logistic fit). We then take these scaled prior Pareto configurations
and analyze their dataset properties in the bottom row (Fig. 3), where γ denotes the fitted exponent in
FLOPs. Under compute scaling, the Pareto configurations use larger synthetic datasets as compute grows,

4.3
Robustness of the Scaling Analysis
Until now, we observed the importance of measuring different evaluation metrics for gauging scaling perfor-
mance, where we measure the aggregated NLL or ROC AUC on the set of selected datasets. However, a
scaling fit can be overfit when the evaluation set is small. To investigate this selection effect and validate
the robustness of scaling fits, we hold out each evaluation dataset i from the total evaluation set. We iterate
over different subset sizes n ∈{1, . . . , 20}, randomly sampling without replacement n datasets, excluding
the i-th held-out set. For a given n, we obtain the metric scored under the subset of n datasets, and extract
the corresponding Pareto frontier. For these selected Pareto configurations, we measure their score on the
single i-th held-out set. Both of these scores are repeatedly computed multiple times for different held-out
sets and n. The difference between these two aggregated scores is displayed as the solid line in Figure 4. The
dotted line measures the in-sample mean residual of the scaling law averaged over the sampled n-datasets.
This residual signal is treated as the accepted noise level where we qualify the difference in performance on
the sampled-n and the held-out as negligible. This performance difference falls below the fitting residual
at n = 8 for NLL but only reaches it at n = 20 for ROC AUC. These findings highlight that low fitting
error alone does not establish reliable scaling laws and that an appropriately selected held-out evaluation is
essential for assessing their generalization and therefore robustness.

Conclusion
In a large-scale scaling grid evaluation of the TabPFNv2 architecture, we studied three choices that shape
a scaling analysis: the metric that is measured and modeled, the number of real-world evaluation datasets,
and the size of the synthetic datasets sampled from the prior. TFMs follow typical scaling trends under
every metric we measured, but synthetic and real-world metrics prescribe different allocations of parameters
and data, so both must be measured for reliable extrapolation. The real-world evaluation set must be large
enough to suppress selection bias. Most importantly, the size of the synthetic datasets is itself a scaling
variable and belongs on the scaling ladder. This opens a clear future direction of developing functional forms
that include the prior as a variable and give sharper prescriptions in practice. Our work offers a blueprint
for making TFM scaling practice reliable.

A
Detailed Experimental Setup
A.1
Training Configuration
We use AdamW with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, ϵ = 10−6, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, optimizing
cross-entropy loss. Training uses float32 precision without mixed precision or learning-rate warmup. We
implement effective batch sizes through gradient accumulation, choosing a micro-batch size that fits within
90% of available GPU memory. The data-gathering experiments were conducted primarily on NVIDIA RTX
2080 Ti and L40S GPUs, using one GPU per training job. The data-gathering runs required approximately
80,500 training GPU-hours, excluding evaluation and the extrapolation runs.
A.2
Search Space and Data Collection
Table 1 summarizes the eight hyperparameters explored in our scaling analysis. We conducted manually
designed sweeps at successive compute budgets, refining the search based on earlier results while extending
the range of model and synthetic prior sizes. In total, we collected 6,075 data-gathering runs across four
compute budgets, as summarized in Table 2. We additionally trained 45 configurations at 1017 FLOPs,
selected around the fitted scaling prescriptions and therefore not restricted to the values in Table 1, and
excluded them from scaling-law fitting.
Prior Ablation
For Figure 3, we compare three subsets of training configurations. The fixed narrow prior
and fixed wide prior restrict configurations to maximum prior dimensions of 4 features and 100 rows, and 16
features and 400 rows, respectively. The scaled prior uses the unrestricted frontier, allowing prior dimensions
to vary with compute. The fixed wide prior is the largest considered in this ablation, rather than the largest
configuration in the full search space.

FLOP-Grid Alignment
Evaluations are recorded after optimizer steps and therefore do not necessarily
coincide with the intended compute budgets.
Recomputing FLOPs further shifted previously recorded
evaluations away from their original milestones. To ensure consistent comparisons, we align all runs to a
common grid Ck = 1011+k/4, k = 0, . . . , 32. For each grid point, we retain an observed evaluation if it
lies within 0.01 dex (2.3%); otherwise, we interpolate linearly in log10 FLOPs between adjacent evaluations
separated by at most 0.35 dex. We discard unmatched grid points without extrapolation and exclude runs
with fewer than two valid evaluations. The same procedure applies to evaluation metrics and cumulative
training cells, ensuring consistent compute alignment.
A.4
Evaluation Protocol
Synthetic Validation Data
We generate two held-out sets of synthetic classification datasets using the
TabICLv1 prior (Qu et al., 2025). The small validation set contains 2,048 datasets with 500 to 1,000 rows
and 5 to 50 features, while the medium set contains 1,024 datasets with 1,000 to 3,000 rows and 50 to 150
features. Each dataset has at most 10 classes. Datasets are generated in batches of 32, with the number of
rows sampled uniformly within the corresponding range. For each batch, we sample an in-context training
fraction p ∼U(0.1, 0.9), assigning the first ⌊pN⌋rows to training and the remaining rows to testing. Both
validation sets are generated once and reused across all checkpoints. We compute the mean loss within each
set and average the two set-level losses equally to obtain the synthetic validation score.
Evaluation-Set Selection Experiment
For each of the 21 evaluation datasets, we hold out that dataset
and sample n ∈{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 16, 20} datasets from the remaining 20, repeating the selection 20 times. At
each compute budget, we select the configuration with the best mean performance on the selection datasets
and record both its selection-set performance and its performance on the held-out dataset. We average these
scores separately across the 21 held-out datasets and construct the corresponding performance frontiers by
independently applying a running maximum for ROC AUC or a running minimum for NLL over compute
budgets. We define the generalization gap as the difference between the resulting frontiers, with positive
values indicating higher selection-set ROC AUC or lower selection-set NLL. We average this gap over the
20 compute budgets and report its median and 10–90% quantiles across the 20 random repetitions. For
comparison, we also report the fitting residuals of scaling laws fitted to the selection-set frontiers.

Extrapolation Protocol
We assess extrapolation by evaluating both scaling-law predictions and
compute-optimal scaling prescriptions at unseen compute budgets.
For each metric, we fit Equation 1
to Pareto-frontier observations up to 1016 FLOPs and evaluate predictions at four held-out budgets between
1016.25 and 1017 FLOPs. We additionally train 45 configurations at a nominal budget of 1017 FLOPs, se-
lected around the fitted scaling prescriptions and excluded from fitting. These configurations are included
when constructing the observed performance frontiers at the held-out budgets. We measure extrapolation
error using the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and best observed performance across the
four budgets.

remain small for synthetic validation loss and real-world ROC AUC, with MAEs of 0.001 and 0.004, respec-
