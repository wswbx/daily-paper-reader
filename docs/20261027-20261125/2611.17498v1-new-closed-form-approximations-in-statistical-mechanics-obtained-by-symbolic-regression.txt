A B S T R A C T
Obtaining compact, analytically tractable closed-form expressions for physically complex systems
is a fundamental challenge in statistical mechanics, where traditional approximation methods often
sacrifice either accuracy or analytical interpretability across the full parameter range of interest. In
this work, we address three approximation problems in polymer statistical mechanics, demonstrating
that data-driven expression discovery offers a systematic pathway from exact numerical solutions to
interpretable closed-form expressions suitable for downstream analytical use. To this end, we conduct
a systematic comparative study using two complementary symbolic regression frameworks — the
Deep Symbolic Optimization (DSO) framework, which employs a recurrent neural network trained
by reinforcement learning, and PyOperon, a genetic programming framework with nonlinear least-
squares constant optimization.
We first apply both frameworks to (i) the force–extension relationship of the Worm-Like Chain
(WLC) model, where DSO achieves a maximum relative error below 0.15% and PyOperon reduces this
further to 0.05%, outperforming the Marko–Siggia formula (∼17%) and the Petrosyan approximation
by factors of up to 18. Then, we consider (ii) the logarithmic factorial ln(𝑛!). Trained directly on
analytical data, both frameworks recover the dominant structure of Stirling’s formula without any
structural prior. Using a residual learning strategy on a known continued-fraction expansion of the
Stirling remainder, both frameworks outperform all nine classical formulas considered, from Laplace
(1820) to Chen (2016): for 𝑛∈[1, 30], PyOperon reduces the maximum relative error by a factor
of 1.3 × 104 relative to the most accurate of them, while DSO recovers the next two levels of the
exact continued fraction. Finally, we consider (iii) the non-Gaussian radial probability density function
of the freely jointed chain (FJC) model, where a physics-informed residual learning strategy yields
improvements by a factor of 11.2 over the approximation reported by Darabi et al. (2023) and 4.6
over the steepest-descent approximation. For the FJC problem, the discovered corrections provide
closed-form approximations to the non-Gaussian radial density without relying on a Gaussian-chain
approximation. Across all three applications, the resulting expressions remain analytically tractable.
Collectively, these results demonstrate that DSO and PyOperon serve as powerful and complementary
tools for discovering accurate, analytically tractable approximations in statistical and continuum
mechanics.
1. Introduction
The derivation of compact, closed-form analytical ex-
pressions for physically complex models is a fundamental
task in both theoretical and applied physics. In statistical
mechanics in particular, exact solutions are seldom avail-
able, and the advancement of the field has historically relied
on the quality of the approximations employed. A suitable
approximation must satisfy several competing requirements:
it must be sufficiently accurate across the relevant parame-
ter domain, simple enough to be manipulated analytically
(integrated, differentiated, inverted), and computationally
inexpensive to evaluate. These requirements are frequently
in tension, and the search for expressions that reconcile
them constitutes a significant body of ongoing research in
polymer physics, soft matter science, and related disciplines
[19, 41, 43].

Symbolic regression (SR) has emerged as a powerful
machine learning tool for the data-driven discovery of math-
ematical expressions. In contrast to classical regression tech-
niques, which fit parameters to a model of pre-assumed
functional form, SR searches the combinatorial space of all
mathematical expressions constructible from a given library
of operators, functions, variables, and constants, yielding an
interpretable closed-form equation rather than a black-box
model. The field traces its roots to the work of Friedberg [20]
and was substantially advanced by the genetic programming
(GP) framework of Koza [24], which encodes expressions
as computational trees subject to evolutionary selection and
mutation. Since then, SR has been applied across a wide
range of scientific domains, including constitutive modeling
[1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 21], computational fluid dynamics [5], materi-
als science [6, 11], and fundamental physics discovery [16,
26, 44]. However, to the best of our knowledge, application
of SR to approximation problems in polymer physics and
statistical mechanics appears missing.
Polymer physics offers a natural and compelling do-
main for SR-based approximation. Models such as FJC and
WLC provide the theoretical foundation for understanding

the mechanical behavior of biopolymers including DNA,
RNA, actin, and synthetic rubber networks [14, 19, 43].
These models are characterized by exact solutions that are

This work employs two complementary symbolic regres-
sion frameworks. The first is the DSO [18, 37] framework.
This framework replaces the classical genetic programming
search with a recurrent neural network (RNN) to generate
expression trees. With iterative improvement through gra-
dient updates based on the reward signal from evaluated
expressions, and guiding the search towards high-reward
regions of the expression space it performs more efficiently
than evolutionary methods alone. The second is PyOperon
[11, 12]. It represents Python bindings of the Operon C++
framework, which combines modern genetic programming
with nonlinear least-squares constant optimization and effi-
cient parallel execution. Applying both frameworks allows a
comparative analysis of their respective strengths across the
three problems studied here, and provides cross-validated
evidence for the reliability of the discovered expressions.
This paper aims to demonstrate how symbolic regression
can be used to derive compact, interpretable analytical ap-
proximations for problems in polymer physics and statisti-
cal mechanics. Specifically, we obtain a new closed-form
approximation of the WLC force–extension relation with
the maximum relative error below 0.15%. Further, we apply
both frameworks to approximate ln(𝑛!) across real values of
𝑛∈[0, 100], improving accuracy for small 𝑛over Stirling’s
formula and benchmarking against nine historical approx-
imations from Laplace (1820) to Chen (2016). Finally, we
obtain a compact closed-form approximation of the non-
Gaussian radial probability density of the FJC model.

the symbolic regression methods, including the DSO and
PyOperon frameworks. Section 3 presents the three applica-
tions mentioned above. Section 4 summarizes the findings
and discusses directions for future work.
2. Methodology
This section introduces the two symbolic regression
frameworks employed in this work and describes the imple-
mentation details. We begin with a brief overview of sym-
bolic regression and genetic programming, which form the
conceptual foundation of both methods. We then describe
the DSO [18, 37] and the PyOperon frameworks [11, 12]
separately, highlighting the key differences in their search
strategies.
2.1. Symbolic Regression
SR is a machine learning technique that seeks a mathe-
matical expression that best describes a relationship between
input variables and a target output, without assuming any
specific functional form in advance [26]. Unlike polynomial
regression, which restricts the model to a fixed class of
functions, or neural network regression, which produces
uninterpretable black-box models, SR searches a combina-
torial space of expressions composed of elementary oper-
ations, for example, addition, multiplication, division, and
subtraction, and functions such as exponential, logarithm,
trigonometric functions, and power laws. The result is a
closed-form algebraic equation, making it directly amenable
to analytical manipulation and providing a white-box model
of the underlying relationship [26].
The most widely established SR framework is Genetic
Programming (GP) [24], which maintains a population of
candidate expressions and iteratively improves them through
four phases: initialization (a population of random expres-
sions is generated from a predefined token library), selection
(expressions are evaluated for fitness and the best individuals
are identified via tournament selection), evolution (offspring
are produced through crossover and mutations), and termi-
nation (the process repeats for a fixed number of generations
or until a fitness threshold is met) [24, 26].
The evolution phase applies four variation operators to
produce offspring expressions: one recombination operator
and three mutation operators. Crossover swaps a randomly
selected subtree from one parent with a subtree from a
second donor expression, combining genetic material from
both. Subtree mutation replaces a randomly selected subtree
by a newly generated random subtree, reintroducing diver-
sity without requiring a donor. Point mutation replaces a
single node by another node of the same arity, introduc-
ing minor local variation without altering the global tree
structure. Hoist mutation replaces a subtree by one of its
own child subtrees, shortening the expression and mitigating
bloat [1, 24]. These operators form the core of PyOperon and
of the genetic programming component of DSO in its hybrid
mode, as described in the following subsections.
2.2. Deep Symbolic Optimization
Deep Symbolic Optimization, proposed by Petersen et
al. [18, 33, 37], is a neural-guided symbolic regression
framework in which a recurrent neural network (RNN)
serves as the primary search mechanism. Unlike pure ge-
netic programming, where the dominant search strategy
is evolutionary mutation and crossover, DSO trains the
RNN through reinforcement learning (RL) [42] to generate
high-quality expressions directly from a learned probability
distribution over the token library. The complete procedure
is illustrated in Fig. 1.
In the DSO framework, expression trees are encoded as
pre-order (prefix) traversals: the root token is sampled first,
followed by child tokens from left to right. At each step 𝑡,
the RNN receives an observation consisting of the identity
of the parent node and sibling node of the current position
in the traversal (Fig. 1a). This hierarchical context allows the
RNN to respect the structural constraints of valid expression
trees (e.g., ensuring that binary operators receive exactly two

procedure for each expression consists of three phases:
1. Initialization: The first token (the root of the ex-
pression tree) is sampled from a predefined function
library containing operators (e.g., +, ×), mathematical
functions (e.g., sin, exp, log), constants, and inde-
pendent variables. At this stage no parent or sibling
context is available, so the RNN receives empty inputs
and samples the root token from its initial output
distribution, subject to the prior constraints.
2. Sequential generation: After each token is sampled,
the partial traversal is extended and the RNN’s obser-
vations are updated to reflect the new parent and sib-
ling relationships. User-defined prior constraints can
be imposed at this stage to exclude syntactically in-
valid or physically meaningless expressions, for exam-
ple, preventing all children of a binary operator from
being constants, or preventing trigonometric functions
from being nested within other trigonometric func-
tions. These priors restrict the search space without
eliminating valid expressions.
3. Termination and evaluation: The generation termi-
nates once all terminal nodes (constants or variables)
have been sampled, yielding a complete expression
tree. The expression is evaluated on the training data
and a scalar reward is computed, which is used to up-
date the RNN policy through reinforcement learning.

The DSO framework also incorporates a constant opti-
mization step: for each sampled expression, any numerical
constants are fitted to the training data by maximizing the
reward using a gradient-based optimizer (L-BFGS-B in this
work) [37]. This is performed as part of the reward compu-

structure search to remain in discrete symbolic space while
numerical constants are handled by continuous optimization.
The DSO package additionally supports a hybrid oper-
ating mode (gp_meld), introduced by Mundhenk et al. [33],
which couples neural-guided search with an inner genetic
programming loop. In this mode, the most recent batch of
expressions sampled by the RNN serves directly as the start-
ing population for 𝑆generations of GP. The GP step applies
the crossover and mutation operations described in subsec-
tion 2.1 to produce refined offspring; the best-performing GP
samples are then included in the policy gradient update for
the RNN. This process repeats for each new RNN batch,
so that the GP starting population improves as the RNN
learns. The hybrid approach combines the stateful learning
of the RNN, which accumulates knowledge across batches
through its parameters 𝜃, with the large structural variation
introduced by GP. This combination produces expressions
that are essentially out-of-distribution from the current RNN
policy and helps escape local optima [33].
2.3. PyOperon Framework for Symbolic
Regression
In addition to DSO, this work employs PyOperon [12],
the Python bindings of Operon, a modern C++ framework
for symbolic regression [11]. In contrast to DSO, which
relies primarily on an RNN policy, Operon is a genetic pro-
gramming framework: it maintains a population of candidate
expressions and improves them through selection and evolu-
tionary operators across successive generations. Its overall
evolutionary loop and internal structure are summarized
in Fig. 2. In the following, we refer to the framework as
PyOperon.
Solution representation. Candidate expressions in Py-
Operon are represented as expression trees stored in a
compact linear (postfix) form: each tree is a contiguous
array of nodes laid out in post-order, so that child nodes
always precede their parents and the root is the final element
(Fig. 2c). Since every node is a fixed-size (40-byte) plain
data type element storing its symbol, arity, length, depth,
and value, whole populations occupy very little memory and
evaluation benefits from strong cache locality [11]. Trees
are initialized by a balanced tree creator that produces con-
trollable distributions of tree sizes and symbol frequencies,
giving a well-mixed initial population. The evolutionary
operators, namely subtree crossover, subtree, point, and hoist
mutations, follow classical genetic programming principles
[24, 26] and exploit the linear encoding to manipulate
subtrees as simple array ranges.
Evaluation and constant optimization. Each tree is eval-
uated over the dataset by an interpreter that uses vectorized
arithmetic (via the Eigen library) and can run in single- or
double-precision mode. Crucially, the same evaluation rou-
tine is generalized through C++ templates to operate on dual
numbers as well as scalars, which yields exact first-order
derivatives by automatic differentiation [11]. During fitness
evaluation, this gradient information drives a Levenberg–
Marquardt nonlinear least-squares optimization of the nu-
merical constants of each candidate expression. This refines
the coefficients within a fixed structure so that fitting ac-
curacy improves without increasing expression complexity.
The optimized constants can be written back into the individ-
ual (Lamarckian model) or used only for fitness evaluation
(Baldwinian model); in this work, the Lamarckian model
is used. Both this local search and every fitness evaluation
draw from a single global evaluation budget, which makes
different algorithmic variants directly and fairly comparable.
Evolutionary loop and offspring generation. PyOperon
organizes evolution around an offspring generator: the main
generational loop (Fig. 2a) repeatedly queries for new in-
dividuals until the offspring population is filled or termi-
nation is triggered. A single generator invocation (Fig. 2b)
selects parents, applies crossover and mutation, evaluates

3. Symbolic Regression for Fundamental
Approximation Problems
Here, we apply the DSO and PyOperon frameworks to
three approximation problems in statistical mechanics. Each
subsection follows a common structure: we first introduce
the physical or mathematical background of the problem
and review existing analytical approximations together with
their known limitations. Then, we present the approximation
derived by DSO, followed by the expression obtained with
PyOperon, together with the corresponding data and search
settings, and compare their accuracy with the existing ap-
proximations. The three problems are ordered in the follow-
ing way: the WLC force–extension relation (subsection 3.1);
the factorial function approximation (subsection 3.2); and
the non-Gaussian radial probability density function of the
FJC model (subsection 3.3).
3.1. Worm-Like Chain Force–Extension
Approximation
The WLC model illustrated in Fig. 3 describes semi-
flexible polymer chains such as DNA and actin filaments as
continuous elastic rods that resist bending but are inextensi-
ble and free of twist or torsion.

rotations of chain segments with respect to each other, the
WLC model accounts for the persistence of local orientation
along the chain length and the angle between each neigh-

force–extension relation for the WLC model was obtained
numerically from the exact solution of the WLC Hamilto-
nian, and then compared with experimental data from DNA
stretching experiments [10, 14, 28].

3.3.2. Physics-Informed Residual Learning and
Search Setup
Residual formulations. Rather than approximating the
exact radial density directly, we use the Kuhn–Grün approx-
imation (27) as a baseline and apply symbolic regression
only to its residual. Since the baseline already captures
the principal inverse-Langevin dependence and finite chain
extensibility, the regression focuses on the remaining finite-
chain correction. Learning this correction therefore reduces
the complexity of the symbolic regression task compared
with reconstructing the full density approximation. Before
constructing the residual, the Kuhn–Grün density is normal-
ized separately for each chain length. We denote the resulting
normalized density by 𝑝KG(𝜆𝑟, 𝑛).

Limitations. The DSO and PyOperon searches were per-
formed with different computational budgets and different
numbers of independent runs. Their results therefore de-
scribe the expressions found under the reported configura-
tions and should not be interpreted as a controlled compar-
ison of the intrinsic performance of the two search algo-
rithms. The highest error appears at the shortest chain, 𝑛= 5,

required for further improvement. The reported results show
accuracy only on the limited extension range and do not
establish generalization to intermediate values of 𝜆𝑟or to

4. Conclusion
This study applied DSO and PyOperon to three approxi-
mation problems in statistical mechanics: the WLC force–
extension relation, the logarithm of the factorial, and the
non-Gaussian radial probability density of the FJC model.
Both direct symbolic regression and residual learning strate-
gies were investigated to obtain analytical approximations.
For the WLC model, the DSO expression achieved a
maximum relative error of approximately 0.15%, while the
PyOperon expression reduced the mean and maximum er-
rors to approximately 0.02% and 0.05%, respectively. Com-
pared to the best existing approximations, the maximum
error was reduced by a factor of 6 with DSO and 18 with
PyOperon.
For ln(𝑛!), both frameworks recovered the dominant
structure of Stirling’s formula directly from data, with maxi-
mum relative errors of 7.5×10−2 (DSO) and 1.2×10−4 (Py-
Operon) for 1 ≤𝑛≤30. Learning only the tail of the known
continued-fraction expansion of the Stirling remainder [8]
reduced these errors by more than five orders of magnitude
for both frameworks. The resulting PyOperon approximation
reached a maximum relative error of 5.8 × 10−10, improving
upon the most accurate classical formula on this interval, that
of Hirschhorn and Villarino [23], by a factor of 1.3×104 and
upon the Chen approximation [15] by a factor of 2.4 × 105.
The DSO approximation (1.5 × 10−7) recovered the next
two levels of the exact continued fraction and was the more
accurate of the two for 𝑛≳2 and beyond the training range.
Thus, the learning formulation had a substantially larger
effect on accuracy than the choice of framework. Both hybrid
approximations retain a closed form suitable for analytical
differentiation.
For the FJC radial probability density, the Padé-constrained
DSO and PyOperon models produced similar mean worst-
case logarithmic errors, 7.7 × 10−3 and 7.6 × 10−3, re-
spectively, both outperforming the Darabi et al. [17] ap-
proximation by a factor of 3.7. The free residual PyOperon
experiment reduced the error to 2.56 × 10−3, corresponding
to improvements by factors of 11.2 over the Darabi et al. [17]
approximation and 4.6 over the steepest-descent approxima-
tion. The free DSO approximation was less accurate and did
not improve existing models.
Across all three applications, PyOperon produced the
lowest numerical errors in the WLC, Stirling-residual, and
free FJC experiments, whereas DSO remained competitive
for the Padé-structured correction to the FJC radial probabil-
ity density. Since the two frameworks were evaluated with
different computational budgets and constant-optimization
procedures, these results reflect the accuracy of the obtained
expressions rather than the intrinsic performance of the
search algorithms.
Given its accuracy and efficiency, the proposed frame-
work, in particular the residual-learning strategy, can be
applied to other approximation problems in statistical me-
chanics.
