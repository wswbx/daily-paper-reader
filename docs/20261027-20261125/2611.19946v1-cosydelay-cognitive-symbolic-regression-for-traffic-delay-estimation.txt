CoSyDelay: Cognitive Symbolic Regression for
Traffic Delay Estimation
Lindong Xie, Xiexin Zou, Yang Zhang, Beiyu Song, Zeren Xing, and Edward Chung*
Abstract—Trustworthy estimation of traffic delays at signalized
intersections is critical for modern transportation systems, as
it supports proactive traffic control for congestion mitigation.
Existing analytical methods often rely on idealized assumptions
that limit their accuracy under complex traffic conditions,
whereas data-driven methods may achieve strong estimation
performance but usually lack transparent structures, which
reduces confidence in their practical application. To address
these limitations, we propose CoSyDelay, a cognitive symbolic
regression (SR) framework for automatically deriving explicit
and physically consistent empirical delay estimation expres-
sions (DEEs). Specifically, CoSyDelay decomposes intersection-
level delay estimation into turning-movement-level subproblems,
thereby reducing the complexity of the symbolic search. During
DEE learning, CoSyDelay constructs domain-informed symbolic
inputs from traffic variables. Large language models (LLMs) are
employed as symbolic search operators to iteratively generate and
refine candidate expression structures, whose movement-specific
coefficients are subsequently calibrated using approach-level de-
lay labels. A composite fitness function evaluates the learned
DEEs jointly in terms of estimation accuracy and compliance
with specified traffic-delay principles. Experimental results on
multiple signalized intersections demonstrate that the resulting
expressions achieve competitive estimation performance while
maintaining physical consistency, thereby supporting trustworthy
delay estimation. Source code and datasets are available at
https://github.com/ForrestXie9/CoSyDelay.
Index Terms—Traffic delay estimation, symbolic regression,
algorithm design, domain knowledge, large language model
I. Introduction
In urban road networks, signalized intersections are critical
nodes that strongly influence traffic efficiency and congestion
patterns [1], [2]. Traffic delay, commonly defined as the addi-
tional travel time experienced by vehicles relative to free-flow
conditions [3], is a key indicator of intersection performance.
It has direct economic implications, including increased fuel
consumption and travel time costs, while remaining intuitive
to both technical and non-technical stakeholders [4], [5]. As
direct measurement of delay at signalized intersections is
difficult, trustworthy delay estimation methods are essential
[6]. In particular, such methods should provide accurate esti-
mates while maintaining explicit, physically consistent model
structures for traffic engineering use.
Existing methods for traffic delay estimation are broadly
categorized as analytical or data-driven. Analytical models,

including Webster’s model [7], Akçelik’s model [3], and the
Highway Capacity Manual procedure [8], estimate delay by
substituting traffic demand, signal timing, and intersection
geometry into manually derived delay equations. Their explicit
structures and modest computational requirements have led
to their widespread use for routine assessment of signalized-
intersection performance [5]. However, simplifying assump-
tions and fixed functional forms may reduce their accuracy
under complex and time-varying traffic conditions [9], [10].
Data-driven methods learn delay relationships from observa-
tions using machine learning or deep learning [11], [12]. They
can capture complex nonlinear interactions and achieve high
estimation accuracy [13]. Such models often encode these
relationships implicitly, making their estimation mechanisms
difficult to inspect and offering no guarantee of compliance
with traffic-delay principles [14], [15].
Symbolic regression (SR) learns explicit mathematical ex-
pressions from data, making the learned relationships di-
rectly inspectable [14], [16]. To guide SR toward expressions
consistent with known relationships, knowledge-guided SR
incorporates prior knowledge into candidate generation or
evaluation through grammar restrictions [17], dimensional
constraints [18], shape constraints [19], or formal verifi-
cation [20]. Two gaps remain when applying knowledge-
guided SR to signalized-intersection delay estimation. First,
a unified intersection-level delay expression must combine
traffic and signal variables across multiple turning movements
and phases. This creates a high-dimensional search space in
which existing symbolic-search methods struggle to identify
accurate expressions [14], [21]. Second, existing knowledge-
guided SR methods generally do not represent traffic-delay-
specific principles in their search and evaluation procedures.
An expression can therefore fit observed delays while violating
required delay behavior [22].
To address these gaps, we propose CoSyDelay, a cogni-
tive symbolic regression framework that automates the itera-
tive process of proposing, calibrating, checking, and revising
traffic-delay formulas. CoSyDelay addresses the first gap by
decomposing an intersection into turning-movement-level SR
subproblems, avoiding a single search over all movement and
phase variables. For each subproblem, it transforms the raw
inputs into the flow ratio, green ratio, and cycle length. These
variables are established traffic-theory inputs for delay esti-
mation [3], [7], [8], [23]. Subsequently, CoSyDelay employs
LLM-guided SR to propose plausible candidate expression
structures from these variables [24]–[26]. The movement-
specific coefficients of each structure are then jointly cali-
brated from approach-level delay labels. CoSyDelay addresses

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• We propose CoSyDelay, a novel SR–based framework
that learns physically consistent empirical delay expres-
sions at the turning-movement level and enables multi-
granularity delay estimation at the movement, approach,
and intersection levels.
• We reduce symbolic search difficulty through turning-
movement-level decomposition, domain-informed SR in-
puts, and LLM-guided symbolic search. We further de-
sign a customized fitness function that jointly considers
estimation accuracy and traffic-delay principles.
• We conduct extensive experiments on diverse signalized
intersections. The results show that CoSyDelay achieves
competitive performance against conventional methods
while producing physically consistent empirical delay
expressions.

We begin with some basic definitions used in the paper.
Definition 1 (Traffic turning movement). A turning movement
is a directional vehicle flow from an entry approach to an exit
approach at an intersection. Turning movements are commonly
classified as through, left-turn, and right-turn according to
the relative orientation of the entry and exit approaches. As
shown in Fig. 1, the illustrative intersection contains 12 distinct
turning movements.
Definition 2 (Signal phase). A signal phase is a time inter-
val during which a predefined set of non-conflicting turning
movements is granted the right-of-way. Each phase consists of
green, yellow, and all-red intervals, which together determine
the service time allocated to the associated movements. The
intersection in Fig. 1 has eight phases, each serving its
assigned movements simultaneously.
Definition 3 (Signal cycle). A signal cycle is a complete,
periodically repeated sequence of signal phases; its duration
is the cycle length.
Definition 4 (Saturation flow rate and flow ratio). The sat-
uration flow rate is the maximum discharge rate that can
be achieved by a lane group or turning movement under
continuous green and ideal operating conditions. The flow ratio
is the ratio of traffic volume to the corresponding saturation
flow rate, reflecting the demand-to-capacity relationship of a
given movement.
Definition 5 (Traffic delay). Traffic delay is measured in
this study as the time loss accumulated within the approach
detection area relative to a reference travel speed. In this paper,
we focus on movement-level delay estimation, which can be

III. Literature Review
A. Traffic Delay Estimation Methods
1) Analytical Model for Delay Estimation: Analytical mod-
els for traffic delay estimation predict vehicle delays at inter-
sections using mathematical formulations grounded in traffic
theory. For instance, Webster’s delay model [7] estimates


IEEE
3
average control delay by relating the signal cycle length,
effective green time, and traffic arrival rate, assuming uniform
vehicle arrivals and computing delay as the time vehicles
spend waiting at red lights. Building on this, Miller’s model
[29] incorporated the effects of non-uniform and fluctuating
arrivals, with the aim of improving applicability under re-
alistic traffic conditions. Akçelik’s model [3] extends signal-
delay estimation to oversaturated and time-dependent queueing
conditions, improving its applicability under high demand.
Similarly, the Highway Capacity Manual (HCM) control delay
model [8] decomposes total delay into uniform delay due to
regular signal stops, random delay arising from stochastic vari-
ations in vehicle arrivals, and overflow delay caused by queues
exceeding the green time. Each component is calculated using
traffic volume, saturation flow, and signal timing, enabling a
detailed representation of intersection performance. Although
these methods are grounded in traffic theory and offer strong
interpretability, their simplified model structures often struggle
to provide accurate predictions when faced with complex and
dynamically changing traffic conditions [30].
2) Data-Driven Models for Delay Estimation: Data-driven
methods are widely used in traffic forecasting, where historical
observations across road networks are used to predict future
flow, speed, or demand. Recent graph-based approaches model
complex spatiotemporal dependencies through space-specific
and multi-view graph learning [31]–[33], temporal-identity
encoding and multiscale interaction [34]–[36], and decoupled
dynamic graph learning [37], [38]. Related data-driven studies
also use multi-representation road-network features for tra-
jectory recovery [39] and station-aware graph attention for
cellular traffic prediction [40]. These studies demonstrate the
flexibility of graph-based representation learning, but they do
not derive explicit traffic-delay equations. Data-driven tech-
niques have also been applied to signalized-intersection delay
estimation. Murat [11] and Preethi et al. [41] develop artificial
neural network (ANN) models using traffic demand, signal
timing, geometric characteristics, and operating conditions.
Bagdatli and Dokuz [42] and Ranpura et al. [30] investigate
machine-learning methods for signalized-intersection delay
estimation. Korkmaz et al. [43], [44] adopt a semi-empirical
approach, using heuristic algorithms to calibrate predefined
linear, exponential, and quadratic delay functions based on the
green ratio and degree of saturation.
Existing data-driven approaches exhibit a trade-off between
modeling flexibility and structural transparency. Graph-based
forecasting and machine-learning delay models can learn com-
plex relationships but encode them implicitly. Semi-empirical
delay models yield explicit equations but confine learning
to predefined functional forms. Consequently, methods that
discover explicit delay-equation structures directly from data
remain limited.
B. Knowledge-Guided Model Discovery
1) Symbolic Regression and Knowledge-Guided Search:
SR discovers explicit mathematical expressions directly from
data, making the learned relationships inspectable [45]. Tra-
ditional SR searches expression structures through genetic
programming, while deep SR uses learned generative models
[46]. In both cases, the search space grows rapidly with the
available variables and operators [14], [21]. Accuracy and
expression complexity alone do not ensure consistency with
known domain relationships. Knowledge-guided SR addresses
this problem through grammar rules [17], dimensional con-
straints [18], shape constraints [19], or formal verification
[20]. Related structured-learning studies encode knowledge in
model representations through ontology-enhanced subgraphs,
temporal graph–text alignment, and relation-guided multi-
modal fusion [47]–[49]. These approaches place knowledge
either in the representation or directly in candidate generation
and evaluation.
2) LLM-Guided Model Discovery:
Recent studies use
LLMs to propose and revise symbolic expressions [24]–
[26]. LLMs also support broader search and optimization:
FunSearch combines LLM generation with external evaluation
to evolve programs [50], while CoE-SAEA uses LLMs to
evolve configuration strategies for surrogate-assisted optimiza-
tion [51]. These studies demonstrate that LLMs can serve
as iterative search operators; their use alone is therefore not
the contribution of CoSyDelay. Knowledge-guided SR con-
strains candidate behavior, while LLM-guided search proposes
and revises expression structures. CoSyDelay integrates both
within a traffic-delay-specific formulation that combines shared
structure discovery, movement-specific coefficient calibration,
and diagnostic feedback from estimation accuracy and traffic-
delay principles.
3) Applications of Symbolic Regression in Traffic Systems:
Applications of SR in traffic systems span prediction, behavior
modeling, and control. For traffic prediction, Li et al. [52] use
genetic programming (GP) with Pareto optimization to derive
highway-speed expressions, while Patelli et al. [53] introduce
lag operators to model temporal dependence in urban traffic
flow. For behavior modeling, Li et al. [54] use neural-network-
based SR to discover explicit car-following relationships. For
signal control, Liu et al. [55] formulate phase switching as an
SR problem and evolve interpretable control rules.
Table I highlights the limitations of existing approaches. An-
alytical and semi-empirical models provide explicit equations
but rely on predefined structures, whereas data-driven models
learn flexible relationships but encode them implicitly. Traffic
SR can discover explicit expressions from data, but existing
applications do not jointly enforce traffic-delay principles and
support multi-granularity delay estimation. Knowledge-guided
SR incorporates domain constraints into symbolic search,
but its existing constraints are not designed specifically for
traffic delay. CoSyDelay combines explicit structure discovery,
traffic-delay knowledge, and diagnostic feedback in one work-
flow. It searches for a shared expression structure and jointly
calibrates its movement-specific coefficients against approach-
delay labels, reducing the complexity of model discovery while
retaining movement-specific behavior. Each candidate is eval-
uated for estimation accuracy and compliance with seven delay
principles, and the accuracy results and diagnosed violations
guide its subsequent revision. The resulting movement-level
estimates are aggregated using traffic-volume weights to obtain
approach- and intersection-level delays.

V. Numerical Experiments
A. Experimental Setup
Experiments use six signalized intersections. Intersections 1
and 2 are simulated standard four-leg intersections (Fig. 4),
with short right-turn lanes at Intersection 2. For Intersec-
tions 3–6, located along Bowen Bridge Road in Queensland,
Australia (Fig. 5), field-collected traffic volumes and signal
timings are used in simulation to obtain the delay labels.
Delay labels measure time loss within the approach detection
area relative to a reference speed determined by road speed
limits and vehicle speed settings. Simulations start from an
empty network and last 4,500 s: 900 s of warm-up and
3,600 s of measurement (900–4,500 s). Only in-window time
loss is counted, including contributions from vehicles entering
during warm-up or remaining at 4,500 s. Vehicles and queues
are retained after warm-up. Simulations are not extended to
clear queues; post-window delay and waiting before network
insertion are excluded. The available delay labels are approach-
level averages. Movement-specific coefficients are therefore
jointly calibrated using approach-level delay labels, and es-
timation accuracy is evaluated at the approach level. Table IV
summarizes their movement configurations and signal phases.
The dataset contains 10,274 samples: 5,909 training samples
for coefficient fitting and evolutionary search, 1,512 validation
samples for final expression selection, and 2,853 test samples
for final evaluation. All methods use the same fixed splits.

B. Estimation Accuracy Comparison with Baseline Methods
We compare CoSyDelay with two groups of baselines. The
first group contains the standard and data-calibrated forms of
three analytical delay models: Webster [7], HCM [8], and
Akçelik [3]. The second group contains data-driven meth-
ods: linear regression, random forest (RF), Extreme Gradient
Boosting (XGBoost) [57], AdaBoost, support vector regres-
sion (SVR), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), graph convolutional
network (GCN), GRANDE [58], ModernNCA [59], Dirichlet
graph auto-encoder (DGAE) [60], and Graph Neural Network
for Urban Interpolation (GNNUI) [61]. All methods are eval-
uated using R2, RMSE, and MAE.
Table V shows that CoSyDelay achieves the best results
at all six intersections and on all three metrics, with an
average rank of 1.00. The Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the
paired differences further show that it significantly outperforms
every standard and data-calibrated analytical model in all 18
intersection–metric comparisons.
Table VI shows that CoSyDelay remains competitive with
flexible data-driven methods. It has the second-best average
rank (3.33) among the 12 methods, achieves the best results on
all three metrics at I1 and I5, and obtains the highest R2 at I4.
The paired tests show that CoSyDelay is significantly better
than XGBoost in 10 of the 18 intersection–metric compar-
isons, statistically indistinguishable in four, and significantly
worse in four. It also significantly outperforms AdaBoost in
all 18 comparisons and linear regression, KNN, and GCN in
16, 16, and 15 comparisons, respectively. CoSyDelay therefore
maintains competitive estimation accuracy while providing an
explicit delay equation evaluated against traffic-delay princi-
ples.

C. Generalization Analysis
We evaluate CoSyDelay’s generalization ability under over-
saturated demand and across intersections, and further assess
its utility for offline signal-timing optimization at new simu-
lated intersections.
1) Delay-Estimation Generalization: Delay-estimation gen-
eralization is evaluated in two settings. First, each inter-
section’s learned expression and coefficients are fixed and
evaluated on 3,644 additional simulated samples under over-
saturated conditions (degree of saturation X = y/λ ≥1.00).
These samples are not used for model development. As shown
in Table IX, CoSyDelay ranks first, with mean R2 = 0.841,
RMSE = 3.754 s, and MAE = 2.630 s, showing that it
maintains strong estimation performance under the tested over-
saturated regime. Second, cross-intersection structure transfer
is evaluated over all 30 ordered source–target pairs among I1–
I6. For each pair, the expression structure discovered at the
source intersection is transferred unchanged, its coefficients
are recalibrated using the target-intersection training data,
and the resulting model is evaluated on the target test set.
CoSyDelay again ranks first, attaining mean R2 = 0.856,
RMSE = 4.477 s, and MAE = 2.937 s. These results show
that the discovered expression structures remain effective after
target-specific coefficient calibration.
2) Downstream Signal-Timing Optimization: We further
test whether the fixed CoSyDelay model learned at I1 can
support offline signal-timing decisions at new intersections.
Its expression and coefficients are applied unchanged to three
newly constructed intersection scenarios in Simulation of
Urban MObility (SUMO) [62]: Beijing Gaojiaoyuan, Chengdu
Guanghua, and Tianjin Zhijingdao. Each scenario is tested
under five traffic regimes. For each intersection–regime combi-
nation, each delay model selects the candidate timing plan with
the lowest estimated delay, and the selected plan is simulated
using 20 random seeds. CoSyDelay is compared with the stan-
dard and optimized variants of Webster, HCM, and Akçelik;
none of the models is calibrated on the three new scenarios.
Table X shows that CoSyDelay produces the lowest delay
at all three intersections. Its overall mean delay is 93.753 s,
compared with 119.507 s for the standard analytical models
and 172.195 s for their optimized variants, corresponding to
reductions of 21.5% and 45.6%, respectively. These results
demonstrate that CoSyDelay can support effective offline
timing-plan selection in the tested scenarios.

E. Effectiveness and Sensitivity of LLM-Guided Search
We assess the LLM-guided search by varying four factors:
the LLM backend, the principle-informed prompt and eval-
uator, generation temperature, and the generation–population
setting. Each setting is evaluated over 20 independent runs at
every intersection. Table XIII reports test Fitness.
First, we test five LLM backends under identical settings. The
results are backend-sensitive: GPT-4.1-mini and Gemini-2.5-
Flash obtain the two best average ranks of 1.667 and 2.000,
respectively.
Second, we test the effects of the principle-informed prompt
and external evaluator. The principles specify only general
requirements for a reasonable delay formula; they do not pro-
vide its mathematical form or coefficient values. We compare
four configurations: neither component, prompt only, evaluator
only, and both components. Table XIII shows that using both
produces the highest Fitness at all six intersections. Evaluator
only also clearly outperforms prompt only, demonstrating the
additional value of explicit verification beyond stating the
principles in the prompt.
Third, we test temperatures of 0.0, 0.3, and 0.7 with
the principle instruction enabled or disabled. The instruction
improves Fitness at every temperature. With the instruction
enabled, no statistically significant differences are detected
among the three temperatures.
Finally, we test nine generation–population combinations.

VI. Conclusion
Traffic delay estimation remains a fundamental yet chal-
lenging problem in intelligent transportation systems. This
paper presented CoSyDelay, a cognitive symbolic regression
framework for trustworthy delay modeling at signalized inter-
sections. CoSyDelay evolves generalized symbolic expressions
at the turning-movement level and calibrates them into physi-
cally consistent empirical models, which are then aggregated
to support delay estimation at the approach and intersection
levels. The framework combines domain-informed variable
design, LLM-guided symbolic search, and a composite fitness
function that evaluates both estimation accuracy and physi-
cal consistency. Experiments on six signalized intersections
demonstrate that CoSyDelay achieves competitive delay esti-
mation accuracy relative to analytical and data-driven baselines
while producing physically consistent expressions. The abla-
tion studies further support the use of turning-movement-level
modeling, domain-informed features, and LLM-guided search.
Future work will improve symbolic-search efficiency and
extend CoSyDelay to larger-scale, real-time signal optimiza-
tion. In addition, geometric and queue information will be
incorporated to better capture interactions between turning
movements.
