Beyond Foundation Models: PFN Priors
Improve Tabular Hyperparameter
Optimization
Artem Chistyakov*,1,2, Renat Sergazinov*,2, Artem Babenko2,1
1HSE University, 2Yandex
Abstract
Synthetic priors have so far served one purpose: pretraining tabular foundation
models. We ask whether the same priors can improve conventional tabular models,
which are trained from scratch on each dataset. We propose PriorTune, a method
that meta-tunes hyperparameter configurations on synthetic tasks alone. Offline,
for a given model, we fit a pool of configurations on datasets sampled from a
published PFN prior and greedily select the subset with the highest winrate over the
random baseline. Online, a new dataset fits only the selected configurations, so the
gain comes at no extra cost. Empirically, we observe that scaling both the number of
configurations and synthetic datasets has an equal scaling effect on the performance
of the final subset of configurations, which we also explain theoretically. On the full
BeyondArena benchmark, LightGBM with TabPrep features with our configurations
ranks above TabPFN-3 and RealMLP, while being much more efficient at inference.
Overall, our results establish existing PFN priors as a scalable source of meta-
training data for hyperparameter selection for non-foundational tabular models.

1. Introduction
Synthetic data drives the development of tabular foundation models. Since TabPFNv1 [1],
every generation has come with a richer prior: TabPFNv2 added new edge types to the
causal graph sampler [2], TabICLv2 added heavy-tailed random graphs and a filter on the
generated datasets [3], and TabPFN-3 scaled the generated datasets to a million rows [4].
To date, these priors have mostly served one purpose: pretraining PFNs.
Conventional models, which are trained from scratch on each dataset, have no direct way
to use these priors. For such models, e.g. GBDTs and DNNs, hyperparameter selection is
the natural way for transferring knowledge across tasks. TabRepo demonstrates that a set
of hyperparameter configurations selected on approximately 200 real datasets transfers
effectively to unseen datasets [5]. Similarly, RealMLP identifies strong MLP architecture and
training defaults through meta-tuning on 118 real datasets [6]. Both rely on a small, fixed
collection of real datasets, which caps the scale and diversity of the meta-training tasks.
This raises a natural question: can datasets sampled from a PFN prior surpass real datasets
when learning transferable sets of hyperparameter configurations? To test this, we sample
4000 synthetic datasets from TabICLv2 prior, evaluate 4000 hyperparameter configurations
on them, and propose a new algorithm to select K best-performing configurations. On a
new dataset, we only train the selected subset of K configurations, so the meta-tuning gains
transfer for free.

We study four efficient tabular models: LightGBM [7], LightGBM with TabPrep [8], an
MLP with cosine embeddings [9], and TabPack [10]. These four models are near the
efficiency-performance Pareto frontier (Figure 1). We use the TabICLv2 prior, with TabPFNv1
and TabICLv1 in ablations, and evaluate transfer on TabArena’s 51 IID datasets [11] and
BeyondArena’s 142 datasets with diverse splitting protocols [12].
As a source of meta-training tasks, synthetic priors have three advantages over real datasets.
First, supply is unlimited and quality is controllable. Real repositories plateau: on TabRepo’s
182 datasets, adding configurations stops helping, whereas on synthetic tasks neither the
number of tasks nor the number of configurations has saturated at 4,000 (Section 5).
Second, synthetic priors can be continuously improved: foundational model have shown that
improving priors yields better model performance. Third, synthetic tasks rule out test-set
contamination by construction. Real data needs an overlap audit: for TabArena, we removed
18 of the 200 TabRepo datasets.
The contributions of this paper are as follows:
1. Meta-tuning hyperparameters on synthetic data. We show that existing PFN priors
can improve conventional tabular models through offline hyperparameter selection.
Per dataset, real tasks help more, but synthetic tasks scale: given equal budget (config-
urations × datasets), hyperparameters selected on 4000 synthetic datasets outperform
those selected on 182 real TabRepo datasets for every model and every K.
2. Improved tuning on real datasets. On both TabArena and BeyondArena, the selected
sets of configurations outperform random sets of the same size. On TabArena, they also
outperform random sets of any size, so meta-selection improves both the quality and
the efficiency of downstream tuning. On BeyondArena, our LightGBM-TabPrep ranks
7th of 58 methods, ahead of TabPFN-3 and behind only recent foundation models.
3. An analysis of scaling and prior choice. We investigate how the quality of the
configuration set depends on the number of synthetic tasks, the candidate pool size,
and the generating prior, and derive a transfer bound and a record-count argument that
motivate the observed scaling forms. Scaling number of datasets and configurations
keeps paying off at 4,000, whereas the choice of prior appears secondary: even the
TabPFNv1 prior is competitive with TabICLv2.

2. Related Work
Transfer of hyperparameter configurations. Good hyperparameter configurations typ-
ically transfer across datasets. Feurer et al. [13] warm-start Bayesian optimization with
configurations that performed well on similar datasets. Later work drops the similarity
and learns one fixed set for all datasets: given a table of scores for every configuration on
every dataset, greedily add the configuration that most improves the average best-member
score over datasets [14–16]. TabRepo scales this offline selection to 1,310 configurations
on 200 datasets, and AutoGluon 1.0 ships the learned set as its default [5, 17]. RealMLP
meta-learns several model components (e.g. preprocessing, activations) on 118 datasets [6].
All of this work is bounded by the supply of real data, 25 to 208 datasets. TabRepo reports
a saturating effect around 150 datasets or configurations. We keep the greedy forward
selection, but run it on synthetic tasks, whose supply is unlimited, and change the scoring
function (Section 3.4).
A second line transfers knowledge into the optimizer rather than into a set of configurations.
One line of work fits the Gaussian-process prior on evaluations from previous tasks [18–22].
OptFormer pre-trains a text transformer on optimization logs [23]. Recent works such as
PFNs4BO and its successors, pretrain PFNs on synthetic optimization tasks [24–27]. All of
these search sequentially and need the surrogate model at tuning time. Our configuration
set is built once offline and can be evaluated in parallel.
Synthetic priors for tabular data. Synthetic priors are the engine of tabular foundation
models. Among others, the recent TabPFN, TabICL, Mitra, LimiX, Xiaomi-TabLDM, Causilo,
and EXAONE Tabular are pretrained on synthetic tables alone [1–4, 28–33]. A typical
synthetic prior has three parts: a directed graph over variables, an edge function that
maps parents to a child, and an observation process. Graphs are random DAGs in TabPFN,
Cauchy random graphs with varied connectivity in TabICLv2, and hierarchical causal models
in LimiX. Edge functions started as small neural networks and grew to include decision
trees and discretization in TabPFNv2, tree ensembles in TabICL, and Gaussian processes,
polynomials, and products in TabICLv2 [2, 33]. Related in spirit, recent work distills a
PFN into a tree ensemble or an MLP on a given dataset [34, 35]. However, distillation
requires PFN during the training phase and has yet to demonstrate significant performance
improvements.

3.1 Setup
Notation. We consider regression and classification on tabular data. We denote the synthetic
data generator by Q and a dataset by D. The loss ℓis the native metric of the task, oriented
so that lower is better: RMSE for regression, log-loss for multiclass, and 1−ROC-AUC for
binary classification, as in TabArena and BeyondArena. We split each dataset into train,
validation, and test subsets Dtrain, Dval, Dtest.
Benchmarks. We use TabArena [11] and BeyondArena [12], which together cover 155
to 1.3 million rows, 3 to 22,215 features, and i.i.d., grouped, and temporal splits. Table 2
summarizes both suites. The main results (Section 4) use the full BeyondArena with every
outer train-test split. The ablations of Section 5 use TabArena-Lite, the first train-test split.
Each split has up to 8 inner train-validation splits. No benchmark data enters the selection.
3.2 Synthetic data generation
For all main experiments, we sample tasks from the TabICLv2 prior [3]. Three settings of
the prior matter most, and we keep them close to the third pretraining stage of TabICLv2.
Dataset size is log-uniform between 400 and 60,000 rows. The feature count is uniform
between 1 and a cap that falls from 100 to 20 as the row count grows. The sampled datasets
are filtered using both of the available filters: reject if the target node of the graph shares
no ancestors with feature nodes, and reject if ExtraTrees cannot beat the constant predictor.
We fix the task mix at 2,000 regression, 1,400 binary, and 600 multiclass datasets with 3
to 10 classes, and split each dataset 64/16/20 into train, validation, and test, stratified for
classification. Appendix A.3 lists all settings.

3.3 Models
For our main runs, we use LightGBM [7], LightGBM with TabPrep [8], MLP with cosine
embeddings [9], and TabPack [10]. We select these models for their tradeoff between
training and inference speed and performance (Figure 1). Each configuration in the pool C
with C = 4,000 is an independent draw from the model’s published search space, which
we list in Appendix A.2. TabPack is a packed ensemble of MLPs, so its unit of sampling is a
single MLP configuration: it shares the pool of C = 4,000 MLP configurations, from which
we select its K = 64 members.
3.4 Subset selection
The selection mimics the deployment rule and scores against random subsets of the same
size. Following TabArena and BeyondArena, we distinguish two deployment rules: tuned
(T), where the validation-best member is deployed, and tuned and ensembled (T+E), where
validation builds a greedy ensemble [36]. We implement the whole procedure as a greedy
forward selection algorithm: at each iteration starting from an empty set, we add a configu-
ration, score it under the given deployment rule, and select the one that yields the highest
winrate against the random subset (Algorithm 1). Appendix B states the objective formally.
Below we motivate each choice on a small example (see Section 5.1 for ablation).
Table 1 gives a toy instance under the tuned rule: four configurations A to D, three synthetic
tasks D1 to D3 for selection, one held-out task D4 standing for a real dataset, and a subset
size of K = 2.
Selecting on validation, scoring on test. Under both deployment rules, the model (or
ensemble) is selected on the validation split and scored on the test split. Our objective mimics
this, which selects configurations that are stable across validation and test. The alternative,
selecting on test directly or on a single out-of-fold score as TabRepo and AutoGluon do
[5], hides that instability. In Table 1, A and D are unstable: their validation and test losses
disagree on most datasets, whereas B and C are stable. Both selections pick B first, then
diverge: test-only selection returns {B, A}, validation-then-test returns the stable {B, C}.
On the held-out D4, {B, A} deploys A and loses; {B, C} deploys C and wins.
Comparing against the random baseline. The alternative is to use losses directly: TabRepo
adds the configuration that most lowers the mean error of the best member over datasets
[5]. Raw losses mix metrics and scales across tasks. In Table 1, any rule on raw losses selects

5.1 Selection rule
Figure 4 ablates the choices of Section 3.4. To avoid potential data leak, we evaluate
out-of-fold on synthetic tasks: randomly select 3,000 datasets, deploy under T on the other
1,000, repeat 200 times. All rules choose from the same 4,000 configurations and are scored
against the same random-K baselines (Appendix A.1.2.1).
Comparing to TabRepo. The TabRepo algorithm scores the best member of a subset directly,
with no validation-then-test step, so we give it one score per task on the joined validation and
test rows. We replace the raw loss with TabRepo’s normalized error, (ℓ−ℓbest)/(ℓmedian−ℓbest)
clipped to [0, 1] per task [5], which removes the metric scale. From the empty set, each step
adds the configuration that minimizes the mean over tasks of the best normalized error
in the subset. It trails R = 16 for every model and K, by 0.010 to 0.038: even with the
scale gone, the loss shape steers the selection into suboptimal regions. A naive improvement
is to count instead the tasks on which a candidate improves the best member (i.e., a win
rate against the current subset). The improved version beats the base in all nine cases,
by 0.004 to 0.017. However, it still trails R = 16 by 0.014 to 0.024 for LightGBM and
LightGBM-TabPrep, where scoring the best member hides configurations whose validation
rank does not hold on test, and ties it on the MLP (−0.003 to +0.003).
Resampling validation and references. The plain hill-climb, with one validation split
and one reference, trails R = 16 by 0.014 to 0.031. If we resample (16 times) just the
validation, we close 60 to 84% of the gap. If we resample just the random references, we
close 41 to 62%. Both matter, but validation noise appears to matter more. The win rate
rises monotonically in R for every model and K: by 0.015 to 0.030 from R = 1 to R = 16,
most at K = 200.

5.3 Meta-tuning versus more random search
A cheaper alternative to meta-tuning is to fit more random configurations. Figure 6 scores
random subsets of size K′ against the random-K baseline (details in Appendix A.1.2.3).
The mean curve rises, peaks at 0.50 to 0.53 for K′ between 500 and 750, and falls to 0.49
to 0.51 at K′ = 4,000: larger random subsets overfit the validation split. At K = 25 and 64,
the mean curve peaks higher, at 0.51 to 0.60, but the meta-tuned subset still sits at least
0.07 above it (Appendix A.4). For a practitioner with a fixed budget, the 200 meta-tuned
configurations replace a random search over 4,000 configurations that fits twenty times
more models and still scores lower on average.
5.4 Choice of prior
We repeat the selection on synthetic tasks from the TabICLv1 [28] and TabPFNv1 [1]
priors (Appendix A.3), with 2,000 configurations on 2,000 datasets for LightGBM, LightGBM-
TabPrep, and the MLP (Figure 7). With all 2,000 configurations and tasks, the prior matters
most for plain LightGBM: TabICLv2 leads at every K, by up to 0.09 over TabPFNv1 (0.644
against 0.551 at K = 25). With TabPrep, the three priors are within 0.02 of each other at
every K. For the MLP, TabICLv2 wins at K = 25 and 64 and TabPFNv1 at K = 200. No prior
wins everywhere, and the prior of TabPFNv1 appears to be competitive. We use TabICLv2,
the newest prior, for the main experiments.

6. Conclusion
We have shown that the synthetic priors built for PFN pretraining are a scalable source
of meta-training tasks for conventional tabular models. PriorTune selects a subset of
hyperparameter configurations on synthetic tasks alone, by win rate against random subsets
under the deployment rule. We show that when scaled the synthetic tasks beat real datasets
of TabRepo at equal budgets. The selected subsets beat random subsets of any size, improve
LightGBM, LightGBM-TabPrep, the MLP, and TabPack on the TabArena and BeyondArena
benchmarks, and make LightGBM-TabPrep the highest-ranked model in Elo on BeyondArena
that is not a pretrained foundation model, above TabPFN-3 and RealMLP, at lower latency
than both. The released subsets are drop-in search spaces: a practitioner replaces random
search over the published space with the K selected configurations.

7. Limitations
We do not apply PriorTune to tabular foundation models. They are pretrained on tasks
from the same kind of prior, so the selection can prefer configurations that change them the
least. Newer priors do not always help PriorTune: the newest prior helps plain LightGBM
most but gives no clear gain for LightGBM-TabPrep and the MLP (Section 5.4). The grid
is fitted once per model and search space, so a new search space needs a new grid. The
synthetic tasks have at most 60,000 rows and 100 features, so the selection does not see
tasks as large as the largest real datasets. The ablations use the tuned rule only, because
selection for ensembles is expensive. The error bars measure variation across repetitions,
not uncertainty over new datasets. Our theory motivates the forms of the scaling curves but
does not prove them; the fits are empirical.

A.1.2.1
Selection rule (Section 5.1)
Protocol. We run 200 repetitions. Each splits the 4,000 synthetic tasks at random into 3,000
selection tasks and 1,000 held-out tasks. Every rule selects K out of 4,000 configurations on
the selection tasks and is deployed under T on the held-out tasks. There it is compared with
200 random-K baselines, and its win rate is averaged over them. All rules in a repetition
use the same tasks and baselines. Error bars in Figure 4 are ±1.96 standard errors of the
win rate over the 200 repetitions.
Our rule. It draws, once per repetition, 16 bootstrap resamples of each task’s validation
rows and 16 random-K references Rr. R < 16 pairs the first R resamples with the first
R references. Validation only pairs the 16 resamples with R1, and reference only pairs the
original validation split with the 16 references.
TabRepo algorithms. They use no validation resamples and no references. They score the
best member of a subset on each dataset’s joined validation and test rows, with the native
18


metric recomputed on the union. The first version adds, at each step, the configuration that
minimizes the mean over datasets of the best normalized error, (ℓ−ℓbest)/(ℓmedian −ℓbest)
clipped to [0, 1], with ℓbest and ℓmedian taken over the 4,000 configurations. The second
starts from the configuration with the lowest mean rank, then adds the one that improves
the best member on the most tasks. Both break ties by a random configuration order drawn
per repetition.
A.1.2.2
Synthetic versus real meta-training data (Section 5.2)
Cells. A cell (C, N) selects K of C configurations on N meta-training tasks by Algorithm 1
with R = 16. Synthetic cells use our pool of 4,000 configurations and 4,000 synthetic tasks.
TabRepo cells use the 182 TabRepo datasets and a bank of 16,000 configurations: our pool
of 4,000 plus 12,000 further draws from the same search spaces, fitted on TabRepo and
TabArena. Validation resamples are drawn once per task and shared across repetitions. Ties
in validation selection and in the greedy step go to the lowest configuration id.
Repetitions. We run 2,000 repetitions, shared by all cells. A repetition permutes the configu-
rations and the tasks once; each cell takes the first C configurations and N tasks of that
permutation.
Evaluation. The selected subset is deployed on TabArena under T and compared with the
first K configurations of the repetition’s permutation of our 4,000-configuration pool. Both
synthetic and TabRepo cells share this baseline. Also both synthetic and TabRepo C = K
cells therefore have 0.5 by construction.
Reported values. Each win rate is the mean over the 2,000 repetitions, its standard error is
below 0.003 in every cell and is not drawn. It measures variation across repetitions on the
fixed benchmark, not uncertainty over new datasets. In Figure 5, each cell (C, N) is a dot
at its budget B = C · N, so the dots above one B are all cells with that budget. The line
joins the best cells within each B.
A.1.2.3
Meta-tuning versus more random search (Section 5.3)
Meta-tuned lines. The synthetic line is the cell C = N = 4,000 of Appendix A.1.2.2. The
TabRepo line selects on all 182 TabRepo datasets from our pool of 4,000 configurations,
with the same protocol, repetitions, and baselines. Each line is the mean win rate over the
2,000 repetitions.
Random subsets. For every K′, we draw 200 subsets of K′ configurations uniformly from the
16,000-configuration bank, independently across K′, and deploy each under T on TabArena.
Each subset is compared with all 2,000 random-K baselines of the meta-tuned lines, and its
win rate is averaged over them. The band therefore reflects only the draw of the random
subset: we report the mean over the 200 subsets and the 95% CI band.

Distributions. For each hyperparameter, the sampling distribution F is given in Appendix A.2.
For a continuous hyperparameter, we plot the density of the selected values divided by the
sampling density, fsel(x)/f(x). The sampling distribution is then flat at 1, and a value above
1 is selected more often than it is sampled from the prior. We estimate this ratio with a
Gaussian kernel (Scott bandwidth) and plot it as the coloured line. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov
(KS) distance is supx | ˆFsel(x) −F(x)|, where ˆFsel is the empirical distribution function of the
selected values. It is in [0, 1]: 0 when the selected values follow the sampling distribution
exactly, and larger when they move away from it. Categorical hyperparameters are shown
as shares.

Every pool is a set of independent random draws from the search space below; no con-

LightGBM. We use the LightGBM search space of TabArena [11], shown in Table 6. Training

validations, without TabPack’s online ensemble.

A.3.1 Graph structure
TabICLv2. A random Cauchy DAG with LogInt(2, 32) nodes. The probability of an edge
from node i to node j is σ(A + Bi + Cj), where A, Bi, Cj are independent standard Cauchy
variables: A sets the overall density, Bi and Cj the out- and in-degree of each node. Features
and the target are assigned to random nodes. A graph is resampled until the feature nodes
and the target node share an ancestor.
TabICLv1. A layered MLP graph as in TabPFNv1: num_layers layers of width hidden_dim,
both drawn from log-scaled truncated normals with per-dataset means in [1, 6] and [5, 130],
with block-wise or Bernoulli dropout of edges. In causal mode (sampled per dataset) features
and target are read from hidden activations; otherwise the root causes are the features and
the last layer is the target.
TabPFNv1. The same layered MLP graph, with layer count and width from gamma meta-
distributions (means up to 6 and 300), num_causes root variables, edge dropout, and the
same causal or non-causal read-out. With probability 1/(1 + ew), w ∼U(2, 10), the dataset
is instead a draw from an RBF Gaussian process on Gaussian inputs [1].
A.3.2 Edge functions
TabICLv2. Eight function types: MLP, tree ensemble (symmetric trees, LogInt(1, 128) trees
of depth 1 to 7), discretization to random centers, Gaussian process with a random kernel,
linear, quadratic, EM-style cluster assignment, and product. The first three follow TabPFNv2,
and the other five are new. With several parents, the prior either concatenates them and
applies one function, or applies a function per parent and aggregates by sum, product, max,
or log-sum-exp. Each node output is standardized, rescaled by a random feature-importance
vector, and multiplied by a random node importance.
TabICLv1. With probability 0.7 an MLP layer: a random affine map followed by an activation
drawn from 19 fixed functions (Identity, Tanh, LeakyReLU, ELU, ReLU, ReLU6, SELU, SiLU,
Softplus, Hardtanh, sign, sine, RBF, exponential,
p
|x|, 1|x|≤1, x2, |x|) or a random Fourier-
feature function. Activations may differ between layers. With probability 0.3 a tree layer:
24


an XGBoost multi-output regressor with at most 4 trees of depth at most 4, fitted on
standard-normal targets and evaluated on the parent values.
TabPFNv1. A random affine map followed by one activation per dataset, shared by all
layers, drawn from Tanh, Identity, or ReLU.
A.3.3 Transformations and filters
TabICLv2. Each scalar feature is min-max scaled and then warped by a Kumaraswamy
function, with exponents drawn from LogNum(0.2, 5). Categorical features come from
discretizing sub-vectors of node values into 2 to 9 categories. Outliers are clamped at 4
standard deviations, and standardized values are clipped to [−100, 100]. Constant columns
are removed, and the feature order and class labels are permuted. Two filters reject datasets.
The ancestor filter rejects a graph whose target shares no ancestor with the features. The
ExtraTrees test fits 25 trees of depth 6 with bootstrapping and keeps a dataset only if their
out-of-bag error beats the mean predictor in at least 95% of 200 bootstrap resamples.
TabICLv1. Gaussian noise is added after every layer, with a standard deviation drawn per
dataset from LogNum(10−4, 0.3). Root causes come from a normal, a uniform, or a mixed
distribution. With probability 0.2, a dataset has categorical features. Each column then
becomes categorical independently, with a uniformly drawn probability, and its values are
binned by rank into max(2, round(Γ(1, 10))) categories. Outliers are clamped at 4 standard
deviations, and the features and the regression target are standardized. Class labels are
permuted. The original prior applies no filter. We apply the ExtraTrees test of TabICLv2, so
that all three corpora are filtered alike.
TabPFNv1. Gaussian noise is added at every node, with a mean and a standard deviation
drawn per node. The standard deviation comes from LogNum(10−4, 0.3). Categorical
features are generated as in TabICLv1: with probability 0.2, binned by rank into Γ(1, 10)
categories. Outliers are removed, and the features and the target are z-normalized. Features
are rescaled by the fraction of columns in use. For classification, class boundaries are
sampled from the data, and class labels are permuted. The original prior applies no filter.
We apply the ExtraTrees test of TabICLv2.
