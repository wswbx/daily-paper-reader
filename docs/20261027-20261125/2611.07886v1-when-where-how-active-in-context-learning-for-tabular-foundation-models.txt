When, Where, How: Active In-Context Learning for Tabular Foundation Models
Christian M. M. Frey 1
Abstract
Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) adapt to a
new task only through the labeled rows in their
context. However, labels are expensive to acquire
in real-world scenarios due to the need for highly
specific domain knowledge to ensure high-quality
data. Active in-context learning transfers classi-
cal acquisition rules to this setting, in which the
model that guides acquisition changes with every
label added. We ask how the value of diversity,
predictive uncertainty and target-aware informa-
tion gain changes as a frozen TFM acquires more
context. For that, we study Target-Aware Nearest
Neighbor (TANN) acquisition and the Expected
Predictive Information Gain in Context (EPIC)
and evaluate them against classical rules. Our
study across twenty benchmarks with two well-
established TFMs and classical GBDTs highlights
three findings: (i) When: diversity-based acquisi-
tion is best during the first acquisition iterations;
(ii) Where: labeling pool rows that serve the target
inputs via a simple target-aware nearest-neighbor
rule matters substantially; (iii) How: EPIC yields
the best models at the label budget, retains on av-
erage 80–87% of the chance-corrected full-data
performance with only 5–10% of the pool labeled
on the eight benchmarks with the largest pools,
and selects labels that also improve the predictive
performance of gradient-boosted decision trees.
1. Introduction
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) like TabPFN (Hollmann
et al., 2023; 2025) and TabICL (Qu et al., 2025; 2026),
pre-trained on a large corpus of synthetic tasks, solve new
tasks by in-context learning (Dong et al., 2024). The frozen
models are given labeled rows together with the query rows
and return predictions in a single forward pass. The labeled

rows are therefore the only information that adapts a TFM
to a new task, and they have a decisive influence on its
predictive performance. This, however, presupposes access
to labeled data that can be fed to the model. In many real-
world applications, labels are the bottleneck (Chai et al.,
2022; Tharwat & Schenck, 2023; Li et al., 2023): they
are the costly resource, as they require expert annotation,
measurement or time, while unlabeled data are abundant.
The choice of the rows to label is therefore a central lever
and a question in its own right.
Recently, active in-context learning (AICL) has emerged
as a paradigm to dynamically grow the context of a frozen
TFM from an unlabeled pool. Tab-AICL (Treerath & Pit-
torino, 2026) transfers conventional acquisition like margin-
, core-set- and hybrid-based rules (Settles, 2009; Sener &
Savarese, 2018) from classical active learning to TFMs. A
key finding is that no rule dominates across datasets and that
the gains are concentrated in the cold start. Their analysis
also makes the assumption that the rules applied remain un-
changed throughout acquisition, yet a frozen TFM changes
with every acquired label. The quantities that acquisition
rules rely on, the model’s uncertainty and its view of which
rows are similar, change accordingly as more labels are ac-
quired. Moreover, existing rules score a candidate by its
own uncertainty or by its distance to the labeled set, and
ignore where the model will be used. In classical active
learning, the dependence of the best strategy on the budget
has been documented (Hacohen et al., 2022), and prediction-
oriented criteria have been proposed that target the inputs
of interest (Yu et al., 2006; Smith et al., 2023); for frozen
TFMs, neither aspect has been examined.
We therefore ask: how does the value of diversity, predictive
uncertainty and target-aware information gain change as a
frozen tabular foundation model acquires more context? An-
swering this question requires measuring the value of a label
for the predictions that are actually required. For conven-
tional active learning approaches, this value, the expected
predictive information gain (EPIG) (Smith et al., 2023),
requires a posterior over parameters and refitting on hypo-
thetical data. A TFM removes both obstacles: its prediction
already approximates a posterior predictive distribution, and
conditioning it on a hypothetical label amounts to appending
one row to its context. We introduce the expected predictive
information gain in context (EPIC), which is assessed on

2. Related Work
Tabular foundation models. TabPFN (Hollmann et al.,
2023) established prior-data fitted networks for small classi-
fication problems, and its successor (Hollmann et al., 2025)
extended it to regression, larger tables and heterogeneous
features. TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) introduced a three-stage
architecture of column-wise embedding, row-wise inter-
action and dataset-wise in-context learning that scales in-
context learning to substantially larger tables, and TabI-
CLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) further improved its accuracy and
efficiency. Common to these models is that labeled data
enter only through the context, which has two consequences
that our work builds on: adapting the model to new labels
requires no training, and the model’s response to a hypo-
thetical label can be obtained by a forward pass.
Active learning. When labels are expensive, active learning
chooses which instances to label (Settles, 2009). Uncer-
tainty sampling queries the instances on which the current
model is least confident, for example by the margin between
the two most probable classes or by predictive entropy, and
diversity-based rules such as core-set selection (Sener &
Savarese, 2018) cover the input space with a small set of
representatives; hybrid rules combine both. These criteria
are proxies: they describe the candidate, not the effect its
label would have on the model, and they do not depend on
where predictions will be required. Transductive experimen-
tal design (Yu et al., 2006) addresses the latter by choosing
the instances that best represent the unlabeled data on which
predictions are needed. Classical active learning further
retrains the model after every batch, which is costly and,
with few labels, prone to an unstable cold start.
Acquisition depends on the budget. Which of these cri-
teria works best depends on how many labels are available.
Hacohen et al. (2022) show that representative, typical ex-
amples are most useful when labels are scarce, whereas
uncertain, atypical examples become more useful as the
budget grows, and propose a typicality-based rule for the
low-budget regime; the cold start of uncertainty sampling,
whose estimates are unreliable while the model is poorly
trained, is a recurring theme of the same literature (Settles,
2009). For a frozen TFM the learner is never trained, and
what changes with the budget is only the context it condi-
tions on. The context’s value of diversity and uncertainty
w.r.t. its size has not been studied in this regime.
Target-aware and information-based acquisition. A prin-
cipled alternative measures the value of a label by the infor-
mation it provides. Lindley (1956) defined the information
of an experiment as the expected reduction of uncertainty
about the quantity of interest, and MacKay (1992) derived
information-based objectives for active data selection, distin-
guishing information about the model parameters from infor-
mation about predictions at chosen inputs. BALD (Houlsby
et al., 2011) selects by the mutual information between a la-
bel and the model parameters. Prediction-oriented Bayesian
active learning (Smith et al., 2023) argues that parameter
information can be spent on aspects of the model that are
irrelevant to the predictions of interest and introduces the
expected predictive information gain (EPIG), the mutual
information between the label of a candidate and the label
of a target input drawn from the deployment distribution.
Context restriction and context selection.

Active in-context learning and our position. Active in-
context learning combines a frozen model, as in context
selection, with label acquisition under a budget, as in active
learning. Tab-AICL (Treerath & Pittorino, 2026) formalizes
this setting for TabPFN, grows the context from one row
per class in batches, and adapts margin, core-set, hybrid and
proxy-filtered rules; the gains over random sampling are
concentrated in the cold-start regime, and no rule dominates
across datasets. Each rule is applied unchanged throughout
acquisition, and none uses the inputs on which the model
will be deployed. Our work addresses both aspects.
We study how the value of diversity, uncertainty and target-
aware information changes as the context grows, extending
the budget dependence observed by Hacohen et al. (2022) to
frozen in-context learners. We show that target awareness,
as in transductive experimental design (Yu et al., 2006),
is a central lever in this setting, already in the form of a
simple nearest-neighbor rule. And we make prediction-
oriented information gain (Smith et al., 2023) computable
for frozen TFMs by replacing the parameter posterior with
in-context conditioning, which requires neither posterior
samples nor retraining. To our knowledge, this is the first
study of acquisition dynamics and target-aware acquisition
for frozen tabular foundation models.
3. Method
We consider active in-context learning with a frozen TFM:
labels are bought from an oracle under a budget, and the
models are affected only through their context.
To as-
sess the acquisitions’ impact, we compare three families:
diversity-based rules, which spread labels over the input
space; uncertainty-based rules, which label the candidates
the model is least certain about; and target-aware rules,
which label candidates for the sake of the inputs on which
the model will be used. We contribute two target-aware
instruments: TANN (Section 3.2) labels, for the targets on
which the model is least certain, their nearest pool rows; and
EPIC (Section 3.3) scores a candidate by the information
its label is expected to carry about the target predictions.

4. Evaluation
We organize the evaluation around the three findings: When
diversity- and prediction-oriented acquisition are most valu-
able (Section 4.2); Where labels should be acquired (Sec-
tion 4.3); and How EPIC performs as a computation of
prediction-oriented information gain (Section 4.4). Sec-
tion 4.5 discusses the limitations. Further results are re-
ported in Section D.
4.1. Evaluation protocol
Benchmarks. We use the twenty OpenML (Vanschoren
et al., 2014) classification datasets of Tab-AICL (Treerath
& Pittorino, 2026) (details in Section B). They range from
145 to 581,012 rows, from 4 to 94 features and from 2 to
7 classes, and include strongly imbalanced tasks such as
Blood-Transfusion, Bank-Marketing and JM1. Following
Tab-AICL, datasets with more than 10,000 rows are subsam-
pled uniformly to 10,000 rows. For every seed, each dataset
is split into a pool of unlabeled candidates and a held-out
test set by a stratified 70/30 split that is redrawn per seed.
We refer to the eight datasets whose pools contain 1,358 to
7,000 rows as large and to the remaining twelve (101 to 700
rows) as small.
Acquisition protocol. The context is initialized with one
uniformly drawn row per class and grown in batches of
b=10 rows until the budget B=100 is reached, or until half
of the pool has been labeled on the smallest datasets. Every
configuration is repeated with ten seeds, which change the
split, the initial context and all random choices.
Competitors. We compare ten acquisition rules, which dif-
fer only in how the next batch is chosen, and group them into
three families: diversity-based, uncertainty-based and
target-aware. Following Tab-AICL’s rules (Treerath & Pit-
torino, 2026): Random (uniform sampling); Margin (small-
est margin between the two most probable classes); Coreset
(greedy k-center in the standardized feature space (Sener
& Savarese, 2018)); Hybrid (the most uncertain half of the
pool by predictive entropy, then the row nearest to each of
b k-means centroids); and Proxy-Hybrid (a shortlist from
a linear proxy model, then Hybrid on the shortlist). As
Target-aware rules (Section 3.2): TANN labels, for each
of the b target inputs with the smallest margin, its nearest
unlabeled pool row in the TFM’s row-representation space;
TANN-feat measures the distance in the standardized feature
space instead. Additionally, we implement Target-Random
that assigns nearest pool rows to uniformly drawn targets,
and Target-Coverage to the targets farthest from the labeled
set; both use the target inputs but no uncertainty. Finally,
EPIC (Section 3.3) with s=64 shortlisted candidates and
mr=256 sampled targets per round. An ablation on s and
mr is provided in Section F.
Baselines with other models. To relate the TabICL-based
rules to the baselines of Tab-AICL, we further evaluate
under the same protocol (Table 6): the frozen TabPFN v3
with the Tab-AICL rules (Random, Margin, Coreset, Hybrid
and Proxy-Hybrid) and TANN-feat, the gradient-boosted
tree models XGBoost-Margin and CatBoost-Margin, which
are refitted on the labeled set in every round and select by
their own margin, and LabelSpreading-Random (Treerath &
Pittorino, 2026), a graph-based semi-supervised model over
the labeled rows, the pool and the test inputs with random
acquisition. In addition, to test whether the findings depend
on the backbone, we repeat the complete study (all rules,
including EPIC and TANN) with TabPFN v3 as the frozen
model (Section E).
Metrics. Following Tab-AICL, we measure the normalized
area under the curve of Cohen’s κ on the test split over the
acquisition rounds (AULC), which equals the average κ over
the trajectory and rewards rules that are accurate early. Be-
cause the purpose of acquisition is the model obtained once
the budget is spent, we additionally report κ at the budget
(final κ) and use it to assess the models’ capabilities at the
budget. Cohen’s κ corrects accuracy for chance agreement,
which matters on imbalanced datasets.
Statistical analysis. For every dataset, each metric is av-
eraged over the ten seeds. Across the twenty datasets we
report mean values and mean ranks, and for pairs of meth-
ods the number of wins, ties (|∆| ≤0.005) and losses
together with the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on
the dataset means. When a method is compared with all nine
others, we additionally apply the Holm correction for mul-
tiple comparisons and state it explicitly. All other p-values
are uncorrected.
Implementation. All acquisition rules of the main compar-
ison use the same frozen TFM, TabICL with the publicly
released v2 classification checkpoint (Qu et al., 2025; 2026),
with an ensemble of eight members and its default inference
settings. The TabPFN baselines use the TabPFN v3 clas-
sification checkpoints (binary and multiclass) (Hollmann
et al., 2025), also with eight ensemble members. XGBoost

4.3. Where: target awareness matters substantially
Using the target inputs helps, and so does choosing which
targets to serve. The target-aware rules see the test inputs,
which the Tab-AICL rules do not. The two controls separate
the use of these inputs from the choice of the targets: they
assign nearest pool rows to targets drawn at random (Target-
Random) or chosen for coverage (Target-Coverage), without
uncertainty. Access to the target inputs alone already im-
proves on random labeling: Target-Random in AULC on
13 of 20 datasets (+0.016, p = 0.008) and Target-Coverage
at the budget on 13 (+0.028, p = 0.012). Choosing the
targets by the model’s uncertainty or by information gain
improves further: at the budget, TANN improves on Target-
Random on 12 datasets (+0.014, p = 0.070), and EPIC
on 15 (+0.022, p = 0.036) and on Target-Coverage on 13
(+0.014, p = 0.091) (details in Tables 1 and 9).

Limitations. (i) No adaptive rule yet. We observe when
each criterion is valuable but do not evaluate a rule that
switches between them; whether such a switch improves
on both, and how to detect the transition, is the most di-
rect test of the first finding. (ii) Transductive setting. The
target-aware rules require the target inputs, or a represen-
tative sample of them, before labeling. (iii) Classification.
EPIC enumerates hypothetical labels and is implemented
for classification only. (iv) Approximations. EPIC selects
batches greedily, so the rows of a batch may carry overlap-
ping information. Its two other approximations, the shortlist
of candidates and the sample of targets, do not affect κ at
the budget (Section F) in our setting.
5. Conclusion
We ask how the value of diversity, predictive uncertainty and
target-aware information gain changes as a frozen tabular
foundation model (TFM) acquires more context. Across
twenty benchmarks and two TFMs, the answer has three
parts. Diversity is the best use of the first few dozen labels,
and prediction-oriented acquisition overtakes it once the
model’s predictions become informative. Where the model
will be used matters substantially: serving the targets on
which the model is least certain with nearby labels captures
most of the benefit of prediction-oriented acquisition, inde-
pendently of how proximity is measured. And in-context
conditioning makes prediction-oriented information gain
computable for a frozen model: EPIC yields the best mod-
els at the label budget, retains on average 80–87% of the
chance-corrected full-data performance with 5–10% of the
pool labeled on the eight benchmarks with the largest pools,
and selects labels that also improve gradient-boosted trees.

A. Implementation Details
A.1. Hyperparameter Settings
We evaluate all models with at most 100 labels, no validation data is available, so all models use their default settings and all
acquisition rules the values below, which are identical for every dataset and seed.
Frozen TFMs. TabICL uses the publicly released v2 classification checkpoint (package tabicl 2.2.0) and TabPFN the v3
classification checkpoints, the binary one for binary and the multiclass one for multiclass tasks (package tabpfn 9.2.0,
with the pre-training limits on the numbers of rows and features disabled). Both use an ensemble of eight members, their
default preprocessing and inference settings, and the seed of the run as random state. No parameter of either model is
updated in any experiment; the model is refitted, i.e., conditioned on the current labeled set, after every acquisition round.
Protocol. For every seed 0, . . . , 9, a stratified 70/30 split into pool and test set; an initial context of one uniformly drawn
pool row per class; batches of b = 10 rows; budget B = min(100, ⌊|U|/2⌋); Cohen’s κ on the test split after every round.
Ties in the win/tie/loss counts are differences of at most 0.005.
Feature space. Coreset, Hybrid, Proxy-Hybrid and TANN-feat measure distances in the feature space standardized with the
mean and standard deviation of the pool; missing values are set to zero after standardization.
Tab-AICL rules. Margin labels the b pool rows with the largest margin uncertainty 1 −(p(1) −p(2)), where p(1) and
p(2) are the two largest predicted class probabilities. Coreset selects greedily the b pool rows farthest (squared Euclidean
distance) from the labeled set (k-center). Hybrid restricts the unlabeled rows Ur = U \ Lr to the max(2b, ⌊|Ur|/2⌋) rows
with the largest predictive entropy, runs k-means with k = b on them (scikit-learn, automatic number of initializations, seed
of the run) and labels the row nearest to each centroid. Proxy-Hybrid fits a logistic regression with balanced class weights (at
most 1,000 iterations) on the labeled rows, shortlists the min(|Ur|, max(200, min(2000, ⌊0.05|Ur|⌋))) rows with the largest
proxy entropy, keeps the 3b of them with the largest TFM entropy, and applies the k-means step of Hybrid.
Target-aware rules. The targets are the inputs of the test split. TANN chooses the b targets with the largest margin uncertainty
under the current context and assigns to each its nearest unlabeled pool row, without repetition. Distances are Euclidean in
the TFM’s row representation, recomputed in every round, after centering and scaling each row’s representation across its
dimensions and normalizing it to unit length; TANN-feat uses the standardized feature space instead. Target-Random assigns
nearest pool rows to b uniformly drawn targets and Target-Coverage to b targets chosen greedily as farthest from the labeled
rows and the targets already chosen, both in the row representation.
EPIC. In every round, mr = 256 targets are drawn uniformly without replacement and a shortlist of s = 64 candidates is
formed from the 21 unlabeled rows with the largest margin uncertainty, the nearest unlabeled rows to the most uncertain
targets in the row representation (up to 42 candidates in total) and uniformly drawn unlabeled rows for the remainder. The
hypothetical contexts DL ∪{(x, y)} for all x in the shortlist and y ∈Y are evaluated in batched forward passes of 64
contexts, and the b candidates with the largest EPIC score are labeled. With TabICL, the preprocessing statistics are fitted
once on DL and the shortlist with placeholder labels and shared among the hypothetical contexts; with TabPFN, every
hypothetical context is preprocessed separately (Section E).
Baselines with other models. XGBoost (package xgboost 3.4.1, histogram tree method) and CatBoost (package
catboost 1.2.10) use their library defaults and are refitted on the labeled rows in every round; Margin uses their predicted
probabilities. LabelSpreading (scikit-learn 1.9.1) uses a k-nearest-neighbor kernel with seven neighbors and at most 100
iterations on the standardized features of the labeled rows, the pool and the test inputs.
Label reduction. The budget is 10% of the pool and the batch size 0.5% of the pool (rounded up); EPIC labels at most 32
rows per round, so its larger batches are split into several rounds. κ at 1%, 2%, 5% and 10% of the pool is interpolated
linearly from the learning curve and divided by the κ of the same backbone with the fully labeled pool as context on the
same split.
Context analysis. Local contexts consist of the K ∈{25, 50} labeled rows nearest to a query in the row representation,
completed so that every class is present, and of K = 100 rows when the whole pool is labeled. Pseudo-labeled rows are
pool rows that TabICL, conditioned on the acquired labels, predicts with probability at least 0.9; the per-query variants add
the 50 such rows nearest to the query. SMOTE generates 500 synthetic rows by interpolating between an acquired row and
one of its five nearest acquired rows of the same class in the standardized feature space, of which the 50 nearest to the query
are added.

how the acquired labels are presented to the model, and confident pseudo-labeling is a complementary addition to, not a
substitute for, the choice of labels.

D.3. Cost and the information signal
EPIC takes 48 s per run on the small and 145 s on the large datasets at the 100-label budget, roughly an order of magnitude
more than the other rules, and 2 to 30 minutes per run when labeling up to 10% of a pool of up to 7,000 rows; its cost does
not grow with the pool beyond the shortlist. As an instrument, the largest information gain of a round decreases steadily
with the number of labels, from about 10−1 to about 2 · 10−3 nats over the budget. Used as a stopping rule, i.e., stopping a
run once this value falls below a threshold τ, it improves only marginally on stopping every run at the same fraction of its
budget, by at most 0.010 κ (Table 8).

F. Ablation of EPIC’s Approximations
EPIC approximates the information gain over all targets and all unlabeled rows in two ways (Section 3.4): it averages over
mr = min(m, 256) targets drawn per round instead of all m = |T | targets, and it scores only a shortlist of s candidates
instead of every unlabeled row. We vary one of the two at a time with TabICL as the backbone, keeping the other at its
default (s = 64, mr = 256) and the protocol unchanged (batch size b = 10, ten seeds). The shortlist size is varied on
the eight datasets with the smallest pools (101 to 437 pool rows), on which scoring every unlabeled row is affordable; the
number of sampled targets is varied on all twenty datasets (44 to 3,000 targets).
Shortlist size. Final κ is insensitive to the shortlist size: for s ∈{16, 32, 128} and for scoring every unlabeled row, it stays
within 0.01 of the default without any significant difference, and every configuration remains well above TANN and Hybrid
(Figure 10, Table 16). Over the trajectory, however, larger shortlists are worse: with s = 128 the AULC decreases by 0.014
(0/4/4 datasets, p = 0.04) and with every unlabeled row scored by 0.017 (0/1/7, p = 0.008), below the level of Hybrid
and TANN, whereas s = 16 and s = 32 match the default. The shortlist is thus not only a reduction of cost. A plausible
explanation is its composition: two thirds of it consist of rows with uncertain predictions and of rows near uncertain targets,
and the remaining third is drawn at random, so it restricts the greedy top-b selection to a diverse set of candidates; without
this restriction, the b highest-scoring rows of a round tend to carry overlapping information (Section 3.4), which matters
most in the cold start. In addition, with TabICL the shortlist rows enter the fit of the preprocessing statistics, so a larger
shortlist also changes the statistics shared among the hypothetical contexts. The cost grows linearly with s, from 15 s per run
for s = 16 to 108 s when every unlabeled row is scored.
Number of sampled targets. The number of sampled targets has no measurable effect on the twenty datasets (Figure 11,
Table 17): for mr ∈{16, 32, 64, 1024}, the mean final κ and AULC stay within 0.005 of the default mr = 256, and no
difference is significant (p ≥0.08); all configurations remain above Hybrid and TANN at the budget. This also holds on the
eight datasets with more than 256 targets (583 to 3,000), on which the default uses a genuine subsample: averaging over four
times as many targets (mr = 1024) changes final κ by +0.004 (p = 0.46) and AULC by −0.004 (p = 0.64), and averaging
over only mr = 16 by +0.003 (p = 1.00) and +0.006 (p = 0.31). On datasets with at most 256 targets, the default uses
every target, so mr = 1024 coincides with it there. The cost, in contrast, grows with mr; averaged over all twenty datasets,
it ranges from 49 s per run for mr = 16 to 87 s for the default and 157 s for mr = 1024.
Therefore, both approximations are benign at the budget: a few dozen sampled targets suffice to rank the candidates, and
smaller shortlists are cheaper at equal quality, whereas larger ones reduce the quality over the trajectory. The defaults are
therefore conservative, and EPIC could be run at a fraction of the reported cost.
