Targeted In-Context Poisoning of Tabular
Foundation Models: An Empirical Study
Adem Alparslan1*
1*FOM University of Applied Sciences for Economics and Management,
Essen, Germany.
Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): adem.alparslan@fom.de;
Abstract
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) predict the label of a query row conditioned
on a context of labelled rows, without training on them. Drawn from opera-
tional data, the context becomes an attack surface. We study targeted in-context
poisoning in its simplest form: an attacker with write access to the context’s
source places exact copies of a target row there with the wrong label. On 30
OpenML datasets for binary classification, one such poison row flips on average
53 % (TabICL) and 65 % (TabPFN) of the predictions correct without attack,
and three poison rows flip almost all. The same copy with the true label flips
almost none, and performance on the remaining rows hardly changes. Predic-
tions flip by two routes: in TabICL, the target row’s embedding rotates towards
the mean of the wrong class; in TabPFN, the poison row pulls genuine rows of
the wrong class along. The TFMs thus infer soundly, but from forged evidence.
Predictions of trained models flip as well: almost always for a random forest, for
CatBoost as often as for the TFMs, hardly for a logistic regression. New with
TFMs is not the kind of vulnerability but the timing: the data take effect at the
moment of prediction, not through training, and a poison row in a live context
can be deleted afterwards. Three established requirements must therefore cover
the context: access control for its source, traceability per prediction, which can
also deter, and validation at prediction time. A detection mechanism shows how
such validation can work.
Keywords: tabular foundation models, in-context learning, in-context poisoning, data
poisoning, label flipping, TabICL, TabPFN

1 Introduction
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) carry the in-context learning (ICL) of language
models over to tables [1–5]. They predict the label of a new row, the query row,
from the labelled rows in their context. This happens in a single forward pass and
without training on the task. Instead, they are trained in advance, mostly on a large
number of synthetic tables. These are drawn from a distribution over datasets, the
prior, which is defined by random structural causal models [3, 6–8]. Read in Bayesian
terms, TFMs thereby approximate the posterior predictive distribution that follows
from this prior and the context [2, 3]. For small and medium-sized datasets, TFMs
are reported to match or even exceed the performance of tree-based models and deep
learning models [6, 7, 9].
In operation, the context can come directly from operational data [10]. Data plat-
forms, for example, allow TFMs to be run directly on the table in which the data
reside [11, 12]. Whoever can write to these data changes the input of a TFM and thus
the basis of its next prediction, without waiting for a training run. This makes the
context a potential attack surface.
We examine an attack on this surface in its simplest targeted form and call it
targeted in-context poisoning. The attacker wants a particular query row, the target
row, to receive the wrong label, and writes one to five exact copies of the target row
with the wrong label in place of as many context rows. Such a copy is called a poison
row, the remaining context rows are called genuine context rows. A prediction flips if
the model predicts the target row correctly without attack and wrongly under attack.
No context row can be closer to the target row in its features than its exact copy. If
a model relies on similar rows, this should show most clearly for the exact copy. This
is why we choose this form. A TFM, however, compares rows in its representation,
which it forms from the features and the labels of the context, and there the poison
row need not be close to the target row. Whether and how often the attack works is
therefore an empirical question.
The exact copy is at the same time the most conspicuous form of the attack, and
this is intended. As a duplicate of the target row, it can be found if the context is
checked for duplicates of the query row before every prediction. The study thus does
not look for an attack method that outwits a check of the context. Instead, it aims to
raise awareness that the context of a TFM can be an attack surface through which
its predictions can be steered. Nor does it pass judgement on the performance of the
TFMs examined.
For trained models, the manipulation of the training data, known as data poison-
ing, is well studied [13–18]. For TFMs, in contrast, the study of their vulnerability is
still at an early stage. Djilani et al. [19] study evasion attacks: they perturb the query
row slightly and show that even small, structured perturbations lower the accuracy
of TFMs considerably, while the context remains unchanged. Biloš et al. [20] corrupt
the context as a whole, for example by flipping the labels of its most central rows, and
measure the loss in mean accuracy. Their attacks target the mechanism by which the
models predict, not individual predictions, and lower the performance of the model
overall.
2


Two studies come close to the attack examined here. Anwar et al. [21] manipulate
individual context rows of a transformer that learns in context: for single-layer linear
transformers that learn linear regression in context, they prove that a single altered
context row can force an arbitrary prediction. Wahdany et al. [22] place copies of a
query row with the wrong label in the context of a TFM, but use them for membership
inference: how many copies are needed to flip the prediction reveals whether the row
is already in the context.
As a poisoning attack that redirects the prediction for a chosen query row, the
manipulation of the context of a TFM has, to our knowledge, not been studied so
far. It is analogous to the poisoning of demonstrations for language models [23] and
of retrieval corpora [24]. As in indirect prompt injection [25], the attacker places
the input in data that the model reads only at prediction time. In the taxonomy of
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), the attack is a targeted
poisoning attack: it is not meant to lower the performance of the model overall, but
to redirect the prediction for individual target rows chosen by the attacker [18]. It
requires control over the context, which here takes the place of the training data. The
attacker can thus alter rows of the context and, in particular, flip their labels (label
flipping). For trained models, this form of attack is considered effective [18]. Whether
label flipping is equally effective against a TFM is what this study examines.
We run the attack on two current TFMs of different architecture, TabICLv2 [6]
and TabPFN-3.5 [8], referred to below as TabICL and TabPFN. The basis is 30 public
datasets for binary classification on which both TFMs classify well. Besides the attack
arm, in which the context contains the poison rows, there is a control arm: there the
same copies carry the true label and are called control rows. Comparing the two arms
is meant to separate the effect of the wrong label from the effect that a copy of the
target row in the context has by itself.
The findings on data poisoning concern models that are trained on the poisoned
data. Whether flipping is a peculiarity of TFMs is shown by three such models, trained
on the same poisoned context: a random forest, CatBoost [26] and a logistic regression.
The questions of this study are nevertheless addressed to the TFMs alone, because
the datasets are selected by their performance.
Three research questions guide the study. The first asks about the effect of the
wrong label and, to this end, compares the attack arm with the control arm. It is
posed for each of the two TFMs:
(1a) Do one to five poison rows flip more predictions in TabICL that are correct
without attack than as many control rows?
(1b) Do they also flip more such predictions in TabPFN than as many control rows?
The first question settles whether and how often predictions flip, but not what the
poison row changes inside a TFM. For this, we look in each model at the place where
it forms its decision.
In TabICL, an MLP forms the decision from the embedding of the target row. It
cannot be read off there directly. Biloš et al. [20] report a readout rule that largely
reproduces the predictions of TabICL on classification tasks, and Kurashkin et al.
[9] adopt it in their survey: for each class the centroid is formed as the mean of the
embeddings of its context rows, and the target row receives the class of the nearest
3


centroid. This rule is reported for contexts without attack. If a poison row only shifted
the centroid, it should have little effect, because it enters the centroid of the wrong
class as only one of many rows. If the rule also holds under attack, a flipped target
row lies closer to the centroid of the wrong class and a held one closer to the centroid
of the true class. The second question therefore has two parts:
(2a) In TabICL, does the embedding of a flipped target row lie closer to the centroid
of the wrong class under attack?
(2b) Does the embedding of a held target row lie closer to the centroid of the true
class under attack?
The decision of TabPFN is a weighted vote over the labels of the context rows: the
model forms its prediction in a decoder through attention over these labels, designed as
a soft nearest-neighbour retrieval over the context [7]. Unlike in TabICL, the decision
can be read off directly here, from the weights that the target row places on the context
rows. The prediction flips exactly when the rows with the wrong label together receive
more than half of the weight. If the attack flips a prediction, it is plausible that the
poison rows, as exact copies of the target row, alone receive more than half. The third
question reads:
(3) In TabPFN, do the poison rows receive more than half of the weight for flipped
target rows?
With the answers to these questions and the conclusions drawn from them, the
study makes three contributions. First, it shows that the context of a TFM is an
attack surface and can be manipulated in a targeted way. Second, it describes what
happens inside two TFMs of different architecture when their context is poisoned.
Third, it argues that three established requirements for secure operation must cover
the context of a TFM: access control, traceability and validation.
The study is structured as follows: Section 2 describes the experimental setup, and
Section 3 reports first the effect of the attack, also on trained models, and then what
happens inside the two TFMs. Section 4 answers the research questions, interprets
flipping as a sound inference from forged evidence, derives implications for operation
and names limitations, and Section 5 concludes.
2 Experimental setup
2.1 Threat model, attack arm and control arm
We examine the attack in binary classification. The goal of an attacker is that a
particular query row, the target row, receives the label the attacker wants, while all
other predictions remain unchanged, so that the attack does not show in the overall
performance of the model. In the experiment, this is always the label opposite to the
true one, which we call the wrong label. The feature values of the target row are known
to the attacker before the prediction, for example because the attacker submits the
query row. Knowledge of the model or its weights is not required, and the number of
poison rows is chosen without observing predictions of the model.
For the attack to work, the poison rows must reach the context. We therefore
assume that the attacker can write and overwrite entries of the source from which
4


the context is formed. We further assume that these entries reach the context of the
target row before its prediction, for example because the context comprises the whole
source. Access to the model itself is not required.
To flip the prediction of the target row, the attacker replaces b of the n context
rows with poison rows. A poison row is an exact copy of the target row: it agrees with
it in every feature value but carries the wrong label. The number b of replaced rows is
the budget, with b ∈{1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. The remaining n −b context rows stay unchanged.
Replacing the rows instead of adding them is a design decision. It fixes the context
size n and thus the number of rows the TFM reads. For both TFMs it also fixes the
scaling of the queries in the attention, which depends on the context length [6, 7].
Replacing uses the fact that the attacker can overwrite existing entries.
The rows to be replaced are drawn at random from the whole context, so the
attack does not systematically remove genuine rows near the target row. All models
see the same poisoned context for the same target row. The budgets are nested: the
rows replaced at budget b > 1 include those replaced at b −1, so that two budgets
differ only in the poison rows added.
Every target row is predicted under three conditions: once without attack and,
for every budget, once in the attack arm and once in the control arm, eleven times
in total. In the attack arm, the context contains the poison rows. In the control arm,
control rows replace the same context rows as in the attack arm. A control row is the
same copy of the target row with the true label. The two arms thus differ only in the
label of the copies. A difference between them is therefore due to the label, not to the
copy itself.
2.2 Data
The 30 datasets for binary classification come from OpenML [27] (Appendix A). All
have exclusively numerical features. All models thus receive the same input, and no
model-specific handling of categorical features confounds the results.
On every dataset, both TFMs reach an AUC of at least 0.8 without attack, aver-
aged over the five test folds (see below). The study thus excludes tasks on which a
TFM classifies poorly. The threshold of 0.8 is a pragmatic choice. Both criteria, ex-
clusively numerical features and an AUC of both TFMs of at least 0.8, are tailored to
the TFMs. The datasets do not come from a fixed benchmark suite. We collected 32
datasets for binary classification with exclusively numerical features from OpenML.
Two of them fail the AUC threshold (Appendix A). The selection was based solely
on the data and the performance without attack, not on the effect of the attack. The
remaining datasets range from 540 to 566,602 rows, from 4 to 100 features, and from
a balanced class distribution to a minority class of 0.2 %.
Every dataset is reduced to a stratified random sample of at most 3,000 rows.
This lies in the range of small and medium-sized tables in which TFMs are considered
particularly strong [3, 28]. The features are passed to the models unchanged. Only for
the logistic regression are they imputed and standardised (Appendix F).
Every sample is split into five stratified folds. Each fold serves once as test rows,
the other four as context. The context thus contains at most 2,400 rows and fewer for
small datasets, 432 in the smallest. Even five poison rows therefore make up at most
5


1.2 % of the context. From the test rows of every fold, up to five target rows per class
are drawn at random, so that both classes are equally represented. The exception is
creditcard: each of its test folds contains only one row of the minority class, so that
this dataset has 30 target rows, 25 of the majority class and 5 of the minority class.
This gives 1,480 target rows, 50 for each of the other datasets. Even without attack,
the context contains exact duplicates with the true label in 4 of the 30 datasets, for
17 target rows in total, and 708 for one target row. They remain in the context.
Among the target rows that hold even at five poison rows, most have such a duplicate
(Section 3.1).
Without attack, all target rows of a fold share the same context. In the attack arm
and in the control arm, every target row receives its own context, which contains only
its own copies.
2.3 Quantifying the effect of the attack
Table 1 summarises the quantities of this study. For every target row, πF,0 and πF
denote the predicted probability of the wrong label without and under attack. We
evaluate only target rows for which the model predicts the true label without attack,
as a rule πF,0 < 0.5. For the others, a wrong prediction could not be attributed to the
attack. The evaluated rows are called the attacked target rows of the model: 1,307
of the 1,480 target rows for TabICL and 1,316 for TabPFN (Appendix A). Without
attack, the two TFMs reach a mean AUC over the datasets of 0.958 (TabICL) and
0.962 (TabPFN). An attacked target row is called flipped if the model predicts the
wrong label for it under attack, as a rule πF > 0.5, above the decision threshold, and
held otherwise.
Every attack is directed at a single target row and succeeds if this row flips. To
measure how often it succeeds, the attack is carried out for every target row separately.
The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of successes among these individual attacks
[15], per budget, dataset and model. The target rows are drawn balanced by class
(Section 2.2), but the attacked ones no longer are, because the models predict rows of
the minority class correctly less often (Appendix A). The ASR of a dataset therefore
weights the majority class somewhat more strongly.
Every model has its own attacked target rows. The two TFMs are therefore
compared per dataset, not per target row (Table B1).
Beyond the target row, every model in every arm also predicts the remaining test
rows of its fold with the context of this target row. We record the AUC on them to
check whether the attack stays targeted, that is, does not change the performance
on the remaining rows. It is computed for the context of every attacked target row,
averaged per dataset and then over the datasets.

The trained models are a random forest, CatBoost [26] and a logistic regression,
likewise at their default settings, the latter with up to 5,000 iterations (Appendix F).
They are trained on the same context including the copies, anew for every target row,
every budget and every arm, and once per fold without attack. In these models, the
poisoning thus acts through training. Without attack they likewise reach an average
AUC above 0.8, but a lower one than the TFMs (Appendix F.1).
2.5 Statistical analysis
The unit of analysis is the dataset. Each model makes 16,280 predictions, eleven per
target row. These predictions are not independent: the target rows of a dataset come
from the same distribution, and their contexts derive from the same fold context.
Every quantity is therefore first averaged per dataset over its target rows from all five
folds and then reported over the 30 datasets. The standard deviations are given in
the appendix. Where a count pools the target rows of all datasets, this is noted at
that point. Groups such as flipped and held target rows are formed per dataset and
budget. A dataset belongs to a group as soon as it has one target row in it, so that its
mean can rest on few target rows. Two groups are compared paired over the datasets
that have both.
Each research question from (1a) to (3) is assigned a test (Table E1 in Appendix E).
All tests are Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over the datasets [29]. For (1a) and (1b), the
test is a paired comparison of the attack arm with the control arm. For (2a) and (2b),
it tests m against 1, because that is the threshold of the readout rule. For (3), it tests
aF,P against 0.5, because that is the decision threshold: if the poison rows receive more
than half of the weight, they flip the prediction alone. All tests are two-sided, although
the questions are directional, and are thus conservative. Datasets with a difference of
zero are excluded from a test. They occur only in tests (1a) and (1b) at one poison
row, on datasets where neither a poison row nor a control row flips a target row. A
result is called significant at p < 0.05. Because each test answers a question of its own,
it forms a family of its own with its five budgets, within which we correct according
to Holm [29]. Every reported p is Holm-corrected unless it is explicitly marked as
uncorrected. If one corrects over all 25 tests instead, every significant result remains,
except for test (3) at two poison rows (p = 0.066 with Holm over all 25 tests). The test
tables in Appendix E (Tables E2, E3 and E4) give as effect size the mean difference

3 Results
3.1 Effect of the attack
A single poison row already flips the majority of the attacked target rows in both
TFMs, on average 53 % for TabICL and 65 % for TabPFN. The share rises with every
further poison row, and from three poison rows on almost all flip (Figure 1). The few
target rows that hold even at five poison rows mostly have a genuine duplicate with
the true label in the context (Table B1). The more duplicates there are, the further
πT stays above the decision threshold. Without duplicates or with only a few, it stays
only just above it.
The effect of the attack is due to the wrong label, not to the copy. Across all
datasets, models and budgets, the same copy with the true label flips only one target
row in total. The test confirms this: in both TFMs and at every budget, poison rows flip
statistically significantly more predictions than control rows (research questions (1a)
and (1b), p < 0.001, Table E2). In the control arm, the copy even has a corrective
effect: a single control row already corrects most of the predictions that are wrong
without attack (79 % for TabICL and 84 % for TabPFN, Table B2). The label of the
copy thus determines in which direction the prediction moves.
10


The effect of a single poison row varies strongly across the datasets. On most
datasets, it flips at least half of the target rows. On a few, it flips almost none
(Table B1). At one and two poison rows, about 12 percentage points more target rows
flip in TabPFN than in TabICL, but the datasets on which many predictions flip are
similar for both: at one poison row, the ASR of the two TFMs correlates across the
datasets with Spearman’s ρ = 0.80. With more poison rows the gap shrinks, at three
to 3 percentage points, because almost all target rows then flip in both TFMs.
The attack stays targeted. Averaged over the datasets, the AUC on the remaining
test rows changes by at most 0.001. This holds for both TFMs and all budgets. On no
single dataset does it change by more than about 0.010 (Appendix B.3). The attack
thus does not show in the overall performance of the TFMs.
Whether flipping is a peculiarity of TFMs is shown by the comparison with three
models that are trained on the same poisoned context. Their predictions flip as well,
but how often differs greatly between the models (Figure 1, Table F2): one poison
row flips almost all attacked target rows for the random forest (98.7 %), a good half
for CatBoost (52 %) and almost none for the logistic regression (1.8 %). In the control
arm, no target row flips for any of the three models. Because the datasets are selected
by the performance of the TFMs, these shares do not show whether predictions of
trained models in general flip more or less often than those of TFMs.
It is more informative to look at the datasets on which many predictions flip.
Despite a similar ASR, CatBoost at one poison row differs clearly from the TFMs in
this respect. On Pulsar-Dataset and wilt, one poison row flips more than half of
the attacked target rows for CatBoost and almost none for TabICL and TabPFN. On
phoneme and electricity it is the other way round: half or more flip there for the
TFMs, at most a quarter for CatBoost (Tables B1 and F2). Across all 30 datasets,
the ranking by ASR for CatBoost is hardly related to that for TabICL or TabPFN
(Spearman’s ρ = 0.10 and 0.21).
3.2 Inside the models
3.2.1 TabICL: the position of the target row
Research questions (2a) and (2b) test whether the readout rule reported for TabICL
also holds under attack. Figure 2 shows the mean m per dataset for the flipped and
the held target rows. On average, the rule holds for both groups: flipped target rows
lie above the threshold m = 1 of the readout rule, held ones below it (Table E3). At
every budget, flipped target rows have m > 1 with statistical significance, so they lie
closer to the centroid µF of the wrong class (p < 0.001). At one poison row they lie
only just above the threshold, and further above it with every additional poison row.
Held target rows have m < 1, so they lie closer to the centroid µT of the true class,
significantly at one to three poison rows (p < 0.01). From four poison rows on, only
five datasets still have held target rows, too few for the test to become significant,
because with five datasets even the uncorrected p does not fall below 0.063. At four
and five poison rows, too, the held target rows lie below the threshold on almost all
of these datasets.

The relative closeness m also orders the target rows well: within a dataset, it
separates flipped from held target rows with an average AUC of 0.95 (Table C5). For
the individual target row, however, the threshold m = 1 does not always agree with
the decision of TabICL: at one poison row, about a quarter of the flipped target rows
still lie below it (Table C4). Even without attack, 9 of the 1,307 attacked target rows
already lie above it, although TabICL predicts the true class for them (Table C6). The
reason lies in the measure: m derives only two cosine values from z and the context,
whereas the MLP decides from all dimensions of z.
The target row crosses the threshold through a rotation of its embedding, while the
class centroids hardly shift. For flipped target rows, cos(z, z0) drops to 0.78 on average
already at one poison row and to 0.51 at five. The class centroids, in contrast, barely
move: cos(µT , µT,0) and cos(µF , µF,0) stay at 0.998 or above on average (Tables C7
and C8), because the poison row enters µF as only one of many rows.
Flipped and held target rows differ in their relative closeness without attack and
in how much the attack changes it: at one poison row, flipped target rows lie closer to
the threshold even without attack (m0 = 0.82 against 0.75) and the attack increases
it more (m−m0 = 0.23 against 0.15), both on almost all datasets (Tables C3 and C2).
Under attack, the target row moves into a different neighbourhood at the input of
the MLP. Without attack, hardly any of its 25 nearest genuine context rows carries
the wrong label (νF on average 1.1). Under one poison row, the number is 20.7 for
flipped target rows and 6.6 for held ones (Table C10), so most flipped target rows end
up in a neighbourhood in which the wrong class holds the majority (νF > 12.5). This
is a consequence of the rotation and not the reason for the flip: when the embedding
of the target row rotates towards the wrong class, it is mainly rows of that class that
lie near it afterwards. The MLP does not see these neighbours, it reads only z. νF
thus describes where z ends up, in the same representation that the MLP reads.
12


In the control arm, the control row pulls the target row in the opposite direction,
towards the centroid of the true class: with a single control row, m is already smaller
than without attack on all 30 datasets (Table C6), and νF drops from 1.1 to 0.4
(Table C10). As for the prediction (Section 3.1), the label of the copy thus determines
in which direction the embedding of the target row moves.
3.2.2 TabPFN: the weight of the poison rows
In TabPFN, the decision can be read off directly from the vote. Because πF = aF , the
prediction flips as soon as the rows with the wrong label together receive more than
half of the weight: aF > aT . Without attack, the wrong class is far from this: for the
target rows that flip under one poison row, it receives on average 11 % of the weight
without attack, for those that hold 3 % (Figure 3). Even without attack, flipped target
rows thus start closer to the decision threshold. Because every poison row agrees with
the target row in all features, it is natural to expect that the poison rows alone receive
more than half of the weight. Research question (3) tests this for the flipped target
rows.
For flipped target rows, a single poison row receives significantly less than half of
the weight, on average 31 %. This holds on almost all datasets (research question (3),
p < 0.001, Table E4). It is nevertheless the strongest single row: for an average of 83 %
of the flipped target rows, it carries the largest weight in the context (Table D4). At
the same time, the genuine rows of the wrong class gain weight: their joint weight rises
to 45 % on average, about four times as much as without attack. Flipping is therefore
not due to the poison row alone: only together with the genuine rows of the wrong
class does it lift the wrong class above the decision threshold. Section 4.1 returns to
this.
With more poison rows, the split between poison rows and genuine rows reverses
(Figure 3). Two poison rows receive on average just over half, but only on two thirds
of the datasets, and the result (p = 0.022) does not hold if one corrects over all 25
tests (Section 2.5). From three poison rows on, their weight lies significantly above
the decision threshold on almost all datasets (p < 0.001) and, for most flipped target
rows, suffices alone for flipping. The poison rows then account for 72 % of the weight,
the genuine rows of the wrong class for only 24 %. At one poison row, it is thus mainly
the genuine rows that carry the flip, at three the poison rows. Even at three poison
rows, the genuine rows of the wrong class still receive about three times as much as
without attack. For flipped target rows, less and less weight remains for the true label:
23 % at one poison row, 4 % at three. The flipped prediction is therefore not narrowly
but confidently wrong. For held target rows, the weight of the wrong label stays at
about one third at each of these budgets, because there the poison rows receive only
about one fifth of the weight even at three poison rows.
Under attack, the genuine rows of the wrong class gain weight on every dataset,
for flipped as for held target rows (Table D1). In the control arm it is the other way
round: with one control row they lose weight on all 30 datasets, and at three control
rows the wrong label receives only 1 % of the weight instead of 7 % without attack
(Tables D1 to D3). In both arms, the copy thus shifts the weight towards the class of
its label.

4 Discussion
4.1 Answers to the research questions
A single poison row already flips the majority of the predictions that are correct
without attack in both TFMs, and three poison rows flip almost all. The same copies
with the true label flip almost no prediction, so in both TFMs the effect is due to
the wrong label (research questions (1a) and (1b)). In TabICL, flipped target rows
lie on average closer to the centroid of the wrong class under attack, and held ones
closer to the centroid of the true class, significantly up to three poison rows. The
readout rule reported for TabICL thus also holds under attack (research questions (2a)
and (2b)). In TabPFN, a single poison row receives less than half of the weight for
flipped target rows on average. From three poison rows on, the poison rows receive
more than half, and for most flipped target rows their weight alone suffices for the flip
(research question (3)).
The poison row flips the prediction in TabICL and TabPFN by different routes. In
TabICL, the copy rotates the embedding of the target row towards the mean of the
wrong class, while the class centroids keep their direction. The target row thus moves
into a neighbourhood in which the wrong class holds the majority (Section 3.2.1). In
TabPFN, the label of the copy enters the vote of the decoder directly. The copy usually
receives the largest weight, and together with it the genuine rows of the wrong class
gain weight. Nguyen et al. [30] call it “joint processing” when a label in the context
changes the effect of the other labels in this way. They show it for regression on five
TFMs, among them TabICL in the version examined here and TabPFN in version 3,
14


and find it mainly in the attention weights. Our measurements show joint processing
for classification as well. At one poison row, the copy thus acts less as a vote than as
a catalyst: it gives the genuine rows of the wrong class the weight that it lacks itself
to flip the prediction. From three poison rows on, the copies carry the flip themselves.
At one and two poison rows, more predictions flip in TabPFN than in TabICL. This
difference does not protect TabICL, because the gap shrinks with every poison row,
and two poison rows in TabICL already flip more predictions than one in TabPFN.
Since the attacker chooses the budget, the difference costs the attacker only one ad-
ditional row. The difference between the TFMs fits their two routes: in TabICL, the
copy must first rotate the embedding of the target row, whereas in TabPFN its label
enters the vote directly (Table C9). The two TFMs share the datasets on which many
predictions flip, and CatBoost does not show this ranking (Section 3.1). The ranking
therefore does not follow from the datasets alone.
Four studies that attack or exploit the context help to place these results. For
single-layer linear transformers, Anwar et al. [21] prove that a single altered context
row can force an arbitrary prediction. Our results show something similar for current
TFMs, and without optimisation, because the exact copy is already the nearest ev-
idence. Biloš et al. [20] attack the context in an untargeted way and measure how
much the overall performance drops. Flipping the labels of the most central context
rows lowers it by a few percentage points, and a single row with the wrong label at the
centroid of a class hardly changes it. Here, a single copy that agrees with the target
row in all features flips the majority of the target rows and leaves the other predic-
tions almost unchanged (Section 3.1): what matters is where a row lies, not how many
there are.
Wahdany et al. [22] use the same copy for membership inference, assuming that
rows in the context resist relabelling. Our duplicates confirm this: no single copy
flips a target row against a genuine duplicate with the true label (Table B1). Finally,
Djilani et al. [19] use the context for defence against evasion attacks by gradually
replacing context rows with adversarially perturbed examples. Our control arm shows
the flip side of the attack: copies with the true label correct most wrong predictions
(Section 3.1).
4.2 Not a faulty inference but forged evidence
Since a TFM approximates the posterior predictive distribution given its context
(Section 1), every context row counts as an observation, including a poison row: the
model cannot check whether a label is correct. In feature space, an exact copy of the
target row is the nearest evidence about it that a single context row can provide. Even
one such row can flip the prediction, in TabPFN through its weight in the vote and
in TabICL by rotating the embedding of the target row. Hence, the prediction of a
TFM does not flip because of a faulty inference, but because the model infers soundly
from an observation that is forged in this case.
A faulty inference would have no reason to follow the label, to grow stronger
with the number of copies or to weigh counter-evidence. The results show all three
properties. First, the same copy with the true label flips almost no prediction and
corrects most wrong ones. Second, more evidence has a stronger effect, since more
15


poison rows flip more predictions. Third, counter-evidence is weighed. The target rows
that hold lie deeper in the true class even without attack, in TabICL with a smaller
m0, in TabPFN with less weight on the wrong label. A poison row never prevails
against a genuine duplicate with the true label, and five poison rows prevail against
only a few duplicates. The TFM thus infers from the poison rows as from any other
observation.
Two further findings indicate that the influence of a poison row depends on its
nearness to the target row. The effect of the attack stays confined to the target row,
and the other predictions of its fold hardly change, whereas a faulty inference in the
model would affect them as well. In TabPFN, moreover, the poison row usually receives
the largest weight (Section 4.1), which is how its decoder is designed: it predicts the
class through a soft nearest-neighbour retrieval over the context [7].
Following the forged label is not peculiar to TFMs. Trained models do so as well:
with one poison row, almost all predictions flip for the random forest and, for Cat-
Boost, about as many as for TabICL. Hardly any flip for the logistic regression, which,
however, also has the lowest performance without attack (mean AUC 0.885 against
0.958 and 0.962 for the TFMs). What is peculiar to the TFM is that no training lies
between context and prediction: the poison row acts in a single forward pass. TFMs
are thus in the same position as trained models, for which label flipping is considered
effective [18].
Language models show a similar weakness. Indirect prompt injection exploits the
fact that their input changes their behaviour, which Schneier [31] calls a feature rather
than a bug. A language model is steered by injected instructions, a TFM by forged
evidence. In both cases, the model cannot tell a manipulated input from a genuine
one, so the defence lies at the trust boundary, here at the source of the context.
4.3 Implications for operation
For the protection of the source of the context, the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (EU
AI Act) and the NIST each provide a framework. For high-risk AI systems, the EU
AI Act requires, for example, measures to prevent and detect attacks on the training
data and input data [32, Art. 15(5)]. Against targeted poisoning, the NIST names,
for example, access controls, data validation and attestation of the provenance and
integrity of the data. Against indirect prompt injection, the NIST proposes connecting
untrusted sources only through defined interfaces [18].
Technically, the context of a TFM lies between training data and input data in
the sense of the EU AI Act. The Act defines training data as data used to fit learn-
able parameters, and input data as data on the basis of which the system produces
an output [32, Art. 3(29) and (33)]. Like input data, the context is passed only at
prediction time and does not fit any parameters. Like training data, it provides the
observations from which the TFM infers. Its legal categorisation is left to legal assess-
ment. Regardless of this categorisation, the context differs from training data in when
a poison row takes effect. A poison row reaches a trained model only before training,
through data that are typically compiled, validated and versioned [33–35]. The same
holds for a TFM with a “frozen” context: the context is fixed once and then used for
all subsequent predictions, so here, too, a poison row can only get in beforehand.
16


A “live” context is different. The TFM reads it at prediction time from a table
that is written to in operation, so a new row takes effect at the very next prediction.
It is what Sculley et al. [36] call an unstable data dependency, an input whose content
can change at any time. The attacker can write the poison row as soon as the target
row is known. The easiest position is that of someone who records cases with their
outcome and submits an application of their own: a single copy of the application
with the desired outcome often flips the prediction, a few copies almost always. A
regularly retrained model [35] approaches this case the shorter the interval between
training and prediction. With a live context, there is no such interval: a poison row
takes effect at the very next prediction.
Three modes of operation that are currently offered or studied for TFMs read the
context at prediction time. If their source is written to in operation, the context is
live. A TFM can run as a function in a database and form its context directly from
a table, so that predictions can be made with a database query, without a separate
training step [11, 12]. Agents can assemble the context themselves in response to a
request in natural language, so that predictions can be requested without writing
code [37]. Retrieval-based TFMs draw the nearest neighbours of each query row from
a larger pool, so that TFMs scale to datasets too large to fit into the context [38, 39].
With retrieval, the exact copy is closest to the query row and is therefore selected for
certain. At the same time, it faces fewer genuine rows, though the most similar ones.
The results show that three established security requirements must cover the
context of a TFM: access control, traceability and validation.
1. Access control. Write and overwrite access to the source of the context is access
to the model and its predictions. It should be granted in the sense of the access
controls named by the NIST, following the principle of least privilege [40, 41], for
example as read-only access for everyone who does not need to record cases. The
same applies to the pipelines that read data from the source and assemble the
context: whoever can change them also determines which rows the model sees. In
the experimental setup, in which the whole table forms the context, three poison
rows flip almost all attacked predictions with this access and knowledge of the
target row.
2. Traceability. The context on which a prediction rested should be logged together
with its provenance, that is, with the source and the state of the pipeline that
assembled it [42]. In a TFM with a live context, the poison row can be deleted or
overwritten after the prediction, and with it the trace of the attack. Moreover, a
prediction can then be neither understood nor reproduced from the query row and
the output alone, because it depends on the state of the context at the moment of
prediction, and a single row can reverse it. Anyone who wants to understand such
a decision needs the context on which it rests. Such a log can also deter: whoever
knows that a poison row can be traced even after deletion must expect to be found
out.
3. Validation. A check of the context can be added at prediction time. Since the
operator does not know which row an attack targets, every query row is a potential
target, and the check must run before every prediction. Monitoring the overall
performance of the model does not replace it, because such monitoring hardly
17


notices the attack: the AUC on the remaining test rows stays almost unchanged.
Against the attack studied here, a simple check suffices: an exact copy agrees with
the query row in all features and can therefore be found as a duplicate. Applied
before every prediction to duplicates of the query row, the check finds all poison
rows in the experiment, at every budget, without a model and without a threshold.
Without attack, it raises an alarm for 2 % of all test rows on average (Appendix G).
An alarm is therefore a reason to check the prediction, not proof of an attack.
The three requirements interlock. Access control is meant to prevent manipulation
of the context. Validation is meant to find poison rows that get in nonetheless. Trace-
ability is meant to establish what a prediction rested on, even if a poison row was
deleted or overwritten afterwards. This corresponds to the prevention and detection
required by the EU AI Act [32, Art. 15(5)], extended by investigation after the fact.
4.4 Limitations and open questions
Three limitations determine how far the results reach.
First, the study deliberately examines the most conspicuous form of the attack,
the exact copy of the target row with the wrong label. A poison row that merely
resembles the target row would escape a duplicate check. Harder to find would be a
row that is close to the target row only in the representation of the model or that was
constructed using its gradients. The latter, however, requires knowledge of the model
and goes beyond the threat model considered here.
Second, the scope is narrow: binary classification with numerical features, contexts
of at most 2,400 rows, two TFMs whose results do not necessarily carry over to other
TFMs, and datasets selected by the performance of the two TFMs examined. The
datasets are broadly collected but not a sample from a defined population: the results
describe these datasets, not a rate for tabular data in general. All models run at their
default settings, and more strongly regularised trained models might flip less often.
The target rows are balanced by class, so the ASR describes this sample and not the
queries in operation.
Third, the study describes what happens inside the models and does not explain
it causally. The measures are approximations. m reduces the embedding to two cosine
values, although the MLP reads the whole embedding. The weights in TabPFN do
not show whether the query of the target row or the keys of the context rows shift.
νF counts the neighbours in TabICL with a set threshold.
The measurements of this study open up several questions that can be pursued
with the same setup. Interventions in the attention [43, 44] can clarify whether TabICL
decides by nearest neighbours or by the class centroids, whether the context rows shift
together with the target row, and whether the gap between TabPFN and TabICL
follows from their two routes. Further TFMs will show whether the shared ranking of
the datasets is a property of TFMs in general. With retrieval as the context, the effect
of the poison row against the nearest neighbours of the target row can be measured.
It can also be examined whether experts, alone or supported by a language model,
recognise poison rows. Finally, it can be examined why, on average, about as many
predictions flip for CatBoost as for TabICL, although they flip on different datasets.

5 Conclusion
Targeted in-context poisoning works even in its simplest form. An exact copy of the
target row with the wrong label suffices to flip the majority of the predictions that are
correct without attack in both TFMs, and a few copies flip almost all. The same copy
with the true label flips almost none: the effect is due to the label. The performance
on the remaining rows stays almost unchanged, so monitoring the overall performance
hardly notices the attack.
The TFMs do not err in their inference: they infer soundly from an observation
that is forged in this case. Inside the TFMs, the poison row flips the prediction by
two routes. In TabICL, it rotates the embedding of the target row towards the mean
of the wrong class. In TabPFN, a single poison row receives on average less than half
of the weight and pulls genuine rows of the wrong class along. From three poison rows
on, the poison rows themselves receive more than half.
What is new with TFMs is not the kind of vulnerability, since predictions of trained
models such as random forest and CatBoost flip as well. What is new is the timing:
the data take effect at the moment of prediction, and with a live context a poison row
can be deleted or overwritten afterwards. Three established requirements for secure
operation, which agree with the recommendations of the NIST against poisoning, must
therefore cover the context of a TFM: access control, traceability and validation.
Whoever can write the data from which a model infers can redirect individual
predictions. In a TFM, this happens at the moment of prediction, and that is exactly
where it can be checked.

Appendix F
Trained models
The random forest, CatBoost and the logistic regression are retrained on every context
(Section 2.4). All three run at their defaults. CatBoost fits 1,000 trees of depth six
with an L2 regularisation of 3, a learning rate set by the library and no early stopping.
The random forest and the logistic regression use the defaults of scikit-learn: the
random forest has 100 trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample until its leaves are
pure, and the logistic regression has an L2 penalty with C = 1 and is fitted after mean
imputation and standardisation on the context, with up to 5,000 iterations. Every fit
converged. They are not part of the tests (Section 2.5).
