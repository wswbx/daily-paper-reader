Abstract
In-context tabular foundation models (TFMs), such as TabPFN, predict from a
labelled context without updating their parameters and are increasingly used for
out-of-distribution (OOD) detection in Web applications, whose test rows come
from entities absent from that context. Such applications need open-entity OOD
detection: a new entity must not be mistaken for an unknown class, and an unknown
class must still be caught. Existing detectors fail in this setting, and an entity-split
gap hides the failure: random splits also place the test entities in the context.
The failure arises because every TFM score reflects how unfamiliar a row is to
the context. This produces an entity-over-class bias: known-class rows of new
entities look more OOD than familiar ones, sometimes more than unknown classes.
Specifically, the two OOD scores most widely used with in-context TFMs face an
entity–class trade-off: the TFM’s predictive confidence misses unknown classes,
whereas the distance of a test row to the context flags new entities. To address
this problem, we hold a group of entities out of the context and score them with
the remaining context, which turns them into new entities of known classes and
tells us how such rows should score. To measure detection on new entities, we
build NEO-Tab (New-Entity OOD detection on Tables), 14 grouped and temporal
tables on which all test rows come from new entities. We then propose Context-
Calibrated Detection (CCD), which uses these group-out swaps on in-distribution
data to calibrate three distances and the confidence against new-entity references.
CCD weights them by their sensitivity and sharpness, keeps the model frozen and
uses no OOD data. On NEO-Tab, CCD outperforms 16 existing detectors on every
metric we report, and its lead holds on three TFM backbones.

Introduction
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection [41] with in-context tabular foundation models (TFMs), which
predict from a labelled context without updating their parameters [16, 23, 24], is increasingly deployed
in Web services. These services predict for entities, such as merchants in fraud detection [31] or users
on recommendation platforms [29, 37], so their test rows come from entities absent from the context.
The detector must therefore reject rows of unknown classes while accepting the known-class rows of
new entities, a problem we call open-entity OOD detection. Fig. 1 illustrates it on a marketplace that
classifies listings, where a listing from a new seller in a known product category must be accepted,
whereas a listing in a category absent from the context must be rejected.
Open-entity OOD detection first faces an entity-split gap, illustrated in Fig. 2a, because random
splits conceal how existing detectors fail on new entities. Tabular OOD detection draws on two
kinds of evidence, confidence and distance, each resting on a premise that a new entity breaks.
Confidence-based scores such as the maximum softmax probability (MSP) [14] and its variants
[13, 20] assume that in-distribution (ID) rows are predicted confidently. Distance-based scores such
as the Mahalanobis distance [17] and k-nearest neighbours (kNN) [33] assume that ID rows lie near
the training data. Both premises fail on a known-class row of a new entity, because it is neither
predicted confidently nor close to the context. Random splits [3] hide this failure because they place
the entities of the ID test rows in the context as well, where both premises hold. As a result, replacing
2


only the ID test rows of familiar entities with those of new entities lowers the area under the ROC
curve (AUROC) of all eight detectors by 5–16 points on six tasks and changes their ranking.
Open-entity OOD detection also shows an entity-over-class bias: confidence-based and distance-
based detectors rank known-class rows of new entities as more OOD than those of familiar entities.
With one fixed context, this ranking holds for 13 of the 14 detectors. Fig. 2b shows a case in which
these rows look more OOD than even unknown-class rows. On gesture phase, TabPFN with the same
context has a median confidence of 0.98 on known-class rows of familiar entities. On known-class
rows of new entities it drops to 0.51, below even the 0.76 it gives the unknown class. A likely cause
lies in in-context prediction, because every score of an in-context TFM is determined by the context.
In a simple model of in-context prediction, the confidence measures how close a test row is to the
context and reverts to the prior far from it whatever the class, as Prop. 2 states. Distance-based scores
likewise measure unfamiliarity to the context by construction. To an in-context TFM, a new entity
and an unknown class can therefore look equally unfamiliar. Yet a known-class row of a new entity
should be accepted because the entity changes without changing the class, a covariate shift. An
unknown-class row should be rejected because its class is absent from the context, a semantic shift.
The two kinds of evidence also fail in different places, which creates an entity–class trade-off:
distances react to both kinds of novelty and see the unknown class that confidence misses but are
themselves sensitive to new entities, as Fig. 2c shows. Confidence fails because a label-oriented
output need not retain the features in which an unknown class differs. With TabPFN, MSP stays below
24 AUROC on four tables, while a feature-space kNN reaches 84–87 on three of them. Distances fail
because they carry no class information and therefore flag a new entity far from the context as an
outlier. These complementary failures motivate combining the two kinds of evidence after calibrating
each against a reference for how known-class rows of new entities score.
The property that makes new entities look unfamiliar also lets us create new entities from the context,
and our solution builds on it. To an in-context TFM, a new entity is simply an entity absent from
the context, and which entities are absent is under our control. Holding a group of entities out of the
context and scoring them with the remaining context turns them into new entities of known classes,
and under exchangeability their scores are samples from the new-entity distribution, as Prop. 1 states.
Against the entity-split gap, we build NEO-Tab (New-Entity OOD detection on Tables), 14 grouped
and temporal tables that include the six qualifying BeyondArena tasks [25]. On NEO-Tab, ID and
OOD test rows both come only from new entities. Against the entity-over-class bias and the entity–
class trade-off, we propose Context-Calibrated Detection (CCD), whose group-out context swaps
give, from ID data alone, the reference distribution of every score on new entities and its sensitivity
to them. The same swaps give the fraction of held-out rows on which the confidence stays sharp.
CCD corrects the entity-over-class bias by calibrating the confidence and three distances against
their reference distributions, with one distance in a label subspace. CCD addresses the entity–class
trade-off by weighting each distance by its sensitivity and the confidence by its sharpness before
summing them into one score, as Fig. 3 shows.
CCD is the strongest of the 17 detectors on NEO-Tab on all three metrics, gains 7.4 AUROC points
over a kNN tuned to be least sensitive to new entities (p = 0.0012), and keeps this lead on three
backbones. The three testable predictions of the theory hold on the development trials.
We summarise our contributions as follows.
• We give the first formulation of open-entity OOD detection for in-context tabular models and trace
its entity-split gap, entity-over-class bias and entity–class trade-off to in-context prediction.
• We build NEO-Tab, 14 grouped and temporal tables on which all ID and OOD test rows come
from unseen entities, and release its splits and scripts.
• We propose CCD, whose group-out swaps give a new-entity reference from ID data alone, against
which four scores are calibrated and weighted before fusion.
• We find CCD to be the strongest of 17 detectors on 14 tables, 7.4 AUROC points above a kNN
tuned to be least sensitive to new entities, with a lead that holds on three TFM backbones. The
three testable predictions of our theory hold on the development trials.

Related Work
Tabular foundation models under shift. In-context TFMs such as TabPFN-2.5 [8] and TabICL [26]
now lead tabular benchmarks, and Web applications already use them for graph anomaly detection
[21] and evolving data streams [22]. Work on these models under shift asks how accurate prediction
stays on new entities or later periods, not whether a row belongs to a known class. StablePFN [9],
Drift-Resilient TabPFN [12] and a mixture of in-context experts for recurring concept drift [39] make
prediction robust to these shifts, and grouped benchmarks such as TableShift [7] and BeyondArena
[25] measure how much accuracy they cost. None of these works compares how much more an
in-context model loses on new entities than a trained model does, which is the comparison of our
entity-over-class analysis in Sec. 5.3: three TFM backbones lose 8.5–9.2 points of MSP AUROC on
new entities, whereas CatBoost and TabM lose 5.1 and 2.1.
OOD detection and its evaluation. Existing OOD benchmarks share entities between training
and test, so they never ask a detector to accept a new entity while rejecting an unknown class.
OpenOOD [40, 43] evaluates confidence-based and distance-based detectors against a fixed training
set, and methods based on outlier exposure [15], outlier synthesis [5] or weakly augmented variational
autoencoders [38] additionally train the network, which a frozen in-context model does not allow.
Full-spectrum detection [42] accepts covariate shift and rejects semantic shift, but it defines both
shifts relative to a fixed training set, not to a context that a frozen model reads at inference. Tabular
evaluations either use random splits [3, 10] or treat a new patient group as OOD [1, 34]. NEO-Tab in
Sec. 3.2 measures the setting that these evaluations leave out.
Calibration with held-out data. Mondrian conformal prediction [36] and leave-group-out cross-
validation [28] also hold groups out, to calibrate prediction sets per group or to estimate performance
on unseen groups, but both refit the model or need a held-out calibration set. Our context swaps
instead calibrate OOD scores for new entities and need nothing beyond forward passes of a frozen
model.
3
Open-Entity OOD Detection
3.1
Problem Definition
Rows (x, y, g) carry an entity g such as a user or a region. A time block also counts as an entity, and
we call the entity of a row its group. Given a context C, let Pfam and Pnew be the laws of ID rows
whose entity does or does not occur in C, and Qfam, Qnew those of OOD rows. For a score s (high
means OOD), AUROC(s; P, Q) = Pr(s(Z) > s(X)) with Z ∼Q, X ∼P independent; we assume
continuous scores, write GP for the CDF of s(X), X ∼P, and KS(P, P ′) = supt |GP (t)−GP ′(t)|.
Definition 1 (Open-entity OOD detection). An in-context detector scores each test row given a
labelled context C. In open-entity OOD detection, ID rows follow Pnew and rows of an unknown
class follow Qnew; the detector must accept the former, a covariate shift, and reject the latter, a
semantic shift. Its target is Anew,new = AUROC(s; Pnew, Qnew).
How differently a detector treats new and familiar ID entities is measured by the sensitivity S(s) =

3.2
Why Random-Split Evaluation Misses the Problem
Random-split evaluation of tabular OOD detection [3] measures another point of this square, for
three reasons. P1: familiar ID rows (π ≈0): a detector that ranks new entities as more OOD
is overestimated; a bound limits the gap by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between familiar
and new entities and shows that it requires S̸ = 50. P2: familiar OOD rows (τ < 1): on the six

Our protocol and the 14 tables of Sec. 5.1 form the NEO-Tab benchmark. The protocol measures
Anew,new directly: ID test rows come from the official test split (new entities or future), OOD rows
only from entities absent from the training split or from after its end, and no score is normalised
within the batch. Each trial is paired with a random-split trial of the same size on the same OOD
rows, and the two therefore differ only in the ID rows.
4
Context-Calibrated Detection
NEO-Tab addresses the entity-split gap in evaluation, and a detector evaluated on it needs a reference
for how known-class rows of new entities score. The context can supply one because to an in-context
TFM a new entity is simply one absent from the context. Holding a group of entities out of the context
and scoring its rows with the remaining context therefore yields known-class rows of new entities
from ID data alone. Module (1) of Fig. 3, Steps 1–2 of Sec. 4.1, turns these rows into a reference
distribution for every score.
The entity-over-class bias requires the confidence and each distance alike to be calibrated against
this new-entity reference, not against familiar rows. Calibration against familiar rows fails because
far from the context the confidence reverts to the prior whatever the class, as Prop. 2 states, and a
distance treats distance from the context as outlyingness by construction. The same swaps measure
how systematically a distance ranks new entities above or below familiar ones, and this directional
sensitivity sets its weight among the distances in module (2), Steps 3–4.
The entity–class trade-off rules out dropping either kind of evidence, because confidence and distances
fail in different places. A label-oriented output can discard the features in which an unknown class
differs, whereas distances also respond to new entities. The distance branch therefore keeps weight
one, and the confidence is added with a weight equal to how sharp it stays on held-out entities in
module (3), Steps 5–6. Sec. 4.2 states what each module guarantees.

5
Experiments
We organise the experiments around four research questions:
• RQ1: how much does random-split evaluation overestimate detectors?
• RQ2: how do the entity-over-class bias and the entity–class trade-off affect existing detectors
and backbones?
• RQ3: how does CCD compare with existing detectors on new entities?
• RQ4: which components of CCD contribute?
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. We use all six multiclass BeyondArena tasks with a grouped or temporal split whose
smallest class has at least 30 rows, four with grouped and two with temporal splits. We add ten tables
whose rows are grouped by an entity such as a subject or a course, with reserved test groups. All
sixteen tables follow five selection rules. R1 requires a multiclass target (≥3 classes), and R2 a
group structure, either an official grouped or temporal split or, in an IID table, a column that names
a real entity and is not the label. R3 keeps a table within the stated limits of TabPFN v2 (≤500
features, ≤10 known classes), the restriction TabArena applies to TabPFN v2 [6], and R4 requires
an OOD class, the smallest class with at least 30 rows. R5 excludes tables outside BeyondArena
that appear in the pretraining collections of TabDPT and Real-TabPFN-2.5. Applying R1–R4 to the
metadata of all 142 BeyondArena tasks [25] gives 25 multiclass tasks, of which 8 have an official
grouped or temporal split and 2 of the 17 IID ones have an entity column, the plate in sdss17 and
the course in students. After R3 removes micro_mass (1,082 features), all remaining nine pass R4,
and the six whose smallest class itself has at least 30 rows form the six-task study. The remaining
asp, whose smallest class has 21 rows and whose OOD class is therefore the smallest with at least
30, joins sdss17 and students as an entity table. Other public tables. Seven public tables outside
BeyondArena pass R1–R5 on their metadata and are added, and N-BaIoT also passes but is left out
for its size of about seven million rows. The OOD class is the smallest class with at least 30 rows
and never enters the context, and all ID and OOD test rows come from new groups. The backbone is
TabPFN v2, and the metrics are AUROC, AUPR and FPR95. The 14 tables that enter the held-out

5.3
RQ2: Diagnosis of Existing Detectors
New entities look unknown. With one context and one calibration per trial, 13 of 14 detectors score
ID rows of new groups as more OOD than held-out ID rows of familiar groups, and their AUROC
against the same OOD rows falls by up to 20.3 points, as Fig. 4 shows. Under the controlled protocol,
which redraws only the OOD test rows from new groups, the ID confidence of TabPFN v2 [16],
TabICLv2 [27] and TabDPT [23] falls by 5.6, 4.9 and 5.1 points on new groups, and their MSP
AUROC by 8.5, 8.6 and 9.2 points, against 5.1 for CatBoost and 2.1 for TabM, as Fig. 6 shows. The
MSP AUROC drop is largest on mice (37.7 points) and covertype (10.2).
Mechanism. On cardiotocography, the TFM confidence on correctly classified ID rows falls with
distance to the context more steeply than for trees or RealMLP (−0.21 for TabPFN versus at most
−0.09). This decline is also steeper for the TFM on ghanas and mice. These declines are consistent
with the confidence encoding familiarity to the context, which a new entity lacks. The trade-off
involves a different failure, high confidence on unknown-class rows. On consumer, covertype and
dementia the relation reverses, and rows far from the context receive higher confidence. The output
scores therefore fall below chance (TabPFN MSP AUROC 13–23), while a feature-space kNN reaches
83–86 on consumer and dementia. Label-free detectors share the entity-over-class bias but not this
failure: they treat a new entity as an outlier. With the detector fixed, kNN and LOF lose 7.3 and 10.0
points of AUROC from familiar to new ID rows and the two embedding scores 15.7 and 20.3. The
two families thus fail in different places, which CCD exploits by combining them.

5.5
RQ4: Design Analysis
Ablation. The ablation in Tab. 3 shows that both branches and the fusion rule contribute to CCD.
Removing the label branch lowers the mean AUROC by 3.3 points, the label-subspace distance by
2.4 and the embedding distance by 1.4. Convex fusion of the confidence as in CCD-AW costs 2.4
points in line with Thm. 1(iii), and the constant θ is not critical. The contribution of each branch does
not depend on the TFM that produces the confidence and the embedding, because on the other two
backbones removing the label-subspace distance costs 2.4–3.1 points and removing the label branch
2.5–3.3 points.
Testable predictions. Three predictions of Sec. 4.2 hold on the development trials. The swap
estimates match their targets, as Prop. 1 predicts: the Spearman correlation over tables between ˆS
and S is 0.86–0.92. The lower bound on the confidence AUROC of Sec. 4.2 holds in all 77 table–trial
cells of the development trials, and the sharpness a correlates 0.78 with the AUROC of MSP. The
label-subspace distance is less sensitive than the feature-space kNN (|S −50| 12.9 versus 15.3), as
the invariance of d2 in Sec. 4.2 predicts.
6
Conclusion
Open-entity OOD detection asks a detector to accept the rows of new entities whose class is known
and to reject the rows of an unknown class. For an in-context TFM both can look equally unfamiliar,
because every score it produces is a function of the context. This paper turns that observation into an
evaluation and a method. NEO-Tab measures detection on new entities directly, and on it random-split
evaluation overestimates every detector tested and misranks them. Nearly all detectors also score the
known-class rows of new entities as more OOD than those of familiar entities. CCD holds groups
out of the context to obtain, from ID data alone, how known-class rows of new entities score. It
calibrates and weights three distances and the confidence against that reference before fusing them,
as the entity–class trade-off requires. CCD has the highest mean AUROC among 17 detectors on 14
grouped tables and gains 7.4 AUROC points over kNN†, a lead that holds on three backbones. Its
three testable predictions hold on the development trials. For a Web service whose rows come from
new users or merchants, this means an OOD detector that is evaluated under the entity shift it will
meet in deployment and runs on a frozen TFM without requiring outliers or retraining.
