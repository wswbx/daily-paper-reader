Variable Importance Recovery in Tabular Foundation
Models: A Simulation Study∗
Vasil Yasenov, PhD†
Nicholas Adams-Cohen, PhD‡
November 20, 2026
Abstract
In machine learning, identifying which variables contribute predictive information
can be as important as predictive accuracy. Tabular foundation models have matched
or exceeded the predictive performance of tuned gradient boosting trees on many small-
and medium-sized tabular datasets. Yet their ability to recover population-level variable
importance has received little attention. We design and conduct a Monte Carlo simula-
tion study to compare variable importance recovery across tabular foundation models
and established machine learning baselines. Our framework distinguishes multiple pop-
ulation estimands, pairs each one with an aligned model-agnostic importance estimator,
and evaluates recovery in terms of ranking, support, and relative magnitude. Our
results show that tabular foundation models are generally competitive with established
machine-learning baselines in recovering population variable importance, and often
more accurate. We thus extend the evidence for their capabilities to a previously
underexamined dimension, further strengthening their appeal.
Keywords: Variable importance; tabular foundation models; interpretability; Shapley
values; simulation study; gradient boosting.

Introduction
A recent class of predictive methods, known as tabular foundation models (TFMs), uses
labeled observations as context to form predictions without task-specific parameter estimation.
On many small- and medium-sized tabular datasets, these methods match or outperform
the predictive accuracy of gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) without task-specific
hyperparameter tuning (Hollmann et al. 2025; Grinsztajn et al. 2026; Qu et al. 2026; Ma et
al. 2025; Kong and Das 2026). Yet predictive accuracy is not the only consideration in many
applied settings. Policymakers and other decision-makers may also need to understand and
defend a model’s reliance on its inputs, and in some contexts may be willing to accept lower
predictive performance in exchange for greater interpretability. One important component
of this objective is identifying the variables that are most predictive of an outcome and
quantifying their relative importance. Whether TFMs can accurately recover such importance
remains largely unknown.
Existing work applies SHAP to TFMs and develops pretrained attribution models, expanding
the tools available for assessing feature contributions (Hollmann et al.
2025; Fonseca
and Stoyanovich 2026; Sena and Azevedo 2026). Related studies evaluate how faithfully
attribution methods explain a fitted model (Agarwal et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023). An
attribution may faithfully describe a fitted model’s reliance on a variable without accurately
reflecting that variable’s predictive value in the population. These contributions advance
attribution methodology, but do not establish how TFMs compare with established learners
in recovering population-level variable importance.
This paper compares TFMs with established machine learning predictive baselines in their
ability to recover population level variable importance. We design and conduct a Monte
Carlo simulation study with known data-generating processes and evaluate recovery against
population level ground truth. Our framework distinguishes four distinct population esti-
mands from the importance of a fitted procedure and its finite-sample estimate. We pair
each population target with an aligned model-agnostic importance estimator and evaluate
importance recovery in terms of ranking, support, and relative magnitude. More broadly,
this paper provides a framework for evaluating population variable importance recovery
across AI-based prediction methods.
Our simulations show that TFMs are generally competitive with established machine-
learning baselines in recovering population variable importance and often perform better.
The size and consistency of these advantages depend on the definition of importance and
the simulation setting, with some exceptions. The clearest gains arise when importance
measures a variable’s predictive value on its own: both TFMs outperform the boosted-
tree baselines in identifying important variables and recovering their rankings and relative
contributions. When importance measures the additional predictive value beyond the other
inputs, TFMs also improve average ranking and magnitude recovery, although identification
of the important variables is more often comparable. These findings complement the evidence
on predictive accuracy, suggesting that strong TFM predictions need not come at the expense
of reliable variable-importance recovery. Overall, they strengthen the case for considering
TFMs.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature and
positions our study in context. Section 3 develops the conceptual framework, defining the
population importance targets, their corresponding estimators, and the recovery metrics.

discusses implications for model choice, limitations, and directions for future research. The

Literature Review
Research on TFMs increasingly extends from prediction to explanation. Predictive bench-
marks compare TFMs with GBDTs across many datasets (Qu et al. 2025, 2026; Erickson et
al. 2025). Interpretability studies also compare TFM explanations with classical learners,
using SHAP (Lundberg and Lee 2017) or attention-based importance on applied datasets
and synthetic data with known informative variables (Hollmann et al. 2025; Kolberg et al.
2025). Other work uses in-context learning for Shapley and LOCO computation, including
conditional Shapley estimation (Rundel et al. 2024; Olsen and Christensen 2026). Pretrained
attribution models estimate explanations from data without access to a fitted predictor
(Fonseca and Stoyanovich 2026) or jointly produce predictions and explanations (Sena and
Azevedo 2026). Together, these approaches expand the evidence and tools for interpreting
TFMs. Evaluating population recovery additionally requires an explicit importance target
and an appropriate evaluation criterion.
Variable importance is not a single quantity: different definitions capture different as-
pects of predictive information, particularly when features are dependent. Population-level
frameworks define algorithm-agnostic importance through optimal predictive performance
(Williamson and Feng 2020; Williamson et al. 2021, 2023). LOCO measures additional
predictive value beyond the remaining covariates (Lei et al. 2018; Verdinelli and Wasserman
2024a), whereas Shapley effects allocate explained variance (Song, Nelson, and Staum 2016);
feature dependence affects their interpretation (Verdinelli and Wasserman 2024b). Work on
observational and interventional Shapley explanations, removal-based methods, and marginal
versus conditional associations further clarifies these choices (Chen et al. 2020; Covert, Lund-
berg, and Lee 2020, 2021; Ewald et al. 2024). Permutation importance instead measures
fitted-model reliance (Fisher, Rudin, and Dominici 2019), and unrestricted permutation can
force extrapolation by breaking feature dependencies (Hooker, Mentch, and Zhou 2021).
These distinctions make estimator–target alignment essential when comparing recovery across
predictive procedures.
Simulation benchmarks offer complementary evaluation tools, ranging from synthetic data
with computable reference quantities to broader assessments of attribution faithfulness (Liu
et al. 2021; Agarwal et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023). Controlled local-attribution benchmarks
also evaluate rank recovery and noise-feature mass against analytically or numerically
derived reference attributions (Katakam 2026). Identifying designated informative variables,
explaining a fitted model, and recovering a population importance quantity remain distinct
evaluation objectives.
Our study combines these ideas to compare population variable-importance recovery across
TFMs, gradient-boosted trees, and elastic net. We hold the importance procedures fixed
across predictive models, match each to its population target, and evaluate support, rank,
and relative magnitude recovery under known data-generating processes. This design shows
how model comparisons depend on the importance definition, feature dependence, response
function, and recovery criterion.

3.2.5
Discussion
The four definitions in Equations (2)–(5) measure distinct quantities and need not agree.
Marginal importance credits correlated proxies, conditional importance measures unique
information, Shapley importance distributes shared information, and structural importance
records only whether a variable is required. Even with independent covariates, interactions
can produce different rankings. Under squared loss, the continuous estimands coincide only
in very special cases such as additive models with independent covariates.
For a simple example, let Z, W, U be independent standard normal variables, let Q =
ρZ +
p
1 −ρ2 U be a proxy with |ρ| < 1, and let Y = ZW + ε with independent mean-zero
noise. Among the observed covariates (Z, W, Q), only Z and W are structurally important,
while all three marginal importance values are zero. Their conditional importance values are
1 −ρ2, 1, and 0, respectively. Shapley importance can nevertheless allocate positive credit
to Q when ρ̸ = 0, because it helps predict the interaction when W, but not Z, is observed.
Thus, recovering the interaction’s active variables and recovering marginal importance are
different tasks, and assigning shared Shapley credit to a proxy is not an error.
The relevant estimand depends on the decision the analysis is intended to inform. For
instance, structural importance suits settings in which the primary question is whether a
variable belongs in the predictive signal at all, irrespective of the magnitude of its incremental
contribution. Marginal importance is instead useful for screening variables, assessing the
predictive content of a low-cost measurement on its own, or identifying variables that can
serve as proxies when the full feature set is unavailable. Conditional importance is more
6


appropriate for incremental measurement or data-acquisition decisions. Shapley importance
is preferable when correlated measurements jointly encode the same construct and credit
must be allocated across them.

3.3.5
Discussion
Marginal refitting and LOCO compare models fitted on different feature sets; SAGE allocates
the predictive performance of the full fitted model. Their paired population targets are
marginal, conditional, and Shapley importance, respectively.
All procedures use n observations for fitting and a separate, independently generated sample
to evaluate prediction losses and variable importance. For TFMs, fitting supplies labeled
context without updating pretrained weights; LOCO removes Xj from both the context
and the evaluation inputs. For tunable procedures, we select hyperparameters once per
replication on the full feature set and reuse them when refitting restricted models, avoiding
the computational cost of a separate cross-validated search for each feature subset. This
protocol holds the selected settings fixed across refits, but settings appropriate for the full
model need not be appropriate for every restricted prediction problem. Appendix A.3 gives
the tuning protocol.

4
Simulation Design
We generate repeated training samples from controlled data-generating processes for which
the population importance vector ψ is known analytically. This section specifies the covariate
distribution and variable roles, the response functions, the scenario grid, the sample sizes,
and the ground truth computation needed to reproduce the design. The design varies sample
size, feature count, within-pair correlation, and the response function. Estimator settings,
model protocol, realized Monte Carlo precision and other details are deferred to Appendix A.
4.1
Predictive Algorithms
We evaluate two tabular foundation models, TabPFN-3 and TabICLv2, against three gradient-
boosted tree implementations, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost, and an elastic-net
baseline. We selected the TFMs for their prominence in tabular prediction, reflected in
their popularity and citation counts, and the classical baselines for their widespread use in
practice. All six procedures are paired with the same model-agnostic importance estimators
rather than their native importance scores.
Computational cost materially constrained the simulation design. Importance estimation
requires repeated model fitting or prediction across feature subsets, making a broad Monte
Carlo comparison particularly costly for TFMs. We therefore balanced scenario coverage
against tuning depth, estimator coverage, and Monte Carlo precision: we limited tuning,
reduced the TabPFN-3 ensemble, used 50 replications per scenario, evaluated SAGE only
at p = 10, and restricted LOCO feature evaluation when n = 200, p = 50. These trade-
offs mean that our results compare the specified computational protocols, rather than
the best attainable performance of each model family. Furthermore, each boosted-tree
procedure receives a random search over ten hyperparameter configurations with five-fold
cross-validation, and TabPFN-3 uses a four-member ensemble rather than the default eight.
Software, model settings, and tuning protocols are given in Appendix A.3.
4.2
Covariates and Variable Roles
Covariates are drawn as X ∼N(0, Σ) with unit marginal variances, Σjj = 1. Every covariate
receives one of four construction labels:
• signal: a base variable that enters f ∗, directly or through its average with a redundant
variable. These variables let us assess recovery of genuine response drivers.
• null: independent of every other covariate and absent from f ∗. They test whether
estimators avoid assigning importance to irrelevant variables.
• redundant: an additional variable paired with a signal; both enter f ∗through their aver-
age, so swapping them leaves f ∗unchanged. They test whether estimators distinguish
shared predictive information from a variable’s unique contribution.
• proxy: an additional variable paired with a signal but absent from f ∗; it is correlated
with the signal when ρ > 0. They test whether estimators distinguish predictive infor-
mation carried through correlation from direct involvement in the response function.

4.4
Scenario Grid
The grid crosses n ∈{200, 1000}, p ∈{10, 50}, ρ ∈{0, 0.5, 0.9}, and the four response
functions, giving 48 scenarios. Covariate-role counts are determined by p, not independently
crossed. For each scenario we compute the relevant structural, marginal, conditional, and
Shapley population targets. All scenarios above keep n and p within the published context
limits of the two primary TFMs (TabPFN-3 and TabICLv2), so neither model needs to
subsample rows to fit within context.
We completed B = 50 Monte Carlo replicates for all grid scenarios, reflecting the compute
cost of repeated full-context TFM fits and refits. Within each replicate, an independent
evaluation sample of size m = 2000 is drawn fresh from P and used both to compute test-
sample prediction error and to estimate importance, bϕ. The training sample Dn is redrawn
independently each replicate. SAGE uses a random 128-point subset of this evaluation
sample.
The primary evaluation metric for prediction is normalized excess risk ( bR −σ2)/ Var(Y ),
where bR is the mean squared prediction loss of the full fitted model on this evaluation sample
and σ2 is the noise variance. SAGE settings and Monte Carlo precision are documented in
Appendix A.
4.5
Ground Truth Computation
v(S) = Var(E[Y | XS]) is computed analytically using Gaussian conditional moments. The
additive responses decompose into independent signal-block contributions; each interaction
combines two independent blocks. Shapley additivity therefore reduces the population game
to local terms involving at most four covariates, even at p = 50. Exact local coalition
enumeration preserves known ties and zeros rather than ranking numerical integration noise.
Independent nulls receive zero importance under all targets; proxies have zero conditional
importance.
We use 40,000 auxiliary Monte Carlo draws to estimate signal and outcome variances for
noise calibration and normalization; the importance targets are computed analytically.
Appendix A.8 derives the Gaussian moment identities and exact local Shapley calculation,
and describes calibration and validation.

5.1
Structural Importance
Figure 1 contains four vertically stacked panels for the linear, threshold, nonlinear, and

Mean F1 scores are shown as the colored markers and medians as black crosses. Bars are

Overall, Figure 1 indicates broadly comparable structural recovery for the TFMs and boosted

substantially weaker for nonlinear and interaction-only responses (mean LOCO F1: 0.517

Discussion
This paper asks whether tabular foundation models can compete with established machine-
learning baselines in recovering population variable importance. Our Monte Carlo framework
makes this comparison systematic by distinguishing population targets from fitted-model
estimates, matching targets and estimators, and evaluating support, rank, and relative
magnitude recovery against known ground truth. The results show broad competitiveness,
with clearer marginal-recovery advantages and more comparable performance on other
criteria, although conditional support recovery is lower in a highly correlated interaction
setting. Alongside their predictive performance, these findings show that the strengths of
this new generation of models extend to an important, previously underexamined aspect of
model interpretation.
These findings do not make TFMs the preferred choice for every application. Population
variable-importance recovery does not measure human comprehensibility or transparency
of the model itself. Requirements for an inspectable functional form, explicit coefficients,
or a sparse specification such as elastic net can favor established models regardless of their
importance-recovery performance. Computational cost, fitting and inference speed, scalability,
tuning effort, and deployment requirements also matter, as do reproducibility, robustness,
calibration, and uncertainty quantification. Established baselines may offer advantages on
these dimensions, which our study does not benchmark. Model choice therefore depends
on the combination of predictive performance, explanation capabilities, and operational
requirements that fits the application.
Our study has important limitations. The evidence is restricted to continuous Gaussian
covariates with controlled dependence and four regression response functions, rather than
16


the full diversity of applied tabular data. We evaluate two TFMs and three importance
estimators under specific fitting and computational protocols, so the comparisons should not
be interpreted as isolating architectural advantages or establishing optimal performance for
each model family. Reusing full-model hyperparameters may disadvantage classical baselines
when the appropriate model complexity changes with the feature set, particularly for single-
variable boosted-tree refits that retain the full-model tree count without further early stopping.
Some of the observed TFM advantages in marginal and conditional importance recovery
may therefore reflect this protocol; our experiments do not establish how these comparisons
would change under subset-specific tuning. SAGE uses finite evaluation data and Monte
Carlo approximations with a drift-based stopping rule, which limits the resolution of small
Shapley-recovery differences. Computational constraints also restrict the evaluated scope.
Future work should extend the simulations to include categorical and missing covariates, more
complex dependence and interactions, heterogeneous noise, and distribution shifts, alongside
larger datasets. Evaluating estimated conditional distributions and tuning performed sep-
arately for each feature subset would help separate model performance from explanation
and fitting protocols. Using the known covariate distribution also leaves open how recovery
changes when that distribution must be estimated. A distinct extension concerns causality:
our targets describe predictive information, not effects of intervening on variables. Explicit
causal structures could support evaluation of causally relevant variables or intervention-based
quantities, but would require corresponding causal estimands and identification assumptions.

A.2
Estimator Settings
• SAGE: the permutation-sampling estimator of Covert et al. (2020), with conditional
feature removal via the Gaussian conditional law, using 128 evaluation points and 64
conditional draws per coalition. A minimum of 128 permutations is required. Every
32 permutations thereafter, the maximum coordinatewise standard deviation of the
last 32 running estimates is compared with 5% of their current cross-feature range;
a zero range is replaced by one for this comparison. Sampling stops when this drift
criterion holds or at 1024 permutations. This is a computational stopping heuristic,
not a Monte Carlo standard-error or convergence certificate. The growing coalitions
within each sampled permutation are scored in one batched forward pass. SAGE is
restricted to p = 10 for every procedure because p = 50 was too costly; the other
importance estimators retain both feature dimensions.
• LOCO: one full fit and p leave-one-covariate-out fits per replicate, subject to the scope
restriction below. The full fit is shared with prediction evaluation and SAGE.
A.3
Model Protocol
• TFMs (TabPFN-3 and TabICLv2): pinned software versions (tabpfn 8.2.0 and tabicl
2.1.1) and checkpoints fixed across the run, with software and configuration recorded
in the durable checkpoint manifest; no task-specific tuning. TabPFN-3 is selected
explicitly via ModelVersion.V3. To limit computation, TabPFN-3 uses a reduced
ensemble (n_estimators=4, auto-scaling disabled) in place of the package default of 8:
a paired sensitivity check at both an easy and a hard p = 50 cell found the change in
importance recovery negligible relative to Monte Carlo error, while roughly halving the
ensemble’s compute. TabICLv2 uses its default checkpoint and eight-member ensemble,
with its key–value cache enabled only for the full fitted model. TabPFN-3’s fit cache is
28


disabled. Both use the full training sample as context at n ∈{200, 1000}, within each
model’s published context limits.
• Query-batch sensitivity: TabPFN’s fingerprint preprocessing can amplify small floating-
point differences when the same query appears in different batches.
The frozen
production settings, including fingerprinting and batched SAGE prediction, were
retained throughout all 50 replicates.
A GPU diagnostic on one easy linear and
one hard interaction draw at n = 200, p = 10 reproduced their saved SAGE vectors
exactly. Holding each fitted draw and sampled coalition inputs fixed, separate-coalition
prediction and an alternative full-coalition calculation changed normalized recovery
RMSE by less than 5.1 × 10−5, with unchanged support recovery and feature ranks.
This two-draw screen does not establish general batch invariance or assess marginal-refit
or LOCO recovery sensitivity.
• XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost: random search over 10 configurations with 5-fold cross-
validation on the full Dn. Each tuning fold allows up to 1000 trees with early-stopping
patience of 30 rounds. For the selected configuration, the median selected tree count
across folds is converted to an integer and clipped to [50, 1000]. This count and the other
selected hyperparameters are reused for the full fit and all marginal-refit and LOCO
restricted fits, without further tuning or early stopping. Tuning space is max depth
∈{3, . . . , 10}, learning rate ∈log-uniform(0.01, 0.3), and subsample/column-subsample
∈(0.5, 1). LightGBM retains the default maximum of 31 leaves per tree.
• Elastic net: the regularization path and α ∈{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} plus α = 0.001 in
place of pure ridge (α = 0), chosen by 5-fold cross-validation on the full Dn and
reused, without retuning, for all marginal-refit and LOCO restricted fits. Covariates
are standardized using training-sample means and scales.
• Classical software: XGBoost 3.4.0, LightGBM 4.7.0, CatBoost 1.2.10, and scikit-learn
1.9.0.
• Seeds: replicate r combines the master seed 10000+r with a stable hash of the semantic
scenario identifier. Data generation, each procedure fit, and each importance estimator
receive separate label-derived streams, so results are invariant to grid ordering, filtering,
the requested value of B, and the presence or failure of other procedures or estimators
in the TFM continuation. The GBDT and TFM runs are persisted separately but use
the same data-generation and noise-calibration seeds. Within each scenario, replicate r
therefore has identical training and evaluation data and the same feature permutation
in both stores. Method comparisons are paired at the scenario–replicate level, although
the reported summaries do not use paired differences.
• Failed fits: a non-convergent or errored fit is excluded from that procedure’s results for
that replicate only. Fit and estimator failure rates are recorded separately; affected
cells are omitted when the corresponding failure rate exceeds 2%.
A.4
Scope Restriction for p = 50 LOCO
Full LOCO (all p leave-one-covariate-out fits) is used for every p = 10 scenario and for p = 50
scenarios at n = 1000. For p = 50 at n = 200, LOCO is restricted to the covariates with a
defined structural role (signal, redundant, or proxy — at most psig + nred + nproxy variables
under Table A1) plus a fixed random sample of 10 null variables, rather than refitting on
29


every null variable. Null variables are exchangeable under the design, and random feature
permutation makes the first ten nulls in feature-index order a random subset. This reduces
computation but changes the scoring universe: support selection, off-support mass, and
magnitude normalization use the evaluated covariates only, with no extrapolation to unscored
nulls. These diagnostics are not identical to their full-p counterparts. Results tables and
heterogeneity figures flag the restriction.
A.5
Scope Restriction for Rank Recovery
Spearman and Kendall’s τb (Section 3.4.2) are computed over non-null variables only, rather
than over all p. Null variables have ψj = 0 exactly and represent 60% of the covariates in
every scenario (Table A1); a correlation computed over the full vector is then dominated by
whether the null block is pushed as a group toward the bottom of the ranking, obscuring
the ordering among non-null covariates. Restricting J to non-null variables removes this
tie-block dominance while retaining proxies, whose target importance can be positive or zero
depending on the estimand. Support and magnitude recovery retain evaluated nulls and
proxies, subject to the restricted LOCO universe described above.

Secondary Recovery Metrics and Figures
We considered three alternatives to the main-text recovery metrics: off-support mass,
Kendall’s τb, and normalized mean absolute deviation (MAD). These diagnostics use the
same grid scenarios, B = 50 replication budget, scope restrictions, and undefined-value
handling as the main analysis. Their numerical results appear alongside the main metrics in
Appendix B.
Off-support mass measures the proportion of absolute estimated importance assigned outside
the matched target’s strictly positive support, within the evaluated universe I:

This is target-specific off-support mass, not necessarily mass assigned to variables having the

composition of a selected set.
Kendall’s τb is a tie-adjusted measure of concordance between estimated and population
importance rankings. Like Spearman correlation, it is computed over the non-null variables
with an available estimate, retaining proxies (Appendix A.5).

These alternatives preserve the broad qualitative conclusions of the main recovery analy-
sis: both TFMs outperform the boosted-tree benchmarks in marginal recovery, whereas
conditional and Shapley comparisons remain response- and criterion-dependent. Individual
method orderings are not identical across metrics; in particular, allocating little importance
