Abstract
Symbolic models offer interpretability, but their construction through generational evolution can be computation-
ally expensive. This article presents Random Symbolic Boosting (RSB), a method that constructs explicit additive
symbolic models via boosting over populations of randomly generated syntax trees, eliminating the need for gener-
ational evolution. The idea combines two observations: high-dimensional spaces can be spanned by enough linearly
independent vectors, and the semantics of syntax trees can be directly manipulated as such vectors. It is shown that,
for Ordinary Least Squares, the boosting process guarantees a monotonic reduction of the training error for both re-
gression and classification. Furthermore, an adaptive geometric early stopping mechanism is introduced. It is based on
the angle between the prediction and target vectors, is independent of the problem’s scale, and requires no adjustments
across tasks. Evaluated on a standard benchmark for symbolic regression, the method is located at the Pareto-optimal
point between predictive accuracy and training time, ranking first on the real-world datasets. Additionally, it was
tested on a large tabular classification benchmark with 200 problems, achieving competitive results.
Keywords: Symbolic Regression, Gradient Boosting, Semantic Genetic Programming, Early Stopping
1. Introduction
There are two main approaches in data modeling
through supervised learning (Rudin, 2019). The first,
and most popular, is the use of black-box models, such
as neural networks or decision-tree ensemble methods,
which are fast and accurate, but their internal logic is
not directly interpretable. The second approach seeks
interpretability through symbolic models, such as Ge-
netic Programming (GP) (Koza, 1994) or its seman-
tic variant, Geometric Semantic Genetic Programming
(GSGP) (Moraglio et al., 2012), but the generational,
population-based process of these methods is computa-
tionally expensive. In black-box models, optimization is
usually restricted to tuning model parameters or finding
decision thresholds, which is more efficient than evolv-
ing symbolic structures. However, this can lead to local
optima that are only corrected by changing the model
(Elsken et al., 2019).

To address these limitations, the Random Symbolic
Boosting (RSB) method is proposed.
Although the
modification or elimination of standard generational
evolution has been explored by previous approaches,
such as Fast Function Extraction (FFX) (McConaghy,
2011) and Kaizen Programming (KP) (de Melo, 2014),
RSB achieves state-of-the-art performance using a
boosting scheme (Friedman, 2001) that fits linear com-
binations of the semantics of stochastically generated
syntax trees. This reduces the computational cost by re-
placing GP’s evolutionary search with an optimization
over the residuals. The result is an explicit symbolic
model that achieves competitive accuracy on regression
and classification tasks.
Furthermore, an early stopping mechanism based on
the geometry of the semantic subspace is introduced.
Instead of evaluating an unbounded metric to decide
when to stop, the angle formed between the target and
the prediction is calculated, which, in addition to being
bounded, is applicable to supervised learning tasks.

mance, respectively. Finally, Sections 6 and 7 discuss

2. Related Work
GP is the classical method for generating symbolic
models through evolution (Koza, 1994). However, it
often suffers from convergence problems. GSGP ad-
dresses this by working directly on the semantics of the
solutions, inducing a unimodal error surface (Moraglio
et al., 2012). GSGP can also be seen as building each
offspring as a combination of its parents’ semantics
(Moraglio et al., 2012), and Dick (2024) interprets it
as ensemble learning, linking semantic mutation and
crossover to boosting and stacking. Semantic backprop-
agation GP (SBP-GP) (Virgolin et al., 2019) also op-
erates on semantics, but defines its variation operators
by inverting program execution to obtain the desired se-
mantics at selected nodes (Pawlak et al., 2015). Hybrid
methods such as Multiple Regression Genetic Program-
ming (MRGP) (Arnaldo et al., 2014) and MAP-Elites
Genetic Programming (MEGP) (Zhang et al., 2023) in-
tegrate linear regression and diversity maps, respec-
tively, with the goals of improving generalization ca-
pability and enhancing search space exploration. Along
the lines of MRGP, Parallel Multidimensional Genetic
Programming with Multidimensional Populations for
Symbolic Regression (M5GP) (Cárdenas Florido et al.,
2024) focuses on evolving multidimensional feature
transformations through a stack-based representation
with GPU parallel processing, where the new seman-
tic dimensions are subsequently evaluated by a multiple
linear regression model to produce the final prediction.
Nevertheless, all these variants still rely on evolutionary
search to obtain their solutions.
Beyond evolving a single solution, empirical evi-
dence suggests that the aggregation of multiple sim-
ple models frequently outperforms algorithms that
search for a single complex solution (Grinsztajn et al.,
2022). In symbolic regression, algorithms such as FFX
(McConaghy, 2011), which is deterministic, and KP
(de Melo, 2014), which uses a collaborative evolution-
ary approach, build models through the sum of ba-
sis functions. Specifically, FFX performs a systematic
and exhaustive expansion of basis functions that it sub-
sequently prunes via deterministic minimization with
regularization (Pathwise Regularized Learning). Sim-
ilarly, Evolutionary Feature Synthesis (EFS) (Arnaldo
et al., 2015) evolves nonlinear features bottom-up and
selects a sparse subset via pathwise regularized regres-
sion, yielding a readable linear combination of com-
posed features without a symbolic tree representation.
By contrast, KP initializes a team of experts and ap-
plies statistical significance tests, based on p-values and
coefficient magnitude thresholds, to filter terms and de-
termine the contribution of each. This follows the same
logic as decision tree-based methods like Random For-
est or XGBoost (Grinsztajn et al., 2022), where the er-
ror is reduced through the aggregation of multiple mod-
els. Symbolic Regression-based Random Forest (SR-
Forest) (Zhang et al., 2024) attempts to combine both
approaches by constructing a forest of hybrid models;
each consists of a Ridge regression that linearly com-
bines a set of syntax trees, and a random decision tree
applied to the residuals of this regression. The algo-
rithm applies evolutionary operators to the sets of syn-
tax trees, using a bagging strategy to iteratively retain
and combine complementary models.
The integration of semantic and boosting tech-
niques has also been explored by Symbolic-regression
boosting (SyRBo) (Sipper and Moore, 2021); this
method modifies standard gradient boosting by replac-
ing traditional weak estimators with GP-based sym-
bolic regressors. Similarly, Semantic Boosting Regres-
sion (SBR) (Leite and Schoenauer, 2025) sequentially
aggregates Memetic Semantic Genetic Programming
(MSGP) (Ffrancon and Schoenauer, 2015) models, us-
ing semantic backpropagation for local refinement.
A limitation of most methods is the computational
cost inherent to the structural search and evaluation. For
example, La Cava et al. (2021) reports average times
close to 105 seconds to generate a model with MRGP,
while in the case of SR-Forest, its authors report that the
process requires an average of 104 seconds. However,
Reservoir Computing (RC) (Lukoševiˇcius and Jaeger,
2009) demonstrates that it is not necessary to optimize
the internal structure of the model; it is sufficient to
project the data into a high-dimensional random space
and only fit the final output.
RSB integrates these principles; it generates a popu-
lation of random syntax trees that act as projections into
a semantic space. However, instead of using a static
reservoir, RSB builds the model step by step through a
linear combination optimized with boosting and deter-
ministically selects the sets of trees that best reduce the
residual error, following the additive logic of XGBoost.

3.3. Adaptive Geometric Early Stopping
The objective is to ensure that the updates contribute
significantly to the convergence of the model and to pre-
vent overfitting. To achieve this, an early stopping (ES)
mechanism is included, whose main novelty is the use
of the geometric angle θk between the predictions and
the targets as a convergence metric, combined with a
dynamic tolerance that adapts to the learning speed ob-
served during the initial levels. Unlike traditional stop-
ping criteria (Prechelt, 1998) based on the validation er-
ror, whose range depends on the scale of the problem, θk
is bounded within the interval [0, π], which allows defin-
ing relative and adaptive thresholds independent of the
dataset. This strategy distinguishes between stagnation
due to slow improvement and degradation due to over-
fitting. It allows reverting the model to the best previous
state. Angular quantities have previously been used to
design angle-driven geometric semantic variation oper-
ators in GP (Chen et al., 2019); here, θk is used instead
as a bounded early-stopping criterion on the validation
predictions, with adaptive tolerance and rollback.
The geometric alignment is calculated on a validation
set (Xval, yval). At iteration k, the angle θk is defined by

4. Experimental Analysis
This section analyzes the internal behavior of RSB
before comparing its performance with other state-of-
the-art methods. The datasets employed are described,
followed by a robustness study showing how the algo-
rithm converges globally from different random initial-
izations. Hyperparameter sensitivity is also evaluated
using functional analysis of variance (fANOVA), mea-
suring its impact on accuracy and training time. Feature
importance is then compared with tree-based ensemble
approaches. The section ends with an ablation of reg-
ularization and early-stopping criteria, which motivates
the configuration used in Section 5.

4.3. Population Size: The √n Scaling Rule
The selection of hyperparameters depends on the spe-
cific problem, but M exhibits the greatest variation.
Since n linearly independent vectors are sufficient to
generate all of Rn, the number of semantic columns M
must be less than n when λ = 0. This restriction is nec-
essary to prevent the subspace from being able to mem-
orize noise, thus preventing overfitting. To achieve this
in a scalable manner, M = m· √n is defined; this ensures
a sublinear growth where only the constant factor m is
adjusted, regardless of the dataset size; when λ > 0, the
Elastic Net regularization complements this criterion by
directly penalizing the coefficients.

Lasso and ENet, and in time on two of six for Ridge,
four of six for Lasso, and five of six for ENet. The angle
thus favors accuracy, while the RMSE favors compact-
ness and speed.

6. Discussion
In the SRBench evaluation, RSB is located at the el-
bow of the Pareto-optimal front between accuracy and
training time; Operon is more accurate but consider-
ably slower, whereas linear models are faster but signif-
icantly less accurate. The proposed algorithm achieved
first place in average performance on the real-world
datasets and the third position overall.
On the other hand, in the TALENT benchmark, RSB
obtained the 11th place in binary classification and the
13th in multiclass classification against 31 state-of-the-
art algorithms. In multiclass classification, the perfor-
mance of RSB is located within a range comparable
to that of methods such as XGB and LGBM. In bi-
nary classification, although it ranks below these en-
sembles, RSB demonstrated superior performance to ar-
chitectures such as ResNet, Random Forest, and SVM,
establishing it as a competitive method.

7. Conclusions
This work presented RSB, a symbolic regression
method that avoids generational evolution by construct-
ing explicit additive models through boosting over pop-
ulations of random syntax trees. The proposal stems
from two observations: any subspace of Rn can be ap-
proximated given enough linearly independent vectors,
and GSGP has already demonstrated that the semantics
of syntax trees can be directly manipulated as vectors in
that space. Combining this idea with the additive logic
of ensemble methods, and with the efficiency of linear
solvers, is what gives rise to RSB. Under the Stork tax-
onomy, the method is similar to FFX, EFS, SBR, and
RC, but its sequential boosting mechanism and absence
of generational evolution differentiate it from them.
From a theoretical standpoint, it is shown that, when
λ = 0, the boosting guarantees the descent of the train-
ing error: in regression, the norm of the residual de-
creases for η ∈(0, 2), and in classification, the log-loss
does so for η ∈(0, 8). The angle-based early stopping
mechanism was introduced, based on the angle between
the prediction and the target. Its main advantage is that
θk ∈[0, π] regardless of the problem scale, which makes
it applicable to regression, binary and multiclass classi-
fication without the need for additional adjustments.
Regarding performance, the experiments on SR-
Bench showed that RSB achieves an accuracy compa-
rable to that of advanced symbolic methods such as
Operon and GP-GOMEA, but with significantly lower
training times. On TALENT, the algorithm remained
competitive against the leading state-of-the-art classifi-
cation models, including XGBoost and LGBM.
The clearest limitation of the method is the size of the
resulting models. Being an additive ensemble, the final
expression can become difficult to interpret when many
boosting levels are required, which distances it from the
interpretability typically sought in traditional symbolic
models.
As future work, it is proposed to explore post-hoc
symbolic simplification strategies to reduce the com-
plexity of the final expression without sacrificing accu-
racy.
