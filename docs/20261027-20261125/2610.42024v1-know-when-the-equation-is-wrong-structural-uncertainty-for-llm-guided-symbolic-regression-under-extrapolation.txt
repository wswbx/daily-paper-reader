KNOW WHEN THE EQUATION IS WRONG: STRUCTURAL UNCERTAINTY FOR
LLM-GUIDED SYMBOLIC REGRESSION UNDER EXTRAPOLATION
Mostafa Cham 1 Bayu Adhi Tama 1 Jianwu Wang 1
ABSTRACT
Large language models (LLMs) can help discover mathematical equations from data, but most LLM-guided
symbolic regression methods focus on a single best equation without quantifying uncertainty over alternative
functional forms. This is challenging because distinct equations can fit the observed data similarly while producing
substantially different extrapolations. Our experiments on the LLM-SRBench dataset show that standard conformal
intervals achieve nominal in-distribution coverage but frequently fail under extrapolation. To tackle this challenge,
we introduce LLM-SR-UQ, a framework that retains competing equation structures and uses their disagreement to
characterize uncertainty about the underlying functional form. We extend the standard fixed-width conformal
approach to quantify structural intervals, which measure disagreement among candidate equations and identify
where prediction intervals should widen. Extensive experiments show our framework substantially improves
coverage attainment by incorporating structural disagreement while preserving in-distribution coverage. Our
approach also shows good generalizability when it is applied to other symbolic-regression methods. The code for
this paper is available at https://github.com/MostafaCham/LLM-SR-UQ

INTRODUCTION
Symbolic regression aims to discover interpretable mathe-
matical equations that describe observed data and, ideally,
remain valid beyond the observed range. Recent advances
in large language models (LLMs) have improved symbolic
regression by enabling the generation and refinement of can-
didate equations using scientific context and iterative feed-
back (Shojaee et al., 2025a; Grayeli et al., 2024; Wang et al.,
2025; Xia et al., 2025). Despite these advances, current
LLM-guided symbolic regression systems typically focus
on identifying a single best equation, leaving an important
question unanswered: when several equations can explain
the observed data, how certain are we that the selected equa-
tion is the appropriate functional form, and how reliable are
its predictions outside the observed range?
Different mathematical equations can fit the observed data
equally well while representing different hypotheses about
the underlying relationship. These differences may be dif-
ficult to detect within the observed range but can lead to
substantially different predictions under extrapolation. As a
result, selecting only the highest-scoring equation can hide
whether it is uniquely supported by the data or is one of
several plausible alternatives. Classical symbolic regres-

selection, ensembles, and other approaches that retain infor-
mation about competing equations (Guimer`a et al., 2020;
Fasel et al., 2022; Hirsh et al., 2022; Bartlett et al., 2024).
However, quantifying such structural uncertainty remains
relatively unexplored in LLM-guided symbolic regression.
Evaluating the accuracy of a discovered equation is not suf-
ficient to establish the reliability of its uncertainty estimates.
Existing symbolic regression benchmarks primarily assess
point-prediction accuracy and symbolic recovery (La Cava
et al., 2021; Shojaee et al., 2025b). but these metrics do
not indicate whether prediction intervals contain the true
outcomes at the expected frequency. In particular, an equa-
tion may achieve low prediction error within the observed
range while producing unreliable uncertainty estimates un-
der extrapolation. For scientific applications, it is therefore
important to evaluate not only predictive accuracy but also
whether uncertainty estimates remain calibrated under the
conditions in which the equation is applied.
Conformal prediction offers a practical approach to estimat-
ing predictive uncertainty by constructing prediction inter-
vals from observed prediction errors (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei
et al., 2018; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2021). It provides finite-
sample marginal coverage guarantees when calibration and
test data are exchangeable, even if the underlying model
is incorrect. However, when applied to a single equation
selected through symbolic regression, conventional confor-
mal prediction does not explicitly account for alternative

equations discarded during the search. This limitation is
particularly important under extrapolation, where equations
that fit the observed data similarly may produce substan-
tially different predictions. Even when the coefficients of
a selected equation are well estimated, uncertainty about
its functional form may remain. Once competing equa-
tions have been discarded, their disagreement can no longer
inform the uncertainty estimate. Consequently, post-hoc cal-
ibration may produce reliable intervals within the observed
distribution while failing to capture structural ambiguity
under extrapolation.
To address these limitations, we introduce LLM-SR-UQ,
which retains competing equation structures throughout the
search rather than selecting a single best equation. Evidence-
weighted model averaging produces a point predictor, while
disagreement among retained equations determines how
uncertainty varies across inputs. We extend fixed-width
split-conformal intervals by normalizing held-out calibra-
tion errors using this structural scale and applying the re-
sulting conformal quantile to construct prediction intervals.
These structural intervals widen where candidate predictions
diverge and remain narrower where they agree.
We also test whether uncertainty-related feedback improves
equation discovery.
An optional LLM critic examines
fitting diagnostics and coefficient identifiability, with its
extrapolation-aware setting additionally using disagreement
among candidates on unlabeled inputs beyond the training
range. The critic guides proposals without accessing shifted
test data or altering retained candidates. Ablations compare
it with rule-based feedback.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows: (1)
Structural uncertainty framework. We introduce LLM-SR-
UQ, a framework that retains multiple candidate equation
structures during symbolic regression and combines their
disagreement with conformal calibration to construct input-
dependent prediction intervals that reflect ambiguity in the
underlying functional form (Section 3). (2) Evaluation of
uncertainty under extrapolation. We systematically evaluate
prediction accuracy, coverage, and interval sharpness under
both in-distribution (ID) and extrapolative settings. Our
experiments demonstrate that incorporating structural dis-
agreement improves empirical coverage under extrapolation,
including when applied to independent symbolic-regression
methods (Sections 3 and 5). (3) Calibration-aware search
feedback. We investigate whether uncertainty-related infor-
mation can improve equation discovery through controlled
ablations of rule-based feedback and an LLM-based critic.
Our results show that the critic does not improve calibration
or equation recovery beyond rule-based feedback, highlight-
ing the importance of retaining structural alternatives rather
than introducing additional LLM-guided search mechanisms
(Section 3 and Appendix C).

RELATED WORK
LLM-guided symbolic regression. Recent advances in
LLM-guided symbolic regression have focused on improv-
ing how mathematical equations are generated and refined
during search. LLM-SR (Shojaee et al., 2025a) introduced
LLM-driven program synthesis for equation discovery, with
performance evaluated through prediction accuracy and
equation recovery. Subsequent studies have explored differ-
ent search strategies, including reusable concept libraries
(Grayeli et al., 2024), in-context iteration (Merler et al.,
2024), reasoning over data and previous experience (Wang
et al., 2025), tool-using agents (Xia et al., 2025; Yang et al.,
2026), tree search (Saveliev et al., 2026), structure-guided
evolution (Xia et al., 2026), and hierarchical agent systems
(Zhao et al., 2026). Other studies have examined the role
of LLMs in this process, suggesting that improvements
may arise primarily from effective search control Xie et al.
(2026), while selecting equations from a fixed dictionary
can sometimes outperform LLM-based evolution Li (2026).
Despite these advances, the emphasis remains largely on im-
proving equation discovery and predictive accuracy rather
than quantifying uncertainty arising from alternative func-
tional forms.
Recent studies have addressed the reliability of LLM-guided
symbolic regression by improving equation robustness.
STRIDE combines critic–executor feedback with diversity-
preserving memory to improve out-of-distribution (OOD)
robustness (Su et al., 2026). LLM-PDESR addresses obser-
vation noise and memorization by evaluating candidate equa-
tions through a Pareto frontier (Du et al., 2026). However,
improving equation robustness does not establish whether
the resulting prediction intervals achieve reliable coverage
under extrapolation.
Structural uncertainty in classical symbolic regression.
Classical symbolic regression has long explored methods
for representing uncertainty over alternative equation struc-
tures. Ensemble-SINDy (Fasel et al., 2022) uses bootstrap
sampling to estimate the inclusion probabilities of candi-
date terms, while sparsifying-prior model discovery (Hirsh
et al., 2022) uses Bayesian priors to quantify uncertainty
over model terms. Other approaches, such as the Bayesian
machine scientist (Guimer`a et al., 2020) and exhaustive sym-
bolic regression (Bartlett et al., 2024), explore alternative
mathematical expressions through Bayesian sampling and
minimum description length, respectively. These methods
characterize uncertainty through quantities such as term-
inclusion probabilities, distributions over candidate expres-
sions, or relative model scores, rather than necessarily pro-
ducing prediction intervals with a specified coverage tar-
get. Building on this perspective, our work investigates
how disagreement among retained equations can be used
to construct calibrated prediction intervals in LLM-guided

symbolic regression, particularly under extrapolation.
Distribution-free calibration. Conformal prediction con-
structs prediction intervals with finite-sample marginal cov-
erage guarantees when calibration and test data are ex-
changeable (Vovk et al., 2005; Lei et al., 2018; Angelopou-
los & Bates, 2021). Weighted conformal prediction ex-
tends this approach to certain forms of covariate shift by
accounting for differences between the calibration and test
distributions (Tibshirani et al., 2019). In symbolic regres-
sion, Conformal-SINDy (Fasel, 2025) applies conformal
calibration to predictions from SINDy models. However,
to our knowledge, conformal calibration has not been sys-
tematically evaluated for LLM-guided symbolic regression
under extrapolation, where standard coverage guarantees
no longer apply. Existing approaches characterize uncer-
tainty over candidate structures or calibrate predictions from
fitted models, but these objectives are typically studied sep-
arately. Our work combines disagreement among retained
symbolic equations with conformal calibration to construct
input-dependent prediction intervals and evaluates their em-
pirical coverage under extrapolation.

METHODOLOGY
3.1
Problem setup
Given a dataset of observations {(xi, yi)}N
i=1, where xi ∈
Rd represents the input variables and yi is a scalar target,
together with brief descriptions of each variable, the goal
is to discover a closed-form equation describing their re-
lationship, and uncertainty measurements under both ID
and OOD. An equation is characterized by its structure
M, which defines its functional form, and its coefficients
θ = (c0, c1, . . .), which determine the numerical values
within that form. For example, M(x; θ) = c0x1e−c1x2 rep-
resents a structure with two unknown coefficients, c0 and
c1. For each candidate structure, these coefficients are es-
timated by minimizing the squared prediction error on the
training data. The greater challenge lies in selecting the
appropriate structure, since different functional forms may
fit the observations similarly well but behave differently un-
der extrapolation. Expressions differing only in coefficient
names are considered members of the same structural family.
The objective is to obtain both a point predictor ˆy(x) and
a prediction interval Cα(x) that reflects uncertainty arising
from competing equation structures.
3.2
Dataset
We use the LLM-SRBench dataset (Shojaee et al., 2025b)
and organize the data into separate subsets for equation fit-
ting, uncertainty calibration, and evaluation (Table 2). For
each problem, the training data are randomly divided into
Dfit (80%), used to fit and score candidate equations, and
Dcal-ID (20%), used to calibrate prediction intervals. We
evaluate the resulting models on two benchmark test sets:
Dtest, containing inputs within the training range (interpo-
lation), and DOOD, containing inputs beyond that range
(extrapolation). Both test sets are reserved for final evalua-
tion. In addition, we construct an unlabeled extrapolation
region, Dcal-EXT, just beyond the training range to examine
how candidate equations behave outside the observed data.
This region provides information about disagreement among
equations without revealing the true target values. Thus, no
extrapolation labels are used during equation search or un-
certainty calibration.
3.3
Overview
LLM-SR-UQ consists of an iterative equation search fol-
lowed by prediction and uncertainty calibration, organized
into four stages (Figure 1). In Stage A, an LLM proposes
candidate equation structures. In Stage B, each candidate
is fitted and scored, and the highest-scoring structures are
retained in a buffer with associated weights. In Stage C,
when enabled, a second LLM acts as a critic, examining nu-
merical diagnostics of the retained candidates and providing
feedback to guide subsequent proposals. Finally, in Stage
D, the retained equations are combined to form a single pre-
dictor, while their disagreement determines how prediction
intervals vary across the input space. Conformal calibration
then adjusts the overall interval width based on observed
prediction errors. To measure what each component con-
tributes, we switch Stage C on in four steps, which we call
rungs: A0 gives the proposer only the problem description;
A1 adds a fixed, rule-based summary of the current fits; A2
adds the critic; and A3 additionally shows the critic label-
free evidence about extrapolation from Dcal-EXT. All rungs
use the same budget of LLM calls (Section 4.4).
3.4
Stage A: proposing structures
At each proposal round, the LLM receives the problem
description and information about the input variables, and
generates K = 8 candidate equation structures with free
coefficients c0, c1, . . .. Starting with configuration A1, the
proposer also receives a fixed summary of the current search
results, including the leading equations and their fitting
errors, poorly identified coefficients, unused input variables,
and systematic residual patterns. In configurations A2 and
A3, the latest critic guidance is additionally provided to help
refine subsequent proposals. The proposer does not directly
access the numerical training data. The feedback rules and
prompt templates are provided in Appendices A and D.

3.6
Stage C: the critic
The critic examines the current search results and provides
feedback to guide subsequent equation proposals. Every
fifth language-model call is allocated to the critic instead of
the proposer. The critic receives numerical diagnostics of
the retained candidates, including residual patterns, poorly
identified coefficients, repeatedly proposed structural fami-
lies, and signs of search stagnation. In configuration A3, it
additionally receives label-free information from Dcal-EXT,
describing disagreement among the three leading equations
and changes in prediction-interval width beyond the ob-
served range.
Based on these diagnostics, the critic recommends struc-
tures to avoid, alternative directions to explore, and up to
three modifications of existing candidates. Each suggested
modification must be supported by the available diagnostics
and validated before use. Importantly, the critic only in-
fluences subsequent proposals; candidate retention remains
determined by the score ℓk. The critic’s output format and
validation rules are provided in Appendices A and D.

To assess the contribution of structural disagreement, we
compare two interval constructions centered on the same pre-
dictor ˆy(x). The proposed structural intervals (S), defined
in Equation 9, use the input-dependent structural spread
σS(x) to scale interval widths according to disagreement
among retained equations. In contrast, the split conformal
baseline (split) uses a constant unit scale, yielding absolute-
residual calibration scores si = |yi −ˆy(xi)| and intervals
of fixed width across inputs, determined by the conformal
quantile.
Both constructions use the same fitting and calibration data
and provide finite-sample marginal coverage guarantees
under exchangeability. Since they share the same point
predictor, their comparison isolates the effect of using struc-
tural disagreement to determine interval width. Structural
intervals are independent of the algorithm used to discover
candidate models. Given multiple fitted candidates, their
prediction disagreement provides an input-dependent un-
certainty scale that can be calibrated using held-out ob-
servations without modifying the underlying search pro-
cedure. We demonstrate this transfer with LLM-SR and
Ensemble-SINDy in Section 5.2. In all cases, we report the
highest-weight structure as the discovered equation, while
the retained ensemble is used for prediction and uncertainty
estimation.

EVALUATION AND EXPERIMENT SETUP
We evaluate prediction accuracy and uncertainty reliability
at the problem level, focusing on performance under ex-
trapolation. All prediction intervals target 90% coverage.
Because distribution shifts vary across domains, we report
domain-specific results alongside pooled summaries where
appropriate.
4.1
Accuracy
We evaluate prediction accuracy using median normalized
mean squared error (NMSE) on both in-distribution and out-
of-distribution test sets, along with accuracy-to-tolerance
(Acc0.1) and symbolic accuracy. The metric Accτ measures
the fraction of problems for which all test predictions have
relative errors within a specified tolerance τ. We use τ = 0.1
and count non-finite predictions as failures.
To evaluate symbolic accuracy (SA@k) we replace numer-
ical constants and free coefficients in the discovered and
reference equations with canonical placeholders, then com-
pare the resulting using SymPy. Because different symbolic
expressions can represent the same function, we separately
evaluate functional recovery after refitting candidate coef-
ficients. A candidate is recovered if its predictions match
the reference within specified tolerances across training,
in-distribution, out-of-distribution, and additional extrap-
olation points. We report functional recovery at ranks 1
and 3, alongside SA@k for the highest-weight candidate
structures. The numerical thresholds and detailed recovery
criteria are provided in Appendix A.

4.3
Simplicity and cost
We measure equation complexity using expression-tree node
counts and free-parameter counts, and computational cost
using language-model calls and estimated cost per prob-
lem. We additionally report a plateau statistic measuring
the fraction of proposer rounds after the last improvement
in the best evidence score. Detailed metric definitions and
token-usage limitations are provided in Appendix A.
4.4
Experimental setup
Benchmark and splits. We evaluate on the LSR-Synth sub-
set of LLM-SRBench (Shojaee et al., 2025b), comprising
129 problems across reaction kinetics, population dynam-
ics, stress–strain, and physical oscillators. Each problem
provides 4,000 training observations and separate 500-point
test sets for interpolation and extrapolation beyond the train-
ing range. We exclude LSR-Transform because it lacks
an extrapolation split. Data partitions and the unlabeled
extrapolation region are described in Section 3.2 and Ap-
pendix A. Each problem has a three-hour wall-clock limit,
with timeouts recorded as failures and excluded from paired
comparisons.
Development discipline. We reserve 20 problems (five per
domain) for prompt development and use the remaining 109
for held-out evaluation. All prompts are frozen before final
evaluation, and the critic prompt’s fingerprint is recorded.
Each held-out problem is evaluated once per configuration,
with paired comparisons restricted to problems completed
by both configurations.
The ablation ladder. To assess the contribution of search
feedback, we compare configurations A0–A3 (Section 3.3)
under a fixed budget of 125 LLM calls per problem. Ad-
jacent comparisons evaluate the effects of rule-based diag-
nostics (A1 vs. A0), LLM critic guidance (A2 vs. A1), and
additional label-free extrapolation information (A3 vs. A2).
In A2 and A3, 24 calls are allocated to the critic, leaving
101 proposer calls, compared with 125 proposer calls in
A0 and A1. Thus, all configurations have the same total
LLM-call budget, although the number of equation-proposal
rounds differs. The complete ablation configurations and
their corresponding experimental comparisons are detailed
in Appendix C, Table 6.
4.5
Baselines
We compare LLM-SR-UQ with three complementary base-
lines under a common evaluation protocol.
For LLM-
based comparisons, we use GPT-4o-mini and Llama-3.1-
8B-Instruct.
The first baseline is LLM-SR (Shojaee et al., 2025a), and
we use its official implementation and published 1k-sample
configuration. We compare its single best equation with
split-conformal intervals against the top 24 distinct equa-
tions from its experience buffer, which we refit and evidence-
weight using our procedure. Applying both split-conformal
and structural intervals to the same top-24 predictor isolates
the effect of structural disagreement without changing the
underlying search.
Direct prompting. Each LLM generates a single equation
without iterative search or diagnostic feedback. We apply
split-conformal intervals because no alternative equation
structures are retained. This baseline tests the benefit of
iterative equation search relative to direct generation.
Ensemble-SINDy.
As a non-LLM baseline, we use
Ensemble-SINDy (Fasel et al., 2022), which generates mul-
tiple sparse-regression models from a shared candidate li-
brary. We apply split-conformal and structural intervals to
its ensemble to test transfer beyond LLM-guided search.
This baseline is relevant because LSR-Synth targets are the
right-hand sides of ordinary differential equations, which de-
scribe how dynamical systems evolve over time. SINDy is
specifically designed to recover such governing expressions
from data.
All baselines evaluated in our experiments use the same
fitting set Dfit and calibration set Dcal-ID as LLM-SR-UQ.
Because only 80% of the available training data are used
for fitting, their results may differ from those originally
reported.

The difference between A0 and A1 highlights the contribu-
tion of mechanical feedback to equation discovery. Unlike
A0, A1 provides the proposer with fitting diagnostics and
information about previously evaluated candidate equations,
allowing subsequent proposals to respond to observed er-
rors. This reduces median ID NMSE from 3.3 × 10−6 to
2.0 × 10−8 and median OOD NMSE from 2.4 × 10−3 to
6.0 × 10−5. These improvements indicate that mechanical
feedback helps the search identify more accurate equations.
Structural intervals serve a complementary purpose by us-
ing disagreement among the retained equations to charac-
terize uncertainty under extrapolation. The LLM-SR and
Ensemble-SINDy results further demonstrate that this un-
certainty construction can improve coverage independently
of the proposed search procedure.
Search diversity and the role of the critic. Search diver-
sity provides further insight into the effect of mechanical
feedback. At A1, only 22% of proposed equations belong to
previously explored structural families, compared with 31%
at A0. Under the same budget of approximately 1,000 candi-
date equations, A1 explores 774 distinct structural families,
whereas A0 explores 690. This suggests that mechanical
feedback reduces repeated proposals and encourages explo-
ration of new equation structures, which may contribute
to the improved predictive accuracy observed at A1. De-
spite this increased exploration, the discovered equations
remain relatively compact, with 6.7–8.2 fitted coefficients
and 20–25.5 expression-tree nodes across the evaluated con-
figurations.
The calibration-aware critic produces a different pattern
of search behavior. Across critic-assisted configurations,
repeated proposals account for 33–39% of candidates, com-
pared with 22% at A1, while the number of distinct struc-
tural families decreases from 774 to 494–543. However, the
critic tends to produce simpler reported equations. Rela-
tive to A1, expression-tree size decreases from 25.1 to 22.5
nodes with the 8B critic (p = 0.007) and to 21.9 nodes with
GPT-4o (p = 8 × 10−5) at A3. These results indicate that
critic-assisted search yields more compact equations but
does not improve uncertainty performance.
The critic ablation indicates that adding a calibration-aware
critic does not improve OOD coverage attainment under
the fixed budget of 125 language-model calls. Mechanical
feedback alone (A1) achieves the highest attainment of 0.92
(100/109 problems), whereas the critic-assisted A2 and A3
configurations attain 0.82–0.88 across both critic models.
Although the critic produces valid equation edits, particu-
larly with GPT-4o, these edits rarely outperform the leading
candidates under the evidence criterion. Consequently, they
have limited influence on the retained equations. Because
critic calls replace some proposer calls within the fixed
budget, these results characterize the overall critic-assisted
search configuration rather than the isolated effect of the
critic. The main uncertainty improvements are therefore
achieved without the critic, through retaining alternative
equations and using their disagreement to construct input-
dependent intervals.
6
CONCLUSION
LLM-guided symbolic regression often selects a single equa-
tion, overlooking uncertainty among functional forms that
fit observed data similarly but diverge under extrapolation.
We introduce LLM-SR-UQ, which combines disagreement
among retained equations with conformal calibration to con-
struct input-dependent prediction intervals. Experiments on
LLM-SRBench show that split-conformal intervals main-
tain approximately nominal in-distribution coverage but
frequently fail under extrapolation, with prediction accuracy
alone failing to identify these errors. Across 109 held-out
problems, structural intervals improve OOD coverage attain-
ment from 0.58 to 0.83 at A0 and from 0.53 to 0.92 at A1,
while preserving in-distribution calibration.
These gains also extend to independent search meth-
ods. Structural intervals increase OOD attainment from
0.53 to 0.86 for LLM-SR top-24 and from 0.26 to 0.62
for Ensemble-SINDy without changing point predictions.
Within LLM-SR-UQ, mechanical feedback improves equa-
tion accuracy and search diversity, whereas the calibration-
aware critic produces simpler equations without additional
coverage gains under the fixed computational budget. Over-
all, the results demonstrate the value of structural disagree-
ment for improving empirical uncertainty estimates under
extrapolation.

A.2
Stage A: prompts and proposals
In A0, the proposer receives only the problem description at every round. From A1 onward, a fixed-format feedback
block, limited to 5, 000 characters, summarizes the three highest-weight structures, their training NMSE and coefficient
counts, poorly identified coefficients and their associated terms, unused input variables, systematic residual patterns,
protected-operator activations, repeated structural families, and search stagnation. The leading structures are included
as search diagnostics rather than separate few-shot examples. In A2 and A3, the prompt also includes the critic’s latest
guidance (Section 3.6). Because no candidates have been fitted in the first round, all configurations initially receive the same
problem-only prompt. The complete prompt templates are provided in Appendix D.
Each iteration generates K = 8 candidate equation structures with free coefficients named c0, c1, . . .. This convention
distinguishes fitted coefficients from input variables and enables automatic validation of proposed expressions. For example,
if the proposer writes z3 instead of the declared input x3, the expression is rejected rather than treating z3 as a new coefficient.
Any variable identifier that is neither a declared input nor a valid coefficient name cj is considered invalid.
To preserve exploration beyond the critic’s recommendations, its directives and edits may occupy at most six of the eight
proposal slots. The remaining two slots are reserved for proposals that are not conditioned on critic guidance.
A.3
Stage B: fitting, tempering and feedback labels
Fitting.
Coefficients are fitted on Dfit by nonlinear least squares with a trust-region reflective optimiser, started from 6
initial guesses with the best result kept; each start is limited to 2,000 function evaluations, and no new start begins after
120 s on one candidate. The noise estimate of Equation 1 is floored at 10−12 Var(y), where Var(y) is the variance of the
target on Dfit, so that a structure reproducing the data almost exactly does not send log ˆσ2

Coefficient uncertainty.
We approximate the uncertainty in the fitted coefficients by a Gaussian centred at ˆθk (a Laplace
approximation), whose covariance Σk is the inverse curvature of the fitting loss at ˆθk. Σk is used only for the feedback
labels below, not for the score.
Which coefficients the data determine.
Two labels, computed from Σk, feed the feedback of Stages A and C. Each
coefficient θ is given a broad reference scale max(10, 2|ˆθ|). A coefficient is unidentified when its standard deviation under
the Laplace approximation is at least half that scale—the data have barely narrowed it, and insignificant when it is identified
but its fitted value |ˆθ| is smaller than its standard deviation, so it cannot be told apart from zero. The two call for different
repairs: an unidentified coefficient usually signals a redundant term, an insignificant one a term that may not be needed.

A.4
Stage C: the critic
The diagnostics are measured from the data, not judged by a model.
Language models reason poorly over tables of
numbers and well over named patterns, so the analysis is computed numerically and the critic is given conclusions. For each
candidate in the buffer we report:
1. systematic residual trends against each input, reported when the Spearman rank correlation between residual and input
is at least 0.2 in magnitude, or when mean residuals over five quantile bins span at least 0.5 residual standard deviations
without being monotone;
2. which coefficients are unidentified or insignificant (Stage B);
3. how often the protected operators had to clamp their argument, reported when this affects at least 5% of rows;
4. (rung A3 only) how far the three highest-weight structures diverge from one another on Dcal-EXT: for each pair, the
NMSE between their two predictions there, beside the same quantity on the training inputs.
At the buffer level we report, in rung A3, the median half-width of the prediction intervals of Stage D on Dcal-EXT, relative to
the training target’s standard deviation, and how much the structural spread σS (the disagreement among retained structures,
Equation 7) grows between the two; and, in all critic rungs, a stagnation flag. The whole summary is written in JavaScript
Object Notation (JSON), a structured text format, and capped at 4,500 characters, about 1,500 tokens (the word pieces a
language model reads).
Stagnation is detected numerically, the best score has not strictly improved for 10 rounds, and stated to the critic, which
chooses between an explore mode (ask the proposer for structures unlike the current ones) and an exploit mode (refine
the current leaders). The mode is not forced. On the development set the critic chose explore in about 1 call in 24, and
Appendix E shows a 62-round plateau on which the flag never fired at a critic call. We report this as a limitation (§6).
The critic’s objective.
The objective is stated in the critic’s system prompt rather than computed. In rung A3 it is told
that the goal is structures that extrapolate with narrow, honest intervals; to prefer edits and directives that would make the
leading structures agree beyond the data; and never to propose anything whose only effect would be a wider interval. In
rung A2 the corresponding rule asks for compact, physically plausible forms that fit the data. With no labels on Dcal-EXT,
agreement among near-equally-supported structures is the strongest available surrogate for being right there: when structures
indistinguishable on the data diverge sharply just outside it, at most one of them can be extrapolating correctly. Ordering the
rule this way—agreement first, narrowness second—matters, because either half on its own is degenerate. Asking only for
narrow intervals rewards confident nonsense: a single over-fitted structure has no one to disagree with, so σS is small and the
interval is tight wherever it is wrong. Asking only for agreement rewards a buffer of near-copies, which agree everywhere
and say nothing. Requiring agreement among structures the evidence cannot separate, and only then preferring the narrow
ones, is the combination neither degenerate strategy satisfies. Note that no coverage appears in this rule: Dcal-EXT has no
labels, so coverage there cannot be computed, and the attainment-and-sharpness pair of Section 4 is an evaluation criterion,
not the critic’s objective.
Output, validation and memory.
The critic runs at sampling temperature 0 (its most likely reply, so that runs are
repeatable) with a fixed seed, and must answer in a fixed JSON format; it receives one language-model call in every five
of the problem’s 125-call budget: 24 critic calls and 101 proposer rounds. We run it with two models: the proposer itself
(Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on the same local server) and GPT-4o. Its reply names a mode; up to 5 structural families the
proposer should stop proposing (bans), which must be copied from the evidence’s list of repeatedly proposed families; 2–3
directives, each a short instruction to the proposer (for example, “try a saturating term in t”) with the number of the 8 slots it
should fill; and up to 3 edits, each a complete rewrite of a named buffer entry.
Every edit and directive must cite a piece of the evidence it was shown, and that piece must actually be present; items that

cite something absent are dropped and counted. Edits are also checked against the action the critic says it is taking. An edit
that claims to delete a term must remove one additive term (two from a structure of four or more), or remove the part of a
single term holding a coefficient the evidence lists as weak; one that claims to merge coefficients must keep the same inputs
and use fewer coefficients; one that claims to replace a term must swap out at most one term. Valid edits take slots in the
next round’s batch of 8 and are fitted, never inserted.
The critic’s guidance holds until its next call; a failed call spends its budget and leaves no guidance. There is no persistent
archive: the posterior buffer, which the critic may read but never write, is the only memory. Prompt and schema are in

Stage D: additional notes
A shift-aware variant that did not help. Weighted conformal intervals (Tibshirani et al., 2019), which reweight the
calibration residuals by a logistic-regression density ratio toward the unlabelled shifted inputs, were evaluated offline on the
development buffers. They lowered out-of-distribution coverage (A0: 0.61 →0.36–0.44), so they are not carried forward.
Accuracy and calibration under extrapolation. Recovering the correct equation structure can reduce extrapolation error,
but it does not guarantee calibrated uncertainty. Even with the correct functional form, residual distributions may differ
between calibration and shifted test data because of input-dependent noise or coefficient-estimation errors. Consequently,
accurate point predictions do not necessarily yield reliable prediction intervals under extrapolation. LLM-SR-UQ addresses
these objectives separately: candidate structures are retained based on fitting evidence and complexity, while prediction
intervals are calibrated using held-out observations and scaled by structural disagreement. This approach does not guarantee
OOD coverage, which is evaluated empirically. When multiple structures fit the observations similarly, their disagreement
provides additional information about uncertainty beyond the observed range.

Sensitivity to the tempering target.
Since the spread is produced by tempering rather than by the evidence, the target
Beff = 0.35B is the parameter the construction rests on, and it was fixed on the development set. We therefore re-weighted
every exported held-out buffer at nine targets from Beff = 1 (a point mass) to Beff = 24 (uniform over a full buffer),
re-solving τ, recomputing σS, recalibrating on Dcal-ID and re-evaluating, with the structures, fits, splits and seeds untouched.
Because the predictive mean uses the untempered weights, the point predictions are identical at every target and the
split-conformal reference is a single number per configuration: the sweep moves the interval scale and nothing else. At
Beff = 8.4 the procedure reproduces the published attainment to a mean absolute per-problem coverage difference of 0.0003
or better.
The curve is flat, not peaked, and the value we ran is not the optimum — several larger targets do slightly better on attainment
and on sharpness. This is worth stating plainly in both directions. It means Beff is not a tuned hyperparameter in the
sense that would compromise the result: a quantity tuned on the development set would show a peak at or near the chosen
value, and this one does not. It also means we cannot claim 0.35B is principled. The defensible claim is the measured one:
attainment is insensitive to the tempering target over an order of magnitude, provided the weights are not collapsed onto a
single structure.
The degenerate subset makes the point most sharply. On the 86 A0 problems where the evidence is a point mass and
tempering therefore supplies all of the spread, attainment is 50/86 at Beff = 1 — indistinguishable from split conformal’s
50/86, as it should be — and 73 to 75 of 86 at every target from 2 to 24, with no dependence on where in that range the
target sits. The mechanism needs a set with spread. It does not need a particular amount of it.

The null is not a critic that never fired.
On the development set the critic makes 24 calls and proposes about 70 edits
per problem-run. GPT-4o’s edits pass the mechanical checks 78.5% of the time against 29.1% for the 8B model, enter the
retained buffer about twice as often (2.4 against 1.4 per run at A2, 3.1 against 1.1 at A3), cite missing evidence on 0.3% of
items against 8.4%, and choose exploration in most calls rather than almost none. The critic works, and works better with
a stronger model. The outcome metrics still do not move, and the buffer explains why: an edit must beat the incumbent
structures on evidence to be kept, and only one to three of about twenty valid edits per run do. A critic that proposes well
but rarely displaces anything cannot change what the buffer determines.
Reading the null.
Two of the three hypotheses this paper set out to test were not supported, and it is worth saying plainly
what that leaves standing. What fails is the claim that a language model, shown diagnostics about its own search, will steer
that search toward better-calibrated equations. It does not, for either critic model, on either data set, under any metric we
fixed in advance, and the design has the power to show it: 109 paired held-out problems, five development seeds, two critic
models an order of magnitude apart in capability, and counters confirming the critic was active. What stands is independent
of the critic. The mismatch between accuracy and calibration under shift is a property of the benchmark and the methods
evaluated on it, the interval construction is what delivers calibration (Section5.1); and it works on searches we did not write
(Section5.2). A reader who discards the critic entirely still keeps the measurement, the construction and the wrapper.

Critic input, output, and validation
The critic’s instructions.
The critic receives the following system message before every call. Placeholders are filled from
the problem: the variable list, the batch size, and—in the calibration-aware configuration only—the final rule, which is
omitted otherwise. The text below is the version used in the runs we report, confirmed against the recorded hash of the code
that produced them.
You are the critic in an equation-discovery loop. A proposer writes candidate
expressions; each is fitted to the data and scored by Bayesian evidence, and
only the evidence decides what is kept. You never decide what is kept. You read
the evidence JSON and write edits and directives for the proposer.
Rules.
1. Ground everything. Every edit and directive names an evidence_field: a path
into the evidence JSON, with dots for keys and [n] for list positions, such as
weak_coefficients[0], residual.patterns[1], variables.inclusion.t or stagnation.
An item whose evidence_field does not exist, or points to an empty value, is
discarded.
2. Edits (at most 3) are complete rewrites of one of top_structures, identified
by of_rank, and are fitted like any proposal. The expression is always the WHOLE
structure after the change - never a single term, and never the structure
unchanged. Actions:
delete_term: remove one term whose coefficient the data does not determine
(two, if the structure has four or more terms), or remove the part of one term
that holds such a coefficient (c0*P/(c1 + c2*t) becomes c0*P when
weak_coefficients names c1); keep everything else, and keep the names of the
coefficients you keep.
merge_coefficients: rewrite with fewer coefficients where several only act
together (c0*c1*P becomes c0*P); keep the same inputs.
replace_term: swap exactly one term the residual shows is misshaped for a
better one; keep the other terms.
Example: top structure 1 is c0*P + c1*P**2 + c2*sin(c3*t) and
weak_coefficients[0] names c2 in c2*sin(c3*t). A valid edit is {"of_rank": 1,
"action": "delete_term", "expression": "c0*P + c1*P**2", "evidence_field":
"weak_coefficients[0]"}.
An edit that does not match its action is discarded.
3. Directives (2 or 3) steer the proposer's next rounds. Make them pursue
different hypotheses. Each takes n_slots of the batch of 8; directive slots plus
edits may not exceed 6, so at least 2 proposals stay unconditioned.
4. banned_families (at most 5): expressions copied from repeated_families that
the proposer should stop proposing. Nothing else may be banned.
5. mode: "exploit" to refine the current best structures; "explore" when the
search has stagnated or the best structures look physically implausible.
6. The evidence has a calibration block, computed without any held-out labels:
how far the top structures' predictions diverge beyond the training inputs, and
how much the prediction interval widens there. The goal is structures that
extrapolate with narrow, honest intervals. Prefer edits and directives that
would make the top structures agree beyond the data - forms that discriminate
between them, or physically motivated forms - and never propose anything whose
only effect would be a wider interval.
Expressions: use only the variables t, P, and coefficients named c0, c1, c2, ...
Allowed functions: sin cos tan tanh sinh cosh exp log sqrt abs sign softplus
minimum maximum. Write powers as **. Right-hand side only.
Return JSON only.

goal is a law, not a curve fit: compact, physically plausible forms that fit the data.” Everything else is identical, so the two
configurations differ in exactly one paragraph of instruction and in the presence of one block of evidence.

Scope.
This trace documents the mechanism on one problem: measurements computed from the fits, a critic whose every
