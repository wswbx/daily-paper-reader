# Pretraining for Forecasting in Relational Databases 

Ziyue Lin 1,2 Sebastian Regalado Lozano 1 Michael Guerzhoy 2 Ricardo Baptista 2 George Stein 11Layer 6 AI 2University of Toronto 

## Abstract 

Real-world forecasting increasingly relies on asynchronous transaction logs in relational databases rather than uniformly sampled arrays. While relational deep learning (RDL) models can forecast from these complex histories, they are typi-cally trained separately for each specific target and root entity type. We introduce Query-Conditioned Relational pretraining (QCRP), a self-supervised framework that enables a standard RDL backbone to act as a universal forecaster across a database. QCRP generates diverse forecasting objectives using a structured tempo-ral query grammar and interleaves them through a shared conditional predictor. This produces a robust, frozen encoder reusable for downstream tasks via lightweight predictors or a zero-shot interface. Evaluated on large-scale RelBench databases, QCRP learns frozen representations that support competitive forecasting across tasks and entity types. Furthermore, a single unified QCRP model checkpoint is competitive with isolated, entity-specific specialists. We demonstrate promising results for reusing the learned representations from standard RDL backbones for forecasting tasks in relational databases. 

## 1 Introduction 

Traditional time-series forecasting predominantly focuses on variables measured at a standard, uniform cadence. However, in practice, a vast amount of predictive modeling operates on complex temporal environments captured within relational databases. These environments are characterized by continuous, asynchronous event streams, such as transaction logs, user interactions, irregular clinical observations [15], or sensor readings, rather than neatly aligned, uniformly sampled arrays. Directly forecasting future event occurrences or states from these irregular histories requires architectures capable of handling non-uniform temporal intervals and complex dependencies across multiple data modalities. Such forecasts are typically anchored to different entity types, or “root” tables, such as users, products, and accounts. Although these entities share the same evolving database history, standard relational deep learning (RDL) pipelines train separate models for each target, horizon, and root entity type [5, 24]. Recent relational foundation models and transferable encoders seek greater reuse [29, 21, 23, 11], but often introduce specialized architectures or target cross-database generalization. We instead ask a complementary question: can a conventional relational encoder be pretrained once on the temporal history of a single database and reused across its many forecasting problems? Self-supervised learning offers a pathway to train these conventional architectures on large-scale temporal corpora. Methods like contrastive learning and masked graph reconstruction learn structural representations, but do so without explicitly predicting future temporal outcomes [22, 9, 10]. Alterna-tively, frameworks like TVE predict future relational statistics [28], directly connecting pretraining to entity-level forecasting. Generating pseudo-tasks has also shown promise for tabular adaptation [20] and tabular foundation model pretraining [17, 26], while RelPrism [30] broadens it to relational pretraining. These approaches successfully motivate objectives reflecting downstream predictive needs. However, TVE and RelPrism are both explicitly root-table-centric: their predictive objectives are defined for one root entity type at a time, even though different roots share the same evolving database history. Moreover, a reusable forecaster must support a much broader space of future targets

We describe the pretraining framework in Section 2, evaluate the frozen reuse and multi-root transfer methodology in Section 3, and conclude with implications and limitations for temporal relational forecasting in Section 4. 

## 2 Multi-Root Relational Pretraining 

Relational entity-level forecasting. Following RDL and RelBench [5, 24], let v ∈ V r denote an entity from root table r, and let t be a prediction cutoff. An RDL backbone Eθ maps the entity and its pre-cutoff relational history H<t to a d-dimensional representation: zv,t = Eθ (v, H <t ) ∈ Rd.

Sampling H<t strictly before t prevents future-event features from entering the representation. The forecasting target is defined from events in a future window (t, t + ∆] . Thus, QCRP summarizes historical activity to predict diverse future outcomes (Fig. 1(b)). 

Query-defined future window supervision. Rather than pretraining on a fixed target vector, QCRP defines scalar forecasting objectives through structured temporal queries. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), a query q specifies the future relational quantity to predict, which is encoded as eq = Gη (q) ∈ Rp.

A shared conditional predictor then produces ˆyv,t,q = Dϕ(zv,t , e q ). This extends future-statistic pretraining [28] from a fixed collection of statistics to a structured family of forecasting objectives inspired by predictive query languages [12, 13]. The relational encoder summarizes what has happened , while the query representation specifies which future property should be predicted . This factorization allows the expensive relational encoding zv,t to be computed once and reused across many forecasting queries, while the shared conditional decoder can exploit structure common to related query definitions. The complete query grammar and task inventory are given in Appendix B and F. 

Single-root multi-task pretraining. QCRP processes many forecasting objectives over the same relational computation. At each update, we sample a minibatch B of entity–cutoff pairs (v, t ) and a set S of queries, encode each historical neighborhood once, and reuse the resulting representations across all q ∈ S . Multi-root multi-task pretraining. A single database may support forecasts for several root entity types, such as customers and products. We therefore extend the same model across roots using block-wise interleaving: training alternates between root types while sharing Eθ , Gη , and Dϕ. Each block trains on forecasting queries associated with one root for T gradient updates, exposing the shared representation to different views of the same evolving database. We study the resulting cross-root transfer in Section 3; scheduling and exposure-matching details are deferred to the Appendix D. 

Frozen downstream reuse. After pretraining, the relational encoder is frozen and reused as shown in Fig. 1(c). For a downstream task with labeled examples {(vi, t i, y i)}, we compute frozen representations zvi,t i = Eθ (vi, H <t i ) and train only a two-layer MLP ˆyi = gψ (zvi,t i ), which outputs a regression value or classification logit. This directly tests whether QCRP has learned reusable temporal relational features rather than merely a good initialization. For query-defined tasks, the frozen query encoder and conditional predictor Dϕ(zv,t , G η (q)) additionally provide a zero-shot forecasting interface. 

## 3 Experiments and Results 

Setup. We follow RelBench, a widely adopted benchmark for relational learning on temporal databases [24] focusing on Rel-Amazon and Rel-HM, two large-scale datasets with forecasting tasks spanning multiple root entity types. We evaluate six entity-level tasks using the official temporal splits, and report mean absolute error (MAE) for regression tasks, and ROC-AUC for binary classification. Table 1 compares GraphSAGE [7] pretrained with masked reconstruction (MaskAE; [9]), contrastive relational pretraining (CTR; [22]), Task Vector Estimation (TVE), and TVE combined with CTR (TVE+CTR; [28]). Single-root QCRP root uses separately trained checkpoints for each root entity type, while multi-root QCRP multi uses one checkpoint trained jointly across all evaluated roots in a database. For frozen adaptation, each encoder is fixed and a fresh MLP is trained on its representations using the downstream training labels; the downstream checkpoint is selected by validation performance. We also include supervised training from scratch as a baseline for comparison. GraphSAGE results average three pretraining seeds. Table 2 additionally compares multi-root RelGNN reuse [4] with published results from KumoRFM-2 and RelArena [11, 8]. Detailed protocols and training budgets are given in the Appendix A. 

Future-query supervision supports representation reuse. With frozen GraphSAGE embeddings, QCRP root outperforms TVE on all six tasks and TVE+CTR on five; QCRP multi exceeds both on all six (Table 1). MaskAE and CTR underperform future-statistic pretraining on all six tasks, consistent with their objectives lacking explicit supervision for future-window outcomes. The largest Amazon gain occurs on item-LTV: QCRP root achieves 46.203 MAE versus 54.544 for TVE and 55.096 for TVE+CTR. This target combines future review count with product price, consistent with broader objective coverage helping transfer. Com-parisons match per-root exposure: a multi-root run receives a larger to-tal budget than either specialist alone. QCRP multi remains competitive with single-root specialist QCRP root , im-proving three of six displayed means while losing ground on the other three (Table 1). With RelGNN, QCRP multi achieves the second-best average rank among the state-of-the-art methods, behind RT-PluRel, and the best Amazon user-LTV score (Table 2). Together, these results show that a conventional re-lational encoder can be pretrained once across entity populations and retain competitive frozen forecasting performance without maintaining a separate encoder for each root. 

Transfer to additional forecasting objectives. Finally, to test whether QCRP learns reusable temporal features beyond the objectives seen during pretraining, we evaluate 14 grammar-defined Rel-Amazon tasks whose definitions are excluded from both QCRP’s and TVE’s pretraining pools (Appendix B). An MLP fitted on held-out-task training labels achieves 81.7 mean test AUC with frozen QCRP embeddings, exceeding TVE (79.2) and matching supervised training from scratch (81.6). The retained query encoder and decoder also permit prediction without task-specific fitting: correct query specifications achieve 68.9 mean AUC, compared with 48.8 when query fields are shuffled while inputs, labels, and weights remain fixed. This secondary interface is useful but uneven across tasks; per-task scores and embedding geometry are provided in the Appendix E. 

## 4 Discussion and Conclusion 

We introduced Query-Conditioned Relational Pretraining (QCRP), a framework that translates structured future queries into self-supervised objectives. This approach enables conventional relational encoders to pretrain at the database level rather than optimizing for a single downstream target. Our results demonstrate that standard RDL backbones can achieve broad database-level reusability: a single pretrained RelGNN checkpoint successfully transfers across diverse forecasting tasks and root entity types. This complements relational foundation models, which achieve reuse via specialized large-scale architectures and pretraining regimes, by showing that standard RDL backbones can also achieve similar reuse. Query conditioning additionally enables zero-shot predictions for structured tasks. The learned task representations capture meaningful structure among forecasting objectives. Our study also highlights limitations of current evaluation. Existing relational benchmarks provide only a small number of forecasting objectives per database, a form of task scarcity recently identified as a bottleneck for relational transfer [2]. The six standard tasks considered here further use only one prediction horizon per database (91 days for Rel-Amazon and 7 days for Rel-HM), and several are defined by simple next-window quantities such as activity, lifetime value, or sales [24, 28]. This makes it difficult to assess generalization across target semantics and temporal scales. Richer benchmarks should provide many objectives over the same evolving system while varying target semantics, horizon length, relational depth, and temporal dynamics. Finally, QCRP does not explicitly model temporal structure. Combining database-native relational representations with these temporal signals like lagged target, trajectories, periodicity, or seasonality is a promising direction toward more general foundation models for temporal systems. 4References

A.1 Databases and prediction populations 

A relational database consists of tables of entities and events, linked by foreign keys; relational deep learning represents these tables and links as a heterogeneous graph [5]. In forecasting, we choose a root entity and a prediction cutoff, and use the relational neighborhood available at that point as input. For example, to predict a customer’s spending over the next month, the customer ID identifies the root node, and the model input contains the historical subgraph. Temporal semantics of paths. “Direct events” selects root-linked events in (t, t + ∆] . A dimension path first selects such an event and then joins its linked entity attributes, for example customer– review–product. A same-customer or same-product path first selects a future triggering event and then joins other events sharing that entity key. The second event may not occur later than the triggering event. On HM it must additionally lie within the seven days preceding that event; the Amazon construction does not impose this lower time bound. These joins preserve multiplicities, so they are not equivalent to deduplicating all reached entities or events. The same path semantics are used across the corresponding TVE and QCRP comparisons; TVE supplies next-window aggregate supervision [28]. The inventory is a finite selection of generated objectives, not a fresh enumeration of all grammar combinations at each training step. Candidate predicates require sufficient training prevalence; optional categorical groups require at least 32 members. Query generation uses training-label quantiles and rejects degenerate binary targets. The selected pools contain 38 Amazon product, 40 Amazon customer, 78 HM article, and 65 HM customer objectives. HM customer supervision consists of scalar and binary objectives derived from the same two-hop aggregate families used by task-vector pretraining; the article pool additionally includes filtered and contextualized objectives. This asymmetry is an experimental design choice, not a different inference algorithm.

What is and is not held out. The query audit compares the complete specification, including root, path, aggregation, predicate, context, output type, and readout. Each held-out query differs from every selected training specification. Primitive values are deliberately shared, and numerical readout thresholds are allowed to be new. Five tasks introduce a new readout over a familiar computational scope, six additionally recombine one structural query axis, and three recombine two. These categories describe query construction, not a guarantee of increasing predictive difficulty. 11 This exclusion does not establish independence from training targets, a concern raised in studies of task scarcity and label overlap in relational transfer [2]. For example, P1 tests whether maximum rating is below five, while maximum rating itself is a regression objective. A model capable of estimating that aggregate may support the unseen thresholded target. Similar relations exist between distinct-count objectives and diversity tests. The valid claim is transfer to withheld query definitions, including new readouts and compositions; it is not transfer to functions with no recoverable relation to the training task pool. Nor do we claim that all exact or approximate statistical proxies have been removed. The standard benchmark tasks are not subjected to the same exclusion: direct inactivity and count statistics remain in pretraining.

Offline representation reuse A fixed encoder permits representations to be cached by entity and cutoff, after which task-specific predictors can be trained and evaluated without repeated relational encoding. Tasks with identical historical inputs can share the cache. Reuse requires a fixed checkpoint, preprocessing, and neighborhood construction; a new cutoff generally requires a new representation. With stochastic neighborhood sampling, caching fixes the sampled realization.
