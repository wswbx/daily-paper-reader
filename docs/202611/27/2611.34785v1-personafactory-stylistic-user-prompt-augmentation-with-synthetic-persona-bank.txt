## ABSTRACT 

Large language models increasingly replace real people in the AI development pipeline, as they are used to generate training dialogues at scale and to drive alignment and evaluation. However, out-of-the-box models produce monolingual and stylistically flat texts as synthetic user prompts, failing to replicate the real user-AI communication mechanics. We present PersonaFactory , an end-to-end pipeline that generates detailed user personas and uses them to augment existing user prompts from their point of view, producing a bank of over 740000 structured synthetic personas detailing their background and communication behaviors, and a corpus of roughly 1.24 million persona-styled synthetic conversations spanning 85 languages 1. We evaluate the effect of personal features on style adoption and intent preservation, using an LLM-judge and heuristic metrics to assess augmentation fidelity together with the downstream impact on assistant persona control through training data. Results show that our structured, attribute-based style representation enables per-axis control and better intent preservation at comparable style fidelity than free-text persona descriptions used in existing methods. A human expert evaluation found the augmented conversations plausible, with favorable ratings for intent preservation, style naturalness, and multilingual authenticity. Finally, PersonaFactory conversations achieve comparable performance to original training data on capability benchmarks, are preferred on real user prompts at equal reply length, and adapt their reply style more closely to users without explicit steering. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Conversational datasets are integral to large language model development, shaping how models understand users’ intents and behave in diverse settings. Existing open-source datasets largely focus on LLM-generated synthetic setups because human-based data scaling is cost- and time-intensive. Therefore, AI is now used in all stages of training other models on real-life interaction with human users. This includes user questions and assistant responses, judging the quality of such QA pairs, and evaluating model effectiveness in chat interaction settings. However, this scalability comes at the cost of authenticity and diversity: the raw material for most simulated users is the same narrow band of text: instruction-tuning corpora written in a homogeneous, well-punctuated, standard-register “assistant/user” voice. The result is one averaged user who is monolingual, verbose and stylistically uniform, a narrow reflection of real users across cultures, languages and behaviors. Such datasets, widely used to train open models, lack non-native phrasing, code-switching, terse or lowercase typing, and dialectal variation, giving a narrow picture of how users actually communicate with AI in real-life conditions. 

Personalization gap. When simulating conversations, there are two common design pitfalls: (1) methods that simulate personalized conversations from scratch condition on short persona descriptions and underuse the background details that most shape content and style; (2) rewriting existing conversations preserves the original intent but changes little in how the text reads. Our work. We present PersonaFactory , a pipeline to target these particular issues in synthetic persona and chat data generation (Figure 1). The key idea is to keep the original dialogue trajectory from an existing real or synthetic conversation, but re-simulate the user side under a structured synthetic persona: the request stays fixed, while the register, typing style, and language behavior match those of a simulated user drawn from a large, controllable population. The persona description covers not only basic information such as age, origin, occupation, and a short biography, but also writing style, linguistic quirks, digital fluency and AI stance, painting a much richer background on 

how the persona would interact with an AI model. Since the intervention is a constrained rewrite of existing user turns (real WildChat user logs and synthetic UltraChat and Nemotron seeds) rather than free generation, it keeps the original requests and dialogue trajectories while gaining the diversity and authenticity from the user’s side.

## 2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK 

User and persona simulation. LLM-based user simulation has largely replaced agenda-based simulators (Ni et al., 2026), either by training purpose-built user models (Naous et al., 2026; Kong et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) or by optimizing simulators with reinforcement learning (Wang et al., 2026; Qian et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026); a parallel persona-conditioned line, from PersonaChat (Zhang et al., 2018) to role-play agents (Tseng et al., 2024; Samuel et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b), conditions generation on short biographical profiles. Across both lines, the simulated user remains generic: an averaged user served as a live, hard-to-audit model, with personas that say who a character is but not how they type, and the behavior distribution becomes skewed and hard to control (Li et al., 2026). PersonaFactory instead rewrites existing logs under a quality-gated persona population with a first-class style layer, and releases a curated corpus rather than a simulator. User-simulator benchmarks such as MirrorBench (Hathidara et al., 2026) and SimulatorArena (Dou et al., 2025) score free-generated user turns; they do not fit persona-conditioned rewriting, where the seed fixes the request and only the voice changes, so we evaluate style adoption and intent preservation against the seed. Concurrent work (Konnelly et al., 2026) also authors personas as sociolinguistic style profiles. 

Persona datasets and persona-conditioned chat data generation. Large persona datasets are largely used to improve synthetic training data (Ge et al., 2025; Meyer & Corneil, 2025; Ma et al., 2026; Ryan et al., 2025), on top of persona-free recipes (Wang et al., 2023; Ding et al., 2023), and real-log sources (Zhao et al., 2024). However, adding a persona influences the content of the data. At the same time, the style remains in the same generic voice, as the persona itself is usually a one- to two-sentence free-text description that underspecifies typing behavior. Methodologically, the closest work is SynthPAI (Yukhymenko et al., 2024), which seeds LLM agents with structured profiles, including a description of writing style, to generate realistic Reddit threads as the privacy-preserving alternative to attribute inference evaluation of Staab et al. (2024). However, SynthPAI targets user-user interaction rather than user-AI chat. We build on this idea of a separate style entity: we decompose the style description into structured per-axis keys, which the judge prefers on style at equal prompt length (Section 5) and which keep the request more often at matched content (Appendix D); we ground generation in existing logs with the original intent fixed rather than generating from scratch, and decouple occupation from region to suppress stereotypes (Section 3). 

Algorithmic fidelity and detectability. Argyle et al. (2023) propose four criteria for whether model samples stand in for human ones (Turing test, backward and forward continuity, pattern correspondence); Pagan et al. (2026) show that LLM text stays distinguishable from human text for a trained classifier, trading human-likeness against semantic fidelity. Our pipeline keeps the request, so it sits on the fidelity side; Appendix C evaluates all four criteria and both detector settings. 

Sociolinguistic variation in NLP. Dialect and register variation is known to degrade and bias NLP systems (Blodgett et al., 2016; Ziems et al., 2022), and persona prompting alone induces only limited diversity (Kambhatla et al., 2025); yet the synthetic pipelines producing today’s conversational training data barely vary register, so the variation these systems handle worst is exactly what their training data lacks. PersonaFactory makes this variation (code-switching, typo tendency, 3Preprint capitalization, digital fluency) the explicit object of simulation, as conditioning variables rather than claims of dialect authenticity. 

## 3 SCALING THE PERSONA FACTORY BANK WITH REALISTIC SYNTHETIC 

## PERSONAS 

First, we describe the synthetic profile generation pipeline, which produces a highly diverse bank of over 740000 personas (743,010), focused not only on users’ backgrounds but also on their communication style in a human-AI setting. 

Structured profile representation. We split the persona representation into two layers: background 

and style . The background layer provides information on age (adults only, 18–80 in the generated bank, as we find simulating underage personas unethical), gender, country/region of origin, occupation (with sector), education, native language (with regional dialect), hobbies, AI stance and digital fluency level. Based on this background, we condition the generation of the style layer, covering sentence-length tendency, question framing, native-language error patterns, code-switching frequency and type, a cultural reference pool, a structured seven-key chatting style (capitalization, punctuation, emoji use, abbreviation habits, typo tendency, message structure, sign-offs), several (2–4) linguistic quirks, and an example message the persona would usually type. Separating them lets us use the first layer for content adjustment and the second layer to change how it will be written (Section 4). 

Generating the persona bank. We pass the pre-defined JSON schema into the generator and use it to produce N personas in the specified format. As generator models, we used Gemma-4-31B (Team et al., 2026), Ministral-3-14B (Liu et al., 2026) and GLM-4.7-Flash (GLM-4.5 Team et al., 2025), balancing each model’s geographical and multilingual strengths for various regions; the model selection is equally balanced across generator models. We ground the generation in a manually curated set of 177 countries and 599 sub-regions, combined with 575 concrete occupations spanning 23 sectors (details in Appendix A). We sample occupations independently of origin region to avoid biased profiles, which we initially observed (e.g., for Switzerland, the most popular occupation would be watchmaker or software engineer; Eastern Europe and Asia are more agriculture-heavy). Moreover, digital fluency is matched to the occupation sector to ensure realistic backgrounds. The style layer is filled in the same generation pass. Still, it is explicitly conditioned on the already-sampled background: the generation prompt (Appendix B.1) instructs the generator to derive each style attribute from the persona’s age, education, digital fluency, and native language, choosing every structured key from a fixed set of options that must stay consistent with the free-text style sentence and the verbatim example message. Profiles with a degenerate style layer, that is, a missing style description, fewer than two linguistic quirks, no example message, or empty structured keys, are rejected outright before judging. 

Judge verification. An independent judge ( MiniMax-M2.5 (Chen et al., 2026), not one of the generators, avoiding self-preference) scores every profile on internal coherence, occupation/location plausibility, LLM-user authenticity, stereotype severity, and realism, with a two-pass repair that corrects implausibly low fluency for technical roles and rewrites age-incoherent job titles before re-judging. The gate is strict but rarely triggered: about 95% of generated profiles are kept, judged realism is high, and only 0.2% of profiles are flagged for non-trivial stereotype severity. Appendix A (Table 4) provides full coverage, demographic, and per-generator quality statistics. 

## 4 PROMPT AUGMENTATION USING SYNTHETIC PERSONAS 

A grounded user simulator must satisfy four requirements: (R1) preserve the real task and its constraints, defaulting to its language; (R2) express sociolinguistic diversity beyond the standard register; (R3) stay on the logged conversation’s trajectory; and (R4) remain auditable and leakage-free, so that models trained on it learn no hidden persona shortcut. PersonaFactory’s four stages map onto these requirements: conservative matching and constrained rewriting serve R1, cross-lingual selection and structured style layer serve R2, next-turn continuity supervision serves R3, and storing only observable turns serves R4. We illustrate the pipeline in Figure 1. 4Preprint 

Persona matching and selection. We first detect the language and script of the initial user turn using deterministic Unicode block classifiers (covering CJK, Cyrillic, Arabic, etc.) coupled with a confidence-thresholded language detector. 2 To serve distinct research needs, the pipeline operates under two selection regimes: 1. Conservative (Language-Matched) Selection: A persona is eligible only if it is verified to communicate natively in the prompt’s language and script. Non-Latin prompts are mapped strictly to native-speaking personas sharing the target script, non-English Latin prompts to detected-language personas, and English prompts to the broader English-speaking pool. A single best-matching persona is retrieved through prioritized tiers: geographic location matching, top-1 semantic embedding cosine similarity, or deterministic language-pool fallback. If no compatible persona exists, the pipeline returns the original turn verbatim. 2. Cross-Lingual Diverse Selection: To reflect authentic multilingual populations who interact with LLMs in global languages, this mode samples a single persona uniformly at random from the entire bank (where native-English personas constitute only 19.6%), allowing non-English personas to re-voice prompts with their regional register, cultural framing, and loanwords while relaxing strict script-stability abort gates. Exact-format prompts (e.g., verbatim reproduction, code generation tasks, direct translation, and strict JSON/quoted output instructions) are automatically detected and bypassed by format guards. In a controlled pilot, conservative matching raises style adoption by more than 20 points over a random persona of the same language (Appendix C). 

User-turn rewriting. For eligible turns, the rewriter is instructed to preserve the original intent while introducing only subtle persona traits. Linguistic quirks and colloquialisms are applied probabilistically at a rate of p = 0 .25 per turn. This probability alone has no consistent effect on over-stylization; the effective lever is an explicit cap of at most two realized chat markers per turn (greeting, sign-off, emoji, slang, ellipsis, repeated punctuation), given as an instruction in the rewrite prompt, which we recommend as the default setting (Appendix D). A length-ratio guard automatically rejects over-compressed rewrites ( < 50% of the original length), falling back to the verbatim seed prompt if any validation gate fails. Quoted text, code, URLs and numbers are masked before the rewrite and restored afterwards, and a rewrite that loses a masked span is retried. In cross-lingual mode, we tried two ways of reaching the persona’s language: re-voicing the source turn directly into that language in a single pass, and first translating each turn literally and then restyling the translation with the same-language rewrite. The two-step variant preserves the request and lands in the target language more reliably, at a small cost in style adoption (Appendix C), so the pipeline uses it. 

Assistant regeneration with continuity supervision. The restyled user turn is appended to the running context, and the assistant response is regenerated. The persona background/style is provided to the responder as an unsaved system prompt: the model sees the user’s register during generation, but the stored training example contains only the observable user and assistant turns, so a downstream learner acquires style from conversational cues, not from hidden persona metadata. For non-final turns, a judge checks whether the regenerated response plausibly leads to the actual next user message from the log; if not, a bounded adaptation loop (up to 2 iterations) rewrites the assistant response so that the real historical follow-up remains a coherent continuation, preserving the logged dialogue trajectory. If the judge still flags the response after the last iteration, the last adapted response is kept and left to the sanity gate. The continuity-judge and adaptation prompts are given in Appendix B.1. 

Sanity checks and judge verification. A final judge screens each conversation for six failure classes: language mismatch (for conservative matching), instruction/format violation, truncation, fabricated personal experience, incoherence/hallucination, and failure to address the request. These sanity verdicts are stored as metadata flags, allowing downstream training pipelines to selectively filter for strictly validated, constraint-intact dialogues.

## 5 EVALUATION AND AUGMENTATION ANALYSIS 

We target two main tasks with our proposed persona-based augmentation method: it should sound like the seeded user (style adoption) and still keep the same original topic in the same language (intent preservation). To do this, we define two fidelity axes and evaluate them on the released corpus (Section 6), using both an out-of-family LLM judge and deterministic, judge-free metrics; we then conduct a paired, order-randomized A/B to isolate the effect of style representation by holding generator model, persona, and token budget fixed; and finally, we re-score a stratified subset with four judges to confirm the direction is not a single-judge artifact. 

Corpus-scale method evaluation. We evaluate both fidelity axes through two complementary measures: an out-of-family reasoning judge ( MiniMax-M2.5 ) and deterministic, judge-free heuris-tics (Appendix C). Over the whole corpus, the LLM judge yields 96.6% intent preservation and 67.1% style adoption (64.9% passing both simultaneously), with higher-capacity generator backbones adopting style more faithfully ( Gemma-4-31B : 69.8% vs. Ministral-3-14B : 63.2%). Because this check is lenient toward added details, we additionally give every user turn a strict four-way label (same request, added content, dropped content, scope change): about three in four turns keep the exact request and nearly all keep the core request, and both a second external judge and a blind human check agree with the label (Appendix C). The deterministic metrics show that rewrites introduce substantial surface variation while preserving meaning: same-language rewrites change a large share of the surface form yet retain most core vocabulary, and multilingual embedding similarity remains high for both same-language and cross-lingual rewrites (Table 9). Computed on the same seeds without a persona and with the PersonaHub and SynthPAI forms, these metrics show that each step up in style information moves the text further from the seed and raises style adherence, with our format changing the text most and adhering best (Appendix C). Style adherence varies by attribute: emoji usage is followed almost always, punctuation and capitalization patterns only moderately, and message length grows for personas who write long messages but barely shrinks for those who write short ones. 

Writing style representation ablation. Our central design question concerns representation: to make a user sound a certain way, is it better to provide the rewriter with a single free-text style descrip-tion of 1–2 short sentences (flat) or with the per-axis structured style keys (structured)? We compared the two in a paired A/B on a sample of 553 real user messages (518 after the pipeline’s judge passing stage), using the same rewriter and selected persona. Comparison to other persona representations. We also compare PersonaFactory to the profile formats of PersonaHub (Ge et al., 2025) and SynthPAI (Yukhymenko et al., 2024), keeping all the settings but the profile representation. Table 2 shows a clear pattern: formats that describe writing behavior more explicitly produce stronger style adoption, and PersonaFactory is preferred in every pairwise comparison. PersonaHub, which has no writing-style field, performs only slightly above the no-persona baseline. The ranking holds when the judges see only format-neutral style attributes, under two external judges, and against real PersonaHub personas, while real SynthPAI profiles are on par. Richer style conditioning has a moderate cost: intent preservation drops by about four points, almost entirely on cross-lingual prompts, and external judges rate the rewrites somewhat less natural; capping the number of chat markers realized per turn closes this naturalness gap while keeping most of the style advantage (Appendix D). Since the SynthPAI form carries less style information, this comparison mixes structure with content: at matched content and length, the structured form is on par in style but keeps the request more often and reads more naturally (Appendix D). Appendix D provides the full breakdown and robustness checks. The style layer can also extend existing persona banks: when PersonaHub and Nemotron-Personas samples are converted to our background representation and given a generated style layer, all three judges prefer the resulting rewrites over the real WildChat messages (e.g. 67.6% against 17.6% for 

MiniMax-M2.5 , Appendix D). This preference mainly reflects more casual typing than real users show (greetings, slang and emoji appear several times more often), rather than closer realism, and the marker cap addresses it.

Human evaluation. To validate the effectiveness of the user augmentation pipeline, we run an IRB-approved preliminary expert study in which AI experts rate corpus samples in their native/fluent languages on four tasks: 1) intent preservation after the rewrite step; 2) style following in augmented user prompts in a natural way; 3) human- vs AI-written text classification (our chats mixed with WildChat samples, written by humans); 4) multilingual authenticity of updated conversations. So far, we have collected 11 responses across 9 languages, mostly with one rater per language, so we treat the study as a preliminary signal (Appendix E; per-language results and the scope relative to related work in Appendices E.5 and E.6). Raters are favorable on intent, style and plausibility (median 4 of 5) (Table 18), and the blind test is the more telling result. Raters take verified model rewrites for human-written 59% of the time (Table 20) and separate them from real chat messages only slightly better than chance (55% accuracy, with a wide interval given the sample size). LLM judges are more easily fooled than people: they call 83–89% of the rewrites human-written, and are no better than chance at separating them from real messages. A trained classifier, in contrast, does separate our rewrites from the real turns they came from, in line with the computational Turing test of Pagan et al. (2026), who find that LLM-generated text stays distinguishable from human communication and that pushing it toward human-likeness costs semantic fidelity; by keeping the original request, our pipeline sits on the fidelity side of this trade-off. On synthetic seeds, rewriting still moves typing substantially toward real users and raises style diversity, while on real logs it narrows diversity somewhat, so there its value lies in multilingual extension (Appendix C). 

## 6 THE PERSONA FACTORY CONVERSATIONAL DATASET 

Applying the pipeline at scale to three widely used conversational datasets, UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023; Tunstall et al., 2023), Nemotron Instruction-Following (NVIDIA, 2025a; Nathawani et al., 2025; NVIDIA, 2025b) and WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), yields 1,237,976 augmented conversations after deduplication, language verification and judge filtering (1,606,837 before the final sanity gate), covering 85 languages, as summarized in Table 3. The released conversations contain 2,387,392 user turns, 1.93 per conversation on average. Real WildChat user turns make up more than half of the seeds, which broadens the corpus well beyond English (Appendix G.1). The corpus comprises both English-centered user conversations and multilingual ones, in which the persona’s native language is enforced in place of English. Assistant-side model personas. User-side personalization alone leaves the assistant with one standardized response style, with long, heavily formatted answers regardless of who is asking. As a pilot, we define 12 model personas with different response styles (e.g. answer-first compact prose, headed sections with numbered steps, or a gentle register for stressful topics), each with a free-text description, eight presentation axes (register, structure, density, warmth, explanation, jargon, certainty, emoji), optional signature touches ( p = 0 .25 per reply, as for user quirks) and content safeguards; explicit user instructions override the style. A matcher ( MiniMax-M2.5 ) picks a model persona for each user persona, and the assistant turns are regenerated with both. This applies to part of the release (234,723 conversations carry a model persona); in the assistant-side ablation of Appendix G.2 this setup scores best among the conditioning variants, and the styles can also be applied without a user persona. We treat model personas as a controllable source of assistant-style diversity and a starting point for future work rather than a core component of the pipeline. 

Downstream training. To measure what the data does for a model, we fine-tune 

Apertus-v1.5-8B base (Project Apertus et al., 2026) on four versions of the same language-matched conversations: the original conversations, the original user turns with a regenerated assistant, restyled user turns with the original assistant, and our release (Appendix G.3). Models trained on our data are on par with those trained on the original conversations on capability benchmarks except GSM8K, are preferred on held-out real user prompts at equal reply length, and adapt their reply style 9Preprint to the user more. Regenerating the assistant for the restyled user is essential, as restyling the user side alone lowers instruction following, and training only on the strict subset changes none of these results. On cross-lingual conversations, our data gives the largest gain on multilingual instruction following. 

## 7 IMPACT AND LIMITATIONS 

Impact. We hope our pipeline will stimulate a discussion on the effective use of synthetic personas in open-source data generation and LLM training. We release the persona bank and all generated conversations under a permissive license to further facilitate this effort; a key contribution is a culturally diverse dataset that we expect to be valuable for multilingual LLM development. The persona bank is fully synthetic. The conversations restyle openly released chat logs; for WildChat-seeded conversations, details already present in a seed can carry over into the rewrite, so this part inherits WildChat’s own anonymization (Appendix G.1). The pipeline relies on permissive open-weight models, providing reproducible and responsible resources for LLM training. At the same time, personas are conditioning variables, not real people or groups: they make no claim about how members of any nationality, region, gender, or profession actually communicate, and must not be used for profiling, targeting, or deceptive content presented as the first-person voice of a real individual. 

Limitations. Our work has several limitations, which we plan to address in future work. First, our IRB-approved human study is preliminary: it covers nine languages across three scripts, mostly with a single volunteer expert rater per language, so per-language results are indicative only (Appendix E). Human studies of synthetic personas with larger rater pools have so far been English-only, and multilingual persona datasets report no human evaluation or cover a single region (Appendix E.6); extending ours to several paid native raters per language is left to future work. Generation quality is also weaker in some languages, as the Greek panel shows. Second, the downstream study supports the complete pipeline rather than user-side restyling alone (Appendix G.3). Third, coverage remains imbalanced: a few major languages dominate the multilingual tail, and low digital fluency and code-switching remain minority traits. Fourth, the corpus realizes some chat markers, emoji in particular, more often than real users do, because it was generated without the explicit marker cap that we recommend (Appendix D); pattern correspondence with real users holds by language rather than by country (Appendix C); and dialect labels are not validated in text. Finally, the 12 hand-written model personas are a first step: the matcher favors a few of them, and the size of the persona set and on-policy persona sampling are open directions. When a source conversation carries its own system prompt, the style competes with its format and length constraints, so we leave such conversations without a model persona (Appendix G.2). 

## 8 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we present PersonaFactory, a pipeline that restyles the user side of existing conversations with synthetic personas while preserving the original intent. Our key contributions are a bank of 743,010 structured synthetic personas; a corpus of roughly 1.24 million personalized conversations in 85 languages, produced by a language-aware pipeline that re-simulates the user side of existing conversations while preserving intent and trajectory, with a strict intent label on every user turn; and an evaluation of the impact of persona features on intent and style. We find that separating persona background from writing style gives per-axis control with better intent preservation and moves synthetic seeds toward real-user typing, while a marker cap keeps the style natural. We believe this work lays a foundation for further research into the influence of persona detail on the conversational alignment of LLMs. 

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

The persona bank is fully synthetic, and the augmented conversations are based on the openly released chat datasets UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023), Nemotron Instruction-Following (NVIDIA, 2025a) and WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024), which we credit and use under their original terms. WildChat consists of real user logs whose personally identifiable information (names, phone numbers, email addresses, card numbers, URLs) was removed by its authors with Microsoft Presidio (Microsoft, 2024) and 10 Preprint hand-written rules before release (Zhao et al., 2024). We additionally ran Presidio over WildChat-seeded rewrites: they do not add contact details, but details that remain in a WildChat seed can carry over, so this part of the release inherits WildChat’s anonymization (Appendix G.1). No persona in the bank is derived from, or matched to, an identifiable individual. Personas are conditioning variables rather than descriptions of real groups: they make no claim about how members of any nationality, region, gender, or profession actually write, and we decouple occupation from region precisely to suppress the stereotypical associations we observed when the two were sampled jointly (Section 3). Every profile passes an independent judge that scores stereotype severity, and the residual risk of the release is the obvious one: a persona bank that encodes how people type can also be used to mass-produce stereotyped text, or to present synthetic output as the first-person voice of a real person. We release under a permissive license with these uses explicitly disallowed, and we discuss impact and residual limitations in Section 7. All generator and judge models used here are open-weight models with permissive usage terms. The human evaluation was approved by the ethics review board (IRB) of the authors’ institution and classified as minimal risk; participants were volunteer domain experts who gave informed consent, took part unpaid in their professional capacity, could skip items or withdraw at any time, and were identified only by random participant IDs, with no names, contact details, or IP addresses collected (Appendix E). 

## REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT 

To ensure reproducibility of our work, we are releasing the pipeline codebase and the produced persona bank together with a dataset of augmented conversations. We describe in full detail the process of synthetic persona creation (including persona schema, the curated country/sub-region and occupation grid, the models used for judging and generation) in Section 3 and Appendix A; further pipeline stages are described in Section 4, which provides information to reproduce the conversation augmentation using our proposed personas with details on the models used, generation settings (all sampling parameters in Appendix B.2), persona feature representation and feature-level sampling conditions. We also provide the prompts used to generate these parts (profile generation, user-turn adaptation, and the persona-conditioned responder prompt) verbatim in Appendix B.1 together with end-to-end examples of chat augmentation based on a provided persona in Appendix B.3. Ablation experiments and motivation behind selection of the final setup choices like judge models, style representation during augmentation and others are presented in Section 5, Appendix F and Appendix D. Corpus composition, matching-tier counts, and sanity-gate pass rates are reported in Section 6 and Appendix G.1. We publicly release the persona bank, the augmented conversational dataset, and the pipeline code under a permissive license.

## C EVALUATION DETAILS 

This appendix collects the evaluation details behind Section 5: the corpus-wide LLM-judge scores, two design ablations of the rewriting step, the judge-free metrics, and our checks of how much the results depend on the choice of judge. 

Corpus-scale LLM-as-a-judge evaluation. MiniMax-M2.5 , a reasoning model that is not one of the production rewriters, served with SGLang, scores every user turn of the conversations that carry a model persona (552,654 turns) and every user turn of a uniform random sample of 24,570 of the other conversations (44,687 turns). Corpus-wide rates weight the two strata by their share of the corpus’s user turns. The judge gives two independent pass/fail verdicts per turn: 1. Intent preservation: the rewrite still asks for the same thing as the original, adds no new task or invented content, and stays in the same language. 2. Style adoption: the rewrite takes on the persona’s tone, vocabulary, register or typing habits. Background facts about the persona are ignored; only a generic, unstyled rewrite fails. Homogenization. We measure style diversity as the mean pairwise distance between user turns in a style embedding. Turns of the same persona are more similar to each other than to other personas’ turns, so personas give consistent, distinguishable voices.

## D COMPARISON TO OTHER PERSONA REPRESENTATIONS 

Section 5 compares a structured style representation against a free-text one within our own schema. This appendix asks the same question against the formats the field actually uses: PersonaHub (Ge et al., 2025) and SynthPAI (Yukhymenko et al., 2024). 

Design. We hold the persona content fixed and vary only its serialization. From the released corpus we sample 800 conversations, stratified by persona set and seed source, together with the persona the pipeline matched to each. Sampling is independent of the released sanity verdict, and every condition is regenerated from scratch, so no condition inherits an advantage from the corpus filtering. Each profile is then rendered four ways and used to rewrite the same seed prompt with the same rewriter, decoding parameters, judge and prompt scaffold. Only the persona block differs. The PersonaHub rendering is a single third-person sentence. This is not a simplification on our part: across all 200,000 released PersonaHub personas, only 1.4% mention any writing cue at all, so a projection that described typing behavior would not be testing PersonaHub. The SynthPAI rendering keeps the feature types that schema has, an attribute block plus a free-text writing-style description with a verbatim example, and drops the per-axis style keys, which it has no slot for. Projections were produced by a third model that is neither rewriter nor judge, and were accepted only after matching the real PersonaHub length distribution and style-mention rate. Table 15 reports what survives each rendering. 

Result. Table 16 and Figure 23 show a dose-response in how explicitly a representation encodes writing behavior. Style adoption rises monotonically across the four conditions, and the judge prefers the PersonaFactory rewrite in every pairwise comparison (share of items where the judge prefers the PersonaFactory rewrite, with order randomized per item). Style adoption and intent use the production judge templates; paired bootstrap intervals for the judge-free differences are at most 

±0.05. The contested comparison is against the SynthPAI form, which also carries style information 52 Preprint and differs from ours mainly in whether that information is structured; there the preference is 4.83 to 1. Two observations do not follow from the setup. First, the PersonaHub form is not inert. It moves the rewrite away from the no-persona baseline about as far as the other conditions do, yet lifts style adoption only from 3.1% to 5.5%: it changes the text without changing it toward the persona. Second, the representation propagates past the user turn. Measured against the no-persona reply for the same item, the assistant’s response moves further as the representation grows richer, while assistant sanity stays flat across conditions, so this is a change of register rather than of quality. 

Cost. The gains are not free, and the cost is concentrated rather than uniform. Intent preservation is essentially unaffected in English but falls to 89.2% on the cross-lingual half, and entity retention declines by four points. Transformation-depth metrics also fall, but those measure how much the surface changed and are the intended effect rather than a loss. 

Robustness. Table 17 reports three checks. The ranking is unchanged with Ministral-3-14B as rewriter, though the margin against the SynthPAI form narrows from 4.83 to 2.64, consistent with Appendix F finding that the rewriter dominates attribute effects. Re-judging a 200-item subset with four judges leaves the ordering intact while absolute rates span fifteen points, matching the severity spread in Appendix C; item-level agreement with the primary judge is 0.81 to 0.93. Finally, judge responses that could not be parsed are not uniformly distributed across conditions, so we report a worst-case bound in which every unresolved comparison is assigned to the competing condition. The SynthPAI comparison holds at 76.8% under that assumption. 

Format-neutral re-judging and real personas. In the comparison above, the style and pairwise judges see the persona rendered in the PersonaFactory format, which the PersonaFactory rewriter also saw, so a rewrite could in principle score by echoing phrasing from its own prompt. We therefore re-judge the same 3,200 rewrites against a format-neutral reference that lists only the persona’s writing-behaviour attribute values (structured typing keys, sentence length, question framing, code-switching frequency) with all example phrasing removed, using the pipeline judge and two external judges ( Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Muse-Glimmer-30B ). We also rewrite every item with a real PersonaHub persona and a real SynthPAI profile in their native form, with the same rewriter and template, and compare each with the PersonaFactory rewrite in a reference-free pairwise judgment of which rewrite gives the message a more distinctive and realistic individual voice while keeping the request intact. Figure 21 shows that PersonaFactory keeps the highest style adoption under every judge and is preferred over both projected formats and over real PersonaHub personas, with every interval above parity. Real SynthPAI profiles, whose hand-written style descriptions cover 300 English-speaking Reddit users, are on par with PersonaFactory; they are preferred on cross-lingual prompts, where our persona speaks a different language than the prompt, while PersonaFactory scales the same style fidelity to a bank of nearly 100,000 multilingual profiles with per-axis control. 

Naturalness. Stronger stylization has a cost. In a reference-free judgment of whether a message reads as something a real person would type or as a caricature, the pipeline judge passes 99% of rewrites in every condition, but the two external judges rate PersonaFactory rewrites 7 to 12 points less natural than unconditioned rewrites. We attribute this to the richer behaviour specification: a profile that asks for lowercase typing, dropped punctuation, abbreviations, emoji and a sign-off at the same time invites the rewriter to realize several markers in one short message, which a judge can read as over-performed, whereas formats that specify little change little. The per-turn quirk probability has no consistent effect on this; an explicit cap on the number of markers realized per turn does, as the next paragraph shows.

## E HUMAN STUDY 

In this section we describe the expert human study that complements the LLM-judge evaluation of Section 5. We first describe the study design (§E.1), then give the information and instructions shown to participants (§E.2), the ethics review and compensation (§E.3), the interim results (§E.4), and example items (§E.7). E.1 STUDY DESCRIPTION 

The study uses one form per language, 60 languages in total, each administered as a self-paced online session. A form contains up to 40 items, ten in each of four parts; forms for languages with fewer available samples contain fewer items. Participants are experts in the AI field who rate only the language varieties they know well. They may complete up to three sessions, one per language, and are identified only by a random participant ID. The four parts measure different properties and are never combined into a single score: • Part A, meaning preservation. An original user message is shown next to its persona rewrite from the corpus, and participants rate whether the rewrite preserves the meaning (intent and content) of the original. For cross-lingual rewrites the original is the English source message. • Part B, style fidelity. A persona’s free-text chatting-style description from the persona bank is shown next to a message rewritten for that persona, and participants rate how well the message matches the described style without being an exaggerated stereotype or caricature. • Part C, human or model. Participants judge from the text alone whether a message was written by a real person or generated by a language model, and state their confidence. Each form mixes persona rewrites from the corpus with messages intended to be human-written user requests from a public chat-log corpus (Zhao et al., 2024), in equal proportion; the origin of each text is not disclosed. The answer options follow the wording of the SynthPAI human study (Yukhymenko et al., 2024). • Part D, linguistic plausibility. Participants rate whether a short user message is plausible as something a real speaker of the language variety would write, and may report the item for personal information or offensive content. Parts A, B and D and the Part C confidence use five-point scales. All displayed texts come from public research datasets or machine-generated rewrites and were filtered for personal information and disturbing content before the study. Any item can be skipped or reported, and reported items are removed. E.2 INFORMATION AND INSTRUCTIONS FOR PARTICIPANTS 

We reproduce the study information and instructions as presented to participants, omitting only the investigator names, institution, ethics application number, and contact details. STUDY INFORMATION 

Purpose. This study evaluates a research pipeline that rewrites the user side of chat conversations so that language-model training data better reflects how linguistically diverse people write (informal register, second-language phrasing, code-switching) while preserving meaning. As an expert in the AI field, you judge properties of displayed texts. The study analyzes texts and models, not you; no analyses of individual participants are performed. 

What participation involves. Each item shows one or two short chat messages with a rating question (meaning preservation, style fidelity, human-vs-LLM origin judgment, or linguistic plausibility). Some tasks ask you to judge whether a displayed text was written by a human or generated by a language model; the origin of each text is therefore not disclosed before you answer. This task structure is openly described here and involves no other withheld information. This session contains 40 study items. Completion time varies with reading pace and breaks. You may complete up to 3 sessions, one per language. 

Risks. Minimal, comparable to everyday reading and web use. Texts come from public research datasets or machine-generated rewrites and are filtered for personal information and disturbing 58 Preprint content; residually mildly offensive text cannot be fully excluded. You can skip or report any item; reported items are removed. 

Voluntariness and withdrawal. Participation is entirely voluntary and unpaid, in your professional capacity. Persons in a dependency relationship to the investigators may not participate. You may skip, pause, or withdraw at any point without disadvantage. Deletion of your ratings is possible for as long as they can be linked to your participant ID; after anonymization at study completion it is no longer possible. 

Data protection. Ratings, timestamps, and optional coarse language background are stored pseudony-mously under a random participant ID. No names, contact details, or IP addresses are collected. Data are stored on institutional infrastructure with restricted access; consent records are retained securely and separately. Only aggregated or anonymized results are published. Before any study item is shown, participants confirm each of the following statements: • I have read and understood the study information above and had the opportunity to ask questions. • I am at least 18 years old. • I understand that participation is voluntary and unpaid, and that I may skip items or withdraw at any time without giving reasons and without disadvantage. • I understand how my data will be processed, stored, and published, as described above. • I understand that some tasks involve judging whether a displayed text was written by a human or by a language model, and that the origin of each text is not disclosed to me before I answer. • I agree to take part in this study. ## F PER -ATTRIBUTE AUGMENTATION ANALYSIS 

Aggregate fidelity tells us that persona conditioning works, but not which part of a profile is doing the work or what it costs. We therefore run a controlled, one-attribute-at-a-time ablation that decomposes the augmentation into three separately measured aspects. To our knowledge, prior user-simulation and persona-conditioning work reports fidelity only in aggregate; a per-attribute decomposition of a persona-augmentation pipeline is, to our knowledge, new. 

Setup. From a stratified pool of up to 194 real source messages, we build single-attribute personas: a no-persona baseline plus sixteen conditions that vary exactly one attribute and collapse into eight families (digital fluency, sentence length, AI stance, age, gender, education, occupation, hobbies). Each condition is rewritten by all four generators ( Gemma-4-31B , Ministral-3-14B ,

GLM-4.7-Flash , MiniMax-M2.5 ), holding everything else fixed, for 12,845 valid rewrites. Every rewrite is scored on three orthogonal aspects: style adoption (does the turn take on the attribute’s register?), intent preservation (is it still the same request in the same language?), and rule-based script stability ; the no-persona baseline fixes the script-stability ceiling at 0.987. Table 23 reports the eight families, averaged over the four generators; Figure 24 gives the full per-condition grid behind it. 

Reading the decomposition. We present these numbers as a measurement, not an importance ranking. Across all eight families, style adoption sits in a narrow 0.80–0.82 band, intent preservation clusters near 0.62, and script stability decrements uniformly by roughly 0.06–0.07 from the no-persona baseline. When the pipeline leaves a turn alone. About 94% of released user turns are rewritten in the persona’s voice. The remaining 6% (146,643 turns) are kept unchanged on purpose. We spell out why, because a turn that was deliberately protected looks the same from the outside as one where the rewriter failed. There are five reasons (the remaining 0.2% are rewriter calls that timed out): • Truncation (21.6%, 31,716 turns): the rewrite came back much shorter than the original, which usually means content was lost rather than restyled. • Strict-format tasks (14.4%, 21,116): verbatim repetition, translation or quoted-output requests, where restyling would break exactly what the user asked for. • Lost protected content (31.0%, 45,453): every retry dropped a protected quote, code span or number. • Script or language change (29.1%, 42,607): the rewrite switched the script or language of the turn, or the persona’s language did not match the script of the prompt. • No change (3.7%, 5,460): the rewriter returned the message as it was. In each of these cases we prefer to ship the original turn over a broken request or a language mismatch. One number may look surprisingly low: code-switching markers appear in only 1.95% of user turns (measured on the 552,654 user turns of conversations with a model persona), although many more personas are described as code-switchers. This is intended. Linguistic quirks are applied with a fixed probability rather than at every opportunity, because a persona that uses every mannerism in every message reads as a caricature rather than a person (Section 4). Each quirk is meant to be rare in a single turn and visible across the corpus.

Dialect labels. Personas carry a free-text dialect label: the whole bank has 117,670 distinct labels, and the personas used in the conversations carry 81,388 distinct labels after case and whitespace normalization, so the same dialect can be phrased differently across personas. We group them into canonical varieties per language and keep a variety only when it belongs to the language of the conversation, which leaves 13,714 varieties: 1,639 have at least 100 user turns, 416 have at least 1,000, and 695 are carried by at least 100 distinct personas. Frequent labels include regional varieties such as Northern English, New Zealand English and Swiss German. These counts describe the personas that condition the rewrites; verifying how faithfully each dialect is realized in text would require native speakers for each variety, which we leave to future work.

Matching and generation. During assistant regeneration, the style section is appended to the paper’s persona system prompt on every turn, with an explicit priority order: explicit user instructions about format, length, tone or language first, then the content rules, correctness and safety, and only then the style. A signature touch is offered with p = 0 .25 per reply (25.0% of released assistant turns), and the continuity adaptation step keeps the style. A limitation of this mode appears when the source conversation carries its own system prompt. On 200 single-turn conversations with a checkable system constraint (at most three sentences, a numbered list, under 80 words, or no Markdown), compliance is 86% without a style, but 58% with the style placed below the system prompt and 66% with the system prompt placed last as an explicit override. G.3 DOWNSTREAM FINE -TUNING ON THE RELEASED CORPUS 

The ablation above varies only the assistant side. Here we ask what the released conversations, with their persona-styled user turns, do for a model trained on them, and which part of the pipeline is responsible. 

Setup. We fine-tune Apertus-v1.5-8B base (Project Apertus et al., 2026) for one epoch on four versions of the same 79,817 language-matched conversations from the training split, sampled with 5% of the personas held out for evaluation. The arms differ only in their turns: (A0) the original seed conversation; (A0r) the original user turns with the assistant turns regenerated by the same responder, with no persona, the same continuity loop and the same sanity gate; (A1) the persona-styled user turns with the original assistant turns; and (A2) the released conversation, with styled user turns and assistant turns regenerated for that user. Conversations whose A0r regeneration failed the sanity gate, or that exceed 3,800 tokens in any arm, are removed from all arms. Training uses Megatron-LM with 72 Preprint packed 4,096-token sequences, loss on every assistant turn, global batch 128, and a learning rate of 

10 −5 decayed to 10 −6 with a cosine schedule after 3% warmup. 

Evaluation. We report clean capability with lm-evaluation-harness (thinking disabled), robust-ness as the drop from the clean prompt to a perturbed one on IFEval, GSM8K and MBPP, and a pairwise comparison on real user messages. Perturbations are either rule-based (lowercasing, punctuation removal, light and heavy typos, abbreviations, terse and combined variants) or per-sona rewrites of the benchmark prompt by three held-out personas ( Gemma-4-31B rewriter, kept only if MiniMax-M2.5 confirms that the task is unchanged, 99.6% of rewrites). Results. Table 32 and Table 33 show three findings. First, styled user turns help only together with assistant turns written for them. Training on styled user turns with the original replies (A1) lowers IFEval against the original data, while the released conversations (A2) score highest on IFEval and clearly beat A1. The A2 advantage over A1 on IFEval holds across three training seeds (7.2, 6.5 and 3.3 points with lm-evaluation-harness; 5.7 [1.9, 9.6] with intervals over items and seeds), and on real WildChat prompts both external judges prefer A2 replies over A1 replies at equal length for every seed (57.3 to 60.0%). Second, the released conversations match or improve on the original data: against A0, A2 gains 1.8 points of IFEval strict accuracy on average over seeds and loses 3.1 points on GSM8K, the only benchmark where it is consistently behind, and both judges prefer A2 replies over A0 replies at equal length for every seed (54.6 to 57.5%). Third, none of the arms measurably changes robustness to messy prompts: rule-based gaps stay small for every arm, and no gap difference between arms excludes zero. Persona rewrites of benchmark prompts cost every arm more than rule-based noise. Regenerating the assistant side without a persona (A0r) more than doubles the reply length, so the comparisons against A0r depend on the length correction, and the two judges disagree on A2 against A0r at equal length for every seed. Over three training seeds, A2 gains 4.6 points [1.1, 8.2] of IFEval strict accuracy over A0r and A1 loses 3.8 points [ −6.7, −0.9] against A0 (lm-evaluation-harness, intervals over items and seeds).
