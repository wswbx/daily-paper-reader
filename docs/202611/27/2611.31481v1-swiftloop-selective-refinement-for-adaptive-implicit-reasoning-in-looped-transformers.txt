## SWIFT LOOP : Selective Refinement for Adaptive Implicit Reasoning in Looped Transformers 

Tianyi Zhao 1, Ke Wan 1, Chen Chen 1

Abstract 

Looped Transformers advance implicit chain-of-thought reasoning through recurrent latent refinement. However, existing looped reason-ers typically employ fixed-depth recurrence, updating all latent positions at every iteration and incurring redundancy when further refine-ment is unnecessary. We introduce S WIFT -LOOP , an inference-time framework for se-lective recurrent refinement through position-wise freezing, enabling adaptive computation allocation across both recurrent depth and ac-tive width. SwiftLoop learns lightweight freez-ing policies through hindsight-guided supervi-sion derived from two complementary criteria: K/V reusability at the internal representation level and functional impact at the output level. At inference time, these policies guide adap-tive freezing based on observed hidden states. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Swift-Loop achieves favorable accuracy–computation trade-offs over fixed-loop inference and adap-tive baselines, with measured inference accel-eration. Beyond implicit reasoning, we fur-ther demonstrate its applicability to recurrent prompt prefilling in general looped models. 

1 Introduction 

Implicit chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning has emerged as an efficient alternative to explicit CoT, replacing token-by-token reasoning traces with con-tinuous intermediate states (Wei et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2023, 2024). Representative approaches include Coconut (Hao et al., 2025), which em-ploys curriculum learning to progressively replace explicit reasoning steps with latent states, and CODI (Shen et al., 2025), which learns a latent reasoning pathway through self-distillation from an explicit CoT pathway. Despite substantial ef-fort, implicit reasoning methods have yet to match the performance of explicit CoT on challenging reasoning tasks (He et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026). One direction is to increase internal computation. Looped Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2019; Geip-ing et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Saunshi et al., 2025) thus offer a natural mechanism by repeat-edly applying shared modules for recurrent internal state refinement, increasing effective computational depth while reusing model parameters. Building on this framing, Fan et al. (2026) introduce LOTUS, which iteratively refines a parallel workspace of latent slots, using token-level supervision from ex-plicit CoT steps during training, and close much of the performance gap between implicit and explicit CoT reasoning. Despite effectiveness, it employs a fixed inference strategy: every instance executes the same number of loops, and every latent slot is updated at each iteration, regardless of whether fur-ther refinement is necessary, incurring redundant computation (Figure 1a,b). This motivates adaptive computation alloca-tion (Bae et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Yang and Liu, 2026; Fu et al., 2026), which remains chal-lenging along both depth and width: neither the number of loops sufficient for each instance nor the latent slots that still benefit from further updates are known in advance at inference time. Position-wise freezing offers a unified strategy for adapting both dimensions: a latent slot can cease further updates while its cached keys and values (K/V) remain ac-cessible to active positions through attention. The central challenge is thus determining when further refinements can be safely omitted. Addressing this challenge intuitively entails two perspectives: in-ternal representation level and output function level . For the former, one straightforward way is to consider K/V reusability. Inter-iteration state variation provides a natural but retrospective con-vergence signal (Yang and Liu, 2026). However, stability observed thus far may precede future K/V drift (Figure 1c). Together, they motivate hindsight-guided freezing decisions for adaptive looping that spans both levels. To this end, we propose SwiftLoop , an adaptive looping policy that enables position-wise selective freezing for adaptive computation at inference. Af-ter each loop, a lightweight predictor determines which active slots to freeze based on their observed hidden states, thereby regulating per-position effec-tive depth and per-loop active width. We investigate two alternative supervision objectives for learning this freezing policy: K/V reusability, which charac-terizes the future consistency of cached K/V; and functional impact, which estimates the effect of freezing a latent slot on the model’s final predic-tion. Beyond implicit reasoning, we further extend this selective freezing policy to recurrent prompt prefilling, broadening its scope from specialized latent slots to general context-token states. We evaluate SwiftLoop across four benchmarks using LOTUS (Fan et al., 2026), a looped model for im-plicit reasoning, at varying scales, and further ex-amine its applicability to recurrent prompt prefill-ing on two general looped models, Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) and Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025), across three datasets. Extensive experiments demonstrate that SwiftLoop improves computational adaptivity while maintaining competitive task performance relative to fixed-loop inference.

3 Empirical Analysis of Recurrent Computation 

Setup. We analyze the publicly released LOTUS-3B (Fan et al., 2026) checkpoint, a looped model for implicit reasoning fine-tuned on GSM8K-Aug (Deng et al., 2023). It refines 150 latent slots over six recurrent loops. From the GSM8K vali-dation and test sets, we select 1,648 samples with complete gold reasoning traces to analyze loop requirements. We then use correctly solved valida-tion samples for latent-level analyses. 

Nonuniform Loop Requirements. We investi-gate computational requirements across both sam-ples and latent positions. We group samples from the GSM8K validation and test sets by the number of gold reasoning steps k and examine the loop depth sufficient for correct predictions. As shown in Figure 1a, easier samples, characterized by fewer gold reasoning steps, tend to require fewer loops. At the position level, we examine the earliest loop after which freezing each latent slot individually changes the final prediction margin by less than 1%. These freezing depths vary both across and within samples (Figure 1b). Together, they high-light the potential for adaptive computation alloca-tion across loop depth and active positions. 

Local Convergence and Future Reusability. 

From the internal representation perspective, a key consideration is whether cached K/V remain reusable throughout subsequent computation. We define a slot as reusable after loop t if its cached K/V remain within a tolerance ϵ along the re-maining unfrozen trajectory. A natural proxy for reusability is local convergence over previous loops. However, local K/V stability does not necessarily imply future reusability: 58% of slot–loop obser-vations exhibiting below-threshold inter-iteration K/V changes subsequently drift beyond ϵ = 0.45 (Figure 1c). This motivates future-oriented crite-rion of reusability beyond observed convergence. 

Representation Drift and Functional Impact. 

From the output perspective, we consider func-tional impact, which captures the predictive conse-quences of freezing. Using exact single-slot coun-terfactuals, we measure the relative change in the final prediction logit margin when the remaining loops are recomputed with only that slot frozen. Among slot–loop observations exhibiting substan-tial future K/V drift, 63% induce margin changes below 1% (Figure 1d). These findings highlight the distinction between input-level representational consistency and output-level functional impact as two alternative criteria for adaptive freezing. 

Inferring Future Utility from Current States. 

Both criteria depend on future computation unavail-able when freezing decisions are made. Current hidden states offer a natural source of predictive information, as they feed into subsequent loops. Yet their observed dynamics do not directly track predictive correctness: across 1,350 GSM8K vali-dation and test instances, hidden-state norms follow similar trajectories regardless of when predictions first become settled, increasing sharply in later loops (Figure 2). 5 Experiments 

5.1 Experimental Setup Models. We evaluate SwiftLoop on various looped models spanning two recurrent computation settings. For implicit reasoning , we consider three LOTUS configurations (Fan et al., 2026), each re-fining 150 latent positions over six recurrent loops. Alongside the released LOTUS-3B checkpoint, we train CODI-style LOTUS-1B and LOTUS-3B us-ing self-distillation (Shen et al., 2025) on GSM8K-Aug (Deng et al., 2023), initialized from Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, respec-tively (Grattafiori et al., 2024). For recurrent prompt prefilling , we evaluate Ouro-1.4B (Zhu et al., 2025), which executes four recurrent steps over 24 layers, and Huginn-0125 (Geiping et al., 2025), a 3.5B model with a four-layer recurrent block iterated 32 times. 

Datasets. For implicit reasoning, we evaluate on the GSM8K test set (Cobbe et al., 2021) and three out-of-distribution benchmarks: SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), MultiArith (Roy and Roth, 2015), and GSM-Hard (Gao et al., 2023). For recur-rent prompt prefilling, we consider three bench-marks: WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), and Hel-laSwag (Zellers et al., 2019). 

Baselines. We compare against fixed-depth infer-ence and a broad range of adaptive computation methods. Fixed-depth baselines execute a uniform number of recurrent steps. Adaptive baselines in-clude FlashLoop (Yang and Liu, 2026), which allo-cates computation based on hidden-state changes; H2O (Zhang et al., 2023) and LazyLLM (Fu et al., 2024), which use attention-based importance; CALM (Schuster et al., 2022) and GCEE (Gu et al., 2026), which leverage hidden-state conver-gence; SureLock (Oba et al., 2026) and the Huginn KL exit (Geiping et al., 2025), which use output-distribution stability. More details in Appendix C. 

SwiftLoop Variants. We evaluate three variants of SwiftLoop, sharing the same online freezing pol-icy and predictor architecture but differing in their supervision: K/V reusability, exact counterfactual impact, and integrated-gradient impact. 

Evaluation Metrics. We report prediction accu-racy (%) and average recurrent computation. For implicit reasoning, computation is measured by the average number of loops per latent position, exclud-ing the initialization pass. For recurrent prompt pre-filling, we report the average number of recurrent steps per context token.

6 Related Work 

Implicit Reasoning . Implicit chain-of-thought reasoning replaces explicit reasoning traces with continuous latent-state computation (Deng et al., 2023, 2024). Prior work advances implicit rea-soning through diverse strategies, including pause tokens (Goyal et al., 2024), curriculum-based latent reasoning (Hao et al., 2025), self-distillation (Shen et al., 2025), reasoning compression (Cheng and Van Durme, 2024; Xu et al., 2025), and semantic or step-level supervision (He et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026). PCCoT (Wu et al., 2025b) explores parallel latent reasoning, while LOTUS (Fan et al., 2026) builds on a looped Transformer architecture to iter-atively refine latent slots for implicit reasoning. 

Looped Transformers and Adaptive Computa-tion . Looped Transformers increase effective com-putational depth through repeated application of shared modules (Dehghani et al., 2019). Their ca-8pabilities for algorithmic and multi-step reason-ing have been extensively studied (Giannou et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Saunshi et al., 2025). Re-cent recurrent-depth language models, including Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025) and Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025), further demonstrate the potential of iterative latent computation. The computational demands of recurrence have inspired efficient inference through K/V cache optimization (Wu et al., 2025a; Vendrell et al., 2026), efficient prefilling (Wan and Chen, 2026; Du et al., 2026), and accelerated decoding (Yu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2026). Adaptive computation further improves recurrent efficiency by allocating computation across inputs and positions. Existing approaches learn adaptive depth through dynamic halting and token-wise rout-ing (Graves, 2016; Banino et al., 2021; Bae et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026), while inference-time methods guide selective updates through attention importance, hidden-state dynam-ics, and output-distribution stability (Zhang et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2024; Schuster et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2026; Yang and Liu, 2026; Oba et al., 2026; Geiping et al., 2025). SwiftLoop learns position-wise freezing decisions through hindsight-guided supervision, considering future K/V reusability at the internal representation level and functional im-pact at the output level. 

7 Conclusion 

We present S WIFT LOOP , a hindsight-guided frame-work for selective refinement in looped Transform-ers. Through position-wise freezing and K/V reuse, SwiftLoop adaptively allocates computation across recurrent depth and active width. Lightweight pre-dictors learn from hindsight supervision based on representation-level K/V reusability or output-level functional impact, and guide adaptive freezing us-ing only current hidden states at inference time. Extensive experiments demonstrate favorable ac-curacy–computation trade-offs and practical infer-ence acceleration in implicit reasoning, with further generalization to recurrent prompt prefilling.

B Experimental Details 

This section provides additional details on the model configurations, evaluation benchmarks, pre-dictor fitting, and experimental protocols used in Section 5. 

B.1 Models and Benchmarks LOTUS Models. We evaluate three LOTUS con-figurations (Fan et al., 2026), covering differ-ent model scales and implicit-reasoning training recipes. All three models use a recurrent workspace of 150 latent positions and perform six recurrent refinement loops following an initialization pass. The first configuration is the released LOTUS-3B checkpoint, which follows the Coconut-style post training adaptation. To assess SwiftLoop across different training recipes, we additionally train two CODI-style LOTUS models using the self-distillation approach of CODI (Shen et al., 2025) on GSM8K-Aug (Deng et al., 2023). The 1B and 3B configurations are initialized from Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, respectively 11 (Grattafiori et al., 2024). All three configurations share the same recurrent workspace structure and freezing interface. Under full six-loop inference, CODI-style LOTUS-1B, CODI-style LOTUS-3B, and Coconut-style LOTUS-3B achieve 59.29%, 70.74%, and 70.20% accuracy on GSM8K test, respectively. 

General Looped Models. We further evaluate SwiftLoop on Ouro-1.4B (Zhu et al., 2025) and Huginn-0125 (Geiping et al., 2025), which perform recurrent computation over prompt-token represen-tations. Ouro-1.4B has 24 layers and executes four recurrent steps. Huginn-0125 contains approxi-mately 3.5B parameters and applies a four-layer recurrent block for 32 steps, preceded by a two-layer prelude and followed by a two-layer coda. Unlike LOTUS, where recurrence refines latent reasoning positions, these models recurrently pro-cess context-token states. SwiftLoop applies the same position-wise freezing rule to eligible con-text positions, preserving their cached K/V for at-tention in subsequent steps. We enable adaptive freezing after two recurrent steps for Ouro and four for Huginn, following the warm-up conven-tion of FlashLoop (Yang and Liu, 2026). Huginn’s stochastic initial state is generated using a fixed seed, ensuring identical initialization across evalu-ated methods. 

Implicit-Reasoning Benchmarks. We evaluate mathematical reasoning on four benchmarks: • GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021): 1,319 test prob-lems involving multi-step grade-school arith-metic and mathematical reasoning. • SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021): 1,000 mathe-matical word problems designed to evaluate robustness to variations in problem structure and linguistic formulation. • MultiArith (Roy and Roth, 2015): 180 multi-step arithmetic word problems. • GSM-Hard (Gao et al., 2023): 1,319 chal-lenging numerical variations of GSM8K prob-lems. GSM8K serves as the primary in-distribution benchmark, while SVAMP, MultiArith, and GSM-Hard evaluate out-of-distribution generalization. All models produce answers through greedy de-coding after recurrent computation. We extract the numerical answer and compare it with the reference answer for correctness. 

Prompt-Prefilling Benchmarks. We evaluate re-current prompt prefilling on three benchmarks: • WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2021): a com-monsense reasoning benchmark requiring res-olution of ambiguous references. We use five-shot evaluation on 1,267 validation instances. • ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018): multiple-choice science questions requiring nontrivial reasoning and background knowl-edge. We use 25-shot evaluation on 1,172 test instances. • HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019): a common-sense inference benchmark requiring selec-tion of plausible event continuations. We use ten-shot evaluation on 10,042 validation in-stances. Following the evaluation protocol of lm-evaluation-harness (Biderman et al., 2024), we score each candidate continuation by its conditional log-likelihood and select the highest-scoring candi-date. For ARC-Challenge and HellaSwag, candi-date scores are normalized by continuation length. The same prompting and scoring protocol is used across methods for each backbone and benchmark. 

B.2 Implementation Details Freezing and Feature Extraction. SwiftLoop applies irreversible position-wise freezing while retaining cached K/V for attention by active po-sitions. For LOTUS, the initialization pass is in-dexed by t = 0 and the six recurrent loops by 

t = 1 , . . . , 6. Freezing decisions may be made after passes t = 0 , . . . , 5. The predictor input con-catenates the current hidden state with its most recent update, as defined in Eq. (6). For the initial pass, where no preceding recurrent state is avail-able, we set the update component to zero. For recurrent prompt prefilling, freezing operates on context-token positions. Frozen positions retain their hidden states and cached K/V. The active-position set decreases monotonically over recurrent steps.

Predictor Fitting. We fit separate freezing pre-dictors for each backbone and supervision objec-tive. For recurrent prompt prefilling, predictors are additionally fitted separately for each task. Each predictor receives the concatenated hidden-state features defined in Eq. (6). We consider a lin-ear predictor and a one-hidden-layer MLP with 256 GELU units. Input features are standardized, and both architectures are optimized using AdamW with learning rate 10 −3. We select the predictor architecture and training duration using five-fold cross-validation, partitioned by problem or docu-ment to keep observations from the same input within a single fold. Training is limited to six epochs. The selected configuration is subsequently fitted for online use. Both supervision objectives use the log-scale regression loss in Eq. (8). We set δ = 3 × 10 −5 for functional impact in recur-rent prompt prefilling, and 10 −3 otherwise. Higher predicted freezing scores correspond to smaller es-timated freezing measures. For LOTUS, predictors are fitted using 500 GSM8K validation problems. For Ouro and Hug-inn, supervision is constructed from the training split of the corresponding task, with separate pre-dictors fitted for each model–task combination. 

Threshold Selection. We select the online freez-ing threshold from a candidate grid by evaluating the resulting freezing schedules. For each configu-ration, we choose the threshold minimizing average recurrent computation subject to a prescribed ac-curacy tolerance relative to dense inference. We consider 0.5-point loss , where a validation accuracy degradation of at most 0.5 percentage point is per-mitted. When no candidate satisfies the constraint, we retain the highest-accuracy configuration. For LOTUS, threshold selection uses the same 500 GSM8K validation problems used for predic-tor fitting. The selected threshold is applied un-changed to GSM8K test, SVAMP, MultiArith, and GSM-Hard. For recurrent prompt prefilling, we use task-specific selection instances disjoint from the predictor-fitting inputs. The selection sets contain 600 WinoGrande, 719 ARC-Challenge, and 600 HellaSwag training-split instances, respectively. No selection uses the final evaluation instances. All thresholds are fixed before evaluation. The chosen operating points are determined from full-policy executions, thereby reflecting the joint effect of freezing multiple positions. 

Computational Resources. All methods use the same checkpoint and evaluation protocol within each model configuration. Experiments were con-ducted using NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

C Baselines 

We describe the baseline methods used in our ex-periments, including their original decision criteria, adaptations to recurrent computation, and configu-ration procedures. 

Common Protocol. For adaptive baselines orig-inally designed for other inference settings, we adapt their selection criteria to position-wise recur-rent computation. In LOTUS, freezing operates on latent reasoning positions. In Ouro and Hug-inn, it operates on context-token positions. Frozen positions retain their cached K/V for attention by subsequently updated positions. Unless otherwise specified, baseline configurations are fixed before evaluation. H2O and LazyLLM use the same accuracy-constrained selection rule as SwiftLoop, while methods with established calibration proce-dures or published defaults retain their respective settings. Detailed baseline configurations and cali-bration procedures are described below.

H2O. H2O (Zhang et al., 2023) was originally proposed for efficient generative inference through KV-cache management.
