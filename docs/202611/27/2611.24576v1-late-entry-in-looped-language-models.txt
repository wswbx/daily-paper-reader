Abstract 

Looped language models deepen reasoning at a compact parameter count by iterating a shared layer stack 𝑇 times per token, turning recurrence depth directly into sequential decoding latency. Conventional early-exit decoding halts after initial passes without cache completion, truncating cached key-value states of past tokens to shallow depth. We study the opposite strategy, late entry ,where new tokens skip initial passes and enter directly into the final 𝐾 < 𝑇 passes over a deep context cache. Once lightweight fine-tuning resolves hidden-state norm mismatch at the entry boundary, deep cached context substitutes for skipped token-local passes, allowing late entry to outperform matched early exit across model families and pass budgets. To turn this asymmetry into wall-clock speedups without stalling to build deep caches, candidate blocks verify through only the final 𝐾 passes on the critical path while previously committed tokens complete passes 1 → 𝑇 alongside subsequent steps at zero extra serial calls . Projecting these finished 𝑇 -pass wake states inside the existing output GEMM ( Wake verification , governed by logit-margin tolerance 𝜏 ∈ [ 0, ∞] ) unifies greedy-exact 

𝑇 -pass decoding (𝜏 = 0, matching 𝐾 = 𝑇 block decoding within bf16 reduction order), margin-gated verification ( 𝜏 > 0), and non-rollback lossy late entry ( 𝜏 = ∞) inside a single static CUDA graph. ## 1 Introduction 

Looped language models scale test-time compute without increasing model size by passing each token repeatedly through a shared Transformer stack for 𝑇 recurrent passes (Dehghani et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). During autoregressive decoding, however, running all 𝑇 passes per generated token turns recurrence depth directly into sequential latency. When each new token is restricted to 𝐾 < 𝑇 

serial passes, which passes should it execute? As shown in Figure 1, conventional early exit without cache completion (Schuster et al., 2022) runs the first 𝐾 passes and halts (Figure 1b), leaving past key-value (KV) caches truncated at shallow depth 𝐾 so all future tokens attend to immature context states. We explore the opposite choice, late entry , which skips initial passes and feeds each new token’s embedding directly into the final 𝐾 passes over a deep context KV cache (Figure 1c). At first glance, skipping initial passes seems destined to fail: on off-the-shelf Ouro-1.4B and Ouro-2.6B (𝑇 = 4) (Zhu et al., 2025), feeding raw embeddings into a late pass breaks predictions immediately because late passes expect normalized hidden states from earlier passes. Once lightweight fine-tuning aligns late passes to accept raw embeddings, however, a clear asymmetry emerges between token depth and context depth: one final pass over a four-pass context matches full-depth predictions more closely than two initial passes over a two-pass context ( +10 + accuracy points in sequential decoding). Under identical training data, hyperparameters, and steps, spending 𝐾 serial passes at the end of the recurrence consistently outperforms spending them at the beginning across both Ouro scales ( 𝑇 = 4; Section 2) and 32-pass middle-recurrent Huginn-3.5B ( 𝑇 = 32; Section 4.4), showing that deep cached context retains much of the benefit of recurrent depth. Turning this finding into wall-clock speedups requires building deep context states without stalling genera-tion. In late-entry block decoding paired with Wake verification (Section 3), parameter-free candidate blocks (Liu et al., 2026a; Jin et al., 2026) execute only the final 𝐾 passes on the critical path to emit a 

tentatively accepted frontier and provisional KV states, while earlier tentative tokens complete true passes 1 through 𝑇 as piggybacked batch rows at zero extra serial calls (using spare per-call batch capacity in memory-bound decoding rather than reducing FLOPs). Because that wake finishes exact causal 𝑇 -pass states 

𝐿 = ⌈𝑇 /𝐾 ⌉ steps later, projecting them inside the same output GEMM ( Wake verification , Section 3.4) promotes matching tokens into a permanently verified 𝑇 -pass prefix and rolls back rare mismatches, unifying greedy-exact ( 𝜏 = 0), margin-gated (0 < 𝜏 < ∞), and lossy ( 𝜏 = ∞) decoding in one CUDA graph. 

Key Takeaway 

In looped language models, deep cached context substitutes for token-local early passes. Whereas existing looped speculative decoders (Ha and Kim, 2026; Cho et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026c) accelerate drafting via shallow initial passes while keeping full 𝑇 -pass verification on every accepted token, Late Entry + Wake Verification compresses critical-path verification itself to the final 𝐾 < 𝑇 passes over a pipelined full-depth cache and reuses completed 1 → 𝑇 wake states in the same output GEMM to preserve 𝑇 -pass greedy equivalence ( 𝜏 = 0) or trade controlled margin tolerance ( 𝜏 > 0) for higher throughput. Our study is organized around three concrete contributions: • Pass-allocation asymmetry and error localization. Across Ouro-1.4B, Ouro-2.6B ( 𝑇 = 4), and Huginn-3.5B ( 𝑇 = 32), adapted late entry consistently outperforms matched early exit; fp32 interventions trace this gain to pass-coherent context (𝐾, 𝑉 ) pairs and show that 𝐾 < 𝑇 discrepancies concentrate inside the 6% uncertain tail (Section 2).

## 2 Early Exit vs. Late Entry: Where Should Passes Be Spent? 

Before building an inference system, we isolate a central pass-allocation question under idealized context conditions: when each new token is limited to 𝐾 < 𝑇 serial passes, is it better to spend them on the initial passes or on the final passes? We study this question on Ouro-1.4B (24 Transformer layers) and Ouro-2.6B (48 Transformer layers), two pretrained looped language models that iterate a shared layer stack for 𝑇 = 4passes with pass-specific KV caches and untied input-output embeddings (Zhu et al., 2025). For clarity, Sections 2 and 3 develop our analysis and pipeline on 𝑇 = 4 Ouro before Section 4.4 scales both to 32-pass middle-recurrent Huginn-3.5B ( 𝑇 = 32). In free autoregressive generation, a single altered prediction changes every subsequent token, confounding single-step accuracy with trajectory drift. To measure per-token prediction quality cleanly, we first evaluate 

teacher-forced agreement (Appendix A). We generate reference completions greedily at full depth ( 𝑇 = 4), replay the exact same token prefixes under a reduced-pass configuration, and record the fraction of positions whose greedy next-token prediction matches full depth. 

2.1 Making Late Entry Feasible 

What happens if we apply late entry directly to an off-the-shelf looped language model without any adaptation? We test the most extreme case on released Ouro-1.4B and Ouro-2.6B: preceding context tokens retain their full four-pass KV caches, while the current token skips passes 1 to 3 and feeds its raw token embedding directly into pass 4 ( 𝐾 = 1). As shown in the first row of Table 1, prediction agreement with full depth drops to .552 on Ouro-1.4B and .751 on Ouro-2.6B, well below simply truncating the entire model to pass 1 ( .938 and .949). Inspecting internal activations reveals why raw late entry fails: pass 1 expects raw token embeddings (median ℓ2 norm 0 .76), whereas pass 4 expects layer-normalized hidden states emitted by pass 3 (norms 37 to 51). To test whether this failure reflects an input representation mismatch rather than missing early-pass computation, we fit a closed-form linear projection from raw token embeddings to pass-4 inputs on held-out states while keeping all model weights frozen. This linear probe alone raises agreement to .816 on Ouro-1.4B and .919 on Ouro-2.6B (Table 1), recovering 60% and 70% of the gap to the diagnostic oracle that supplies the true pass-3 state ( .991 and .992). Still, frozen input adapters on an unadapted model plateau at or below one-pass truncation ( ≤ .949; extended adapter diagnostics in Appendix C). 2.3 Late Entry vs. Early Exit Across Scales and Matched Fine-Tuning 

We first compare early-exit and late-entry pass placements on the exact same Stage-2 checkpoints across all reduced budgets 𝐾 ∈ { 1, 2, 3}, both model sizes, and both GSM8K and IFEval. Table 2 compares truncating to the initial 𝐾 passes ( First 𝐾 , depth-𝐾 context) against entering for the final 𝐾 passes ( Last 𝐾 , four-pass context). Across all 12 cells, spending passes at the end beats spending them at the beginning on identical weights, with margins of +6.4 to +14 .1 points at 𝐾 = 1. Note that each agreement score is measured against that checkpoint’s own full-depth output: although unadapted Ouro-2.6B showed .949 one-pass self-agreement in Table 1 versus .898 here, the unadapted base model achieves only .729 full-depth GSM8K accuracy ( .442 on IFEval; Appendix B), lower than even two-pass truncated decoding on our adapted model (.747 and .597). Because Table 2 evaluates checkpoints exposed to late-entry blocks during Stage 2, we next test whether giving early exit its own dedicated Stage-2 training closes the gap. Starting from the exact same Stage-1 checkpoint with identical data order, optimization steps, and random seed, we train a matched early-exit model whose reduced-budget blocks execute the first 𝐾 passes instead of the last 𝐾 (Appendix A). Dedicated early-exit training raises 𝐾 = 1 truncation agreement on Ouro-2.6B from .898 to .925 on GSM8K and from 

.757 to .798 on IFEval (while unexposed late entry stays at .755 and .619), yet it still trails trained late entry by wide margins ( .962 vs. .925 on GSM8K and .887 vs. .798 on IFEval), confirming across both identical weights and matched fine-tuning that cached context depth outweighs token-local early passes. 

## 3 Late-Entry Decoding 

While Section 2 assumed idealized full-depth context states, a practical decoder must construct those states for newly generated tokens without stalling generation. Late-entry decoding achieves this by maintaining

deep context with a short pipeline lag across decoding steps. For a concrete mental model at 𝑇 = 4 and 

𝐾 = 2 (previewed in Figure 5), each decoding step performs three coordinated actions: 1. Tentative late-pass verification: new candidate tokens skip passes 1 and 2 and execute only passes 3 and 4 ( 𝐾 = 2 serial calls) to emit a tentatively accepted token group. 2. Provisional KV exposure: newly accepted tokens temporarily populate slots 3 and 4 with provisional KV states computed from their embeddings so the next step can proceed immediately without stalling. 3. Pipelined cache completion and Wake verification: over the next two decoding steps, those tentative tokens ride along inside ongoing forward calls to compute true passes 1 → 2 and 3 → 4 at 

zero extra serial calls , overwriting provisional KV entries and verifying finished 𝑇 -pass states inside the same output GEMM to advance a permanently verified 𝑇 -pass prefix. We formalize looped pass mechanics in Section 3.1, describe within-step block verification in Section 3.2, detail cross-step cache completion in Section 3.3, and unify greedy-exact and lossy decoding via Wake verification in Section 3.4 (hardware Roofline and concurrency scaling follow in Section 4.7).

Here, asynchronous refers to delayed cache completion across decoding steps inside the same GPU forward calls, rather than a separate background CUDA stream. Because a looped language model shares identical layer weights F across all passes, in-flight tentative token groups and the active (1 + 𝐵 )2-row candidate block can execute different pass indices side by side as parallel batch rows inside the same 𝐾 forward calls, each attending to its own pass-aligned cache. Because tentative groups enter the wake queue in chronological order and advance 𝐾 passes per step, whenever a group recomputes pass 𝑘 , every earlier group has already written its exact pass-𝑘 KV states during a prior step. In Step 3 of Figure 5, for example, Group B computes passes 1 and 2 while attending to Group A’s true pass-1 and pass-2 caches completed in Step 2. Meanwhile, active candidate blocks briefly read provisional KV entries (P3 ∗, P4 ∗) for the one or two most recent groups until true pass-𝑘 states overwrite them. Why does appending these catch-up rows add minimal wall-clock latency? It is important to distinguish serial calls from arithmetic work: zero extra serial calls does not mean zero extra FLOPs, because tentatively accepted tokens execute 𝐾 verification passes plus 𝑇 wake passes. In single-sequence (batch-1) decoding, however, GPU latency is memory-bandwidth bound rather than compute bound: once layer weights F are streamed from memory for a forward call, extra token rows incur little marginal latency (on Ouro-2.6B on an NVIDIA H100 GPU, forwarding 32 parallel tokens takes only 9% longer than forwarding 1 token). At 

𝑇 = 4, 𝐵 = 8, and 𝐾 = 2, the active block has (1 + 8)2 = 81 rows while at most two groups of ≤ 9 tokens catch up simultaneously, adding at most 18 rows ( 18% of a 99-row batch ; all reported speeds include this work). Higher request concurrency gradually saturates compute under a fixed large 𝐵 unless 𝐵 is reduced alongside load (analyzed via our H100 Roofline law in Section 4.7 and Appendix I). Finally, cache-completion ablations in Appendix F verify both halves of this design: reading provisional KV states for one or two steps costs zero measurable accuracy compared with synchronous before-next-step repair ( 𝑝 = .28 to .77), whereas omitting cache completion altogether drops GSM8K accuracy at 𝐾 = 2 by 4.1 points (.839 → .798; Section 4.4 and Appendix H examine how multi-stage lag scales at 𝑇 = 32).

## 5 Related Work 

Looped and recursive language models iterate a weight-tied layer stack to scale latent reasoning depth at a compact parameter footprint (Dehghani et al., 2019; Bae et al., 2025a;b; Geiping et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025). In standard non-recurrent Transformers, SkipDecode (Del Corro et al., 2023) skips bottom layers in favor of upper layers under a monotonically decreasing per-step layer budget so KV caches never require recomputation, while in shared-weight recursive models Relaxed Recursive Transformers (Bae et al., 2025a) introduce cross-request depth-wise batching and Mixture-of-Recursions (Bae et al., 2025b) learns token-level routing across initial passes. Our analysis addresses a distinct regime: because a looped model shares weights across all passes, every token can enter uniformly at the final 𝐾 passes without decaying position 

20 Late Entry in Looped Language Models Preprint 

budgets, while within-sequence pipelined cache completion reconstructs exact 1 → 𝑇 context caches in the background wake. To accelerate autoregressive decoding, speculative and block-decoding methods verify multiple drafted to-kens in parallel using auxiliary drafters, n-gram suffix lookups, or single-pass mask placeholders (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Gloeckle et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2026a; Jin et al., 2026). For looped language models specifically, WaveFront (Ha and Kim, 2026), LoopSpec (Cho et al., 2026), DAS (Li et al., 2026), and LoopAhead (Liu et al., 2026c) accelerate drafting via shallow initial passes: DAS and LoopAhead verify candidates synchronously through a full 𝑇 -pass target call, whereas WaveFront and LoopSpec pipeline single-pass shallow guesses diagonally into deeper passes. Crucially, because those methods only speed up proposal generation while still routing every accepted token through shallow-to-deep passes on the verification path, full 𝑇 -pass verification bounds their speedup as recurrence deepens. Late entry paired with Wake verification is complementary and structurally distinct along three axes: (1) our optimistic frontier executes the final 𝐾 < 𝑇 passes over a deep context cache rather than initial passes over shallow caches, cutting mismatch and rollback rates by an order of magnitude (0 .25 to 2.2 rollbacks per 100 tokens at 𝑇 ≥ 16); (2) in-pass block verification filters (1 + 𝐵 )2 candidate branches at depth 𝐾 so only the ≤ 1 + 𝐵 committed tokens enter the 1 → 𝑇 wake, saving 𝑇 − 𝐾 passes on all rejected branches; and (3) a single static CUDA graph sweeps 𝜏 ∈ [ 0, ∞] from greedy-exact ( 𝜏 = 0) to margin-gated (𝜏 > 0) and lossy ( 𝜏 = ∞) decoding while factorizing orthogonally from the choice of candidate drafter. Finally, adaptive early-exit methods halt computation after initial layers or passes when confidence is high (Schuster et al., 2022), while loop-contrastive methods contrast early and late passes to sharpen reasoning accuracy (Yu et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026b). We show that halting early without cache completion starves a looped language model of coherent pass-aligned (𝐾, 𝑉 ) context depth, whereas optimistic late entry paired with Wake verification preserves full-depth context and exactness while advancing at 𝐾 < 𝑇 serial passes per step. 

## 6 Limitations and Conclusion 

Our study has three main limitations. First, as formalized by our Roofline law in Section 4.7, single-request speedups exploit HBM weight-streaming headroom on an H100 GPU ( 𝑀 𝑗 ≤ 99 ≪ 𝑀 ridge (1) ≈ 348 rows/pass). Under multi-request continuous batching on Ouro-2.6B (Figure 12b; Appendix I), speedup compression across 𝐶 = 1 → 32 stems primarily from (1 + 𝐵 )2 block expansion rather than late entry itself: full-depth 𝐵 = 8 block decoding drops from 3 .90 × to 0 .77 ×, whereas late entry’s incremental gain over same-𝐵 block decoding holds steady (1 .65 × → 1.45 × at 𝐵 = 8 and 1 .84 × → 1.48 × at 𝐵 = 4 for 

𝐾 = 2) because skipping 𝑇 − 𝐾 passes on rejected draft branches cuts total arithmetic work per step (198 vs. 324 row-passes at 𝐵 = 8). Guided by Equation (3) (8 .3% zero-shot MAPE, 11 /12 optimal 𝐵 ∗ (𝐶 ) matches), shrinking 𝐵 = 8 → 4 → 2 with load retains 2 .31 × decode (1 .67 × end-to-end including online prefills) at 

𝐶 = 32 for 𝐾 = 2 (1 .71 × and 1 .47 × for 𝐾 = 3). Second, while Wake verification unifies greedy-exact ( 𝜏 = 0), margin-gated ( 𝜏 > 0), and lossy ( 𝜏 = ∞) decoding inside one engine, Proposition 1 targets greedy decoding (extending Wake rollback to temperature sampling via speculative rejection sampling (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) is left to future work), and 𝜏 = 0 speedups depend on optimistic agreement: at shallow recurrence ( 𝑇 = 8, 𝐾 = 2), frequent rollbacks (12 .5 per 100 tokens) preserve full accuracy ( +23 .2points over 𝜏 = ∞) at the cost of falling below 𝐾 = 𝑇 block speed (1 .96 × vs. 2 .86 ×), whereas for 𝑇 ≥ 16 (0 .26 to 2 .2 rollbacks per 100 tokens) 𝜏 = 0 scales up to 8 .42 ×. Third, unlocking late entry on checkpoints not pretrained with pass-skipping requires a one-time Stage-2 SFT pass (7 ,395 steps, ≈ 1.9B tokens on one 8×H100 node, comparable to training lightweight speculative heads such as Medusa or LoopAhead while

In conclusion, saving passes from the two ends of a looped language model is fundamentally asymmetric: cached context depth substitutes for token-local early passes across Ouro-1.4B, Ouro-2.6B ( 𝑇 = 4), and middle-recurrent Huginn-3.5B ( 𝑇 ∈ { 8, 16 , 32 , 64 }). Pairing late-pass block verification with pipelined cache completion and zero-extra-pass Wake verification turns this asymmetry into a unified 𝜏 ∈ [ 0, ∞] family that accelerates both greedy-exact 𝑇 -pass decoding (up to 5 .2× at 𝑇 = 4 and 8 .4× at 𝑇 = 64) and controlled lossy decoding (up to 6 .8× and 12 .5×).
