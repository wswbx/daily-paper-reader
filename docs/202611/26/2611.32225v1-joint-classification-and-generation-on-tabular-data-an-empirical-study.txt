## ABSTRACT 

Many applications of machine learning on tabular data need both a classifier and a generator of realistic records. The default is to train two separate models, whereas a single joint model promises both from one set of weights. Several families of tabular deep learning models can be turned into such a joint model, which raises the question of which joint model is preferable at which computational budget. In this empirical study, we introduce and compare joint models based on four state-of-the-art tabular data modeling families (energy-based models, probabilistic circuits, tabular foundation models and tabular diffusion models) against measured FLOPs and wall-clock time, on 29 classification datasets. Methods are evaluated based on two defined aggregate scores that take into account different aspects of prediction and generation. We then also compare these joint models with every pair of a separate classifier and generator at different computational budgets, to see when a joint model may be a defendible choice against the regular practice of keeping a separate classifier and generator. We find that the best joint model is a probabilistic circuit at the smallest budgets and, beyond that, a tabular foundation model that generates column by column using a regressor (TabICL). Compared with pairs of separate models, this study finds a joint model is rarely preferable but often very close to the best pair, without the hassle of training and maintaining two separate models. 

Keywords Joint models · Tabular data · Energy-based models · Foundation models · Probabilistic circuits · Synthetic data · Diffusion models · resource efficiency 

## 1 Introduction 

In applications of machine learning on tabular data, it is not uncommon to require a generative model of realistic records in addition to a classifier. Such a generator can augment or rebalance small and incomplete training sets [Cui et al., 2024, Jäger et al., 2021], support out-of-distribution detection, selective prediction and calibration [Liu et al., 2020, Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017, Grathwohl et al., 2019], and produce realistic counterfactual explanations and feature attributions [Redelmeier et al., 2024, Aas et al., 2021]. The straightforward way to achieve this is to train two separate models: the best available classifier and the best available generator. However a single joint model p(x, y ) could in arXiv:2611.32225v1 [cs.LG] 24 Nov 2026 Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data theory offer both in a single training run and a single set of weights to maintain, which is an attractive proposal in resource-constrained settings. The past few years have brought a wave of powerful deep learning methods for tabular data, of which we show some can be turned into such a joint model with little or no modification. Tabular foundation models are transformers pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets that classify a new table in context and match or outperform tuned gradient-boosted trees on small and medium-sized tables [Hollmann et al., 2025, Qu et al., 2025, Kong et al., 2026]. Because any column in a mixed-type table, with numerical and categorical features, can be their prediction target, the chain rule can turn them into generators of whole rows, with the label drawn last from the classifier itself [Hollmann et al., 2025]. Tabular diffusion models are the state-of-the-art in tabular data generation [Zhang et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2025] and can define a classifier through Bayes’ rule, as diffusion classifiers do for images [Li et al., 2023]. Deep probabilistic circuits model the joint distribution of mixed-type data natively and have recently been shown to match these generators [Scassola et al., 2026]. Finally, energy-based models can now be trained on mixed-type data without the costly sampling loop of maximum likelihood [Schröder et al., 2023b, 2024], which makes Joint Energy-based Models (JEMs) [Grathwohl et al., 2019] applicable to tables. We observe that many of these modern tabular methods have been studied in isolation on either classification or generation, but no work exists that compares their capabilities as joint models on both tasks at the same time, and at what computational cost [van Breugel and van der Schaar, 2024]. These costs also sit in different places and differ by orders of magnitude: a foundation model is pretrained once but pays at inference with a forward pass over its entire context, a diffusion model is trained on each new dataset for thousands of epochs and needs multiple denoising passes for every class to make one prediction, and an energy-based model pays for sampling during both training and generation. Moreover, a joint model should not only be compared with other joint models. The natural alternative is to train two separate models, and a joint model is only worth using if it is at least as good as the best such pair at the same cost. We therefore ask: 

Which joint model of tabular data is preferable at which computational budget, and how does it compare with two separate models? 

In this empirical study, we aim to answer this question by comparing different deep tabular joint modeling methods on a set of predictive and generative metrics, at different compute budgets, on 29 classification datasets from UCI and OpenML-CC18. For every method we sweep the hyperparameter that primarily controls its compute budget and record predictive and generative performance against the amount of FLOPs measured to fit, perform inference and generate samples. Next to this we also evaluate the performance of state-of-the-art non-joint tabular classification and generation models, and compare the best joint model with the best pair of specialist models at each compute budget. We find that a probabilistic circuit is the best joint model at the smallest budgets and TabICL, a tabular foundation model, at larger budgets. The families differ in characteristic ways: the foundation models are the strongest classifiers but are limited by their context on large tables, the probabilistic circuit and the diffusion models are the best generators on large tables, and the energy-based models classify competitively but generate less realistic samples. Compared with two separate models, a joint model is rarely preferable. It beats every pair only on small tables at medium budgets, where TabICL’s own classifier is the best one affordable. Elsewhere a pair is at least as good, and the best pair often keeps the generator of a joint model next to a cheap separate classifier. Our contributions are: • A cost-aware comparison of joint models for tabular data To our knowledge, this is the first study that compares joint tabular classifiers and generators from four modern deep learning model families, and the first to ask whether a single joint model beats the best pair of separate models at a certain compute budget. We measure for every method the FLOPs used in fitting, prediction and sampling by counting every floating-point operation they execute. We use an aggregated score for prediction and generation of multiple metrics that are all scaled between a trivial floor and real data. • New joint tabular models from published components We define joint models of mixed-type tables that, to our knowledge, have not been evaluated before: JEMs [Grathwohl et al., 2019] whose marginal energy is trained with energy discrepancy and the mixed-type perturbation kernel of Schröder et al. [2024] (JEM-ED), or with SGLD and exact Gibbs updates of categorical features (JEM-SGLD). We also evaluate diffusion classifiers [Li et al., 2023], built on the tabular diffusion models TabDiff and TabSyn. Finally, we use column-by-column generators for the foundation models TabPFN and TabICL, following Hollmann et al. [2025] and the TabICL package [Qu et al., 2025]. We give the first quantitative evaluation of each as both a classifier and a generator. 2Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data

Samples are generated by integrating the learned reverse process from noise, typically with tens to hundreds of network evaluations. To be applicable to mixed-type tables, categorical variables need a discrete corruption process, such as multinomial diffusion [Hoogeboom et al., 2021] or masking. In that case ϵ and ϵθ in equation 9 cover only the numerical features: a categorical feature is resampled or masked instead of shifted by noise, and the network outputs a distribution over its categories, whose cross-entropy at the original category replaces the squared error for that feature. TabDDPM [Kotelnikov et al., 2023] combines Gaussian diffusion on numerical features with multinomial diffusion on categorical ones. TabSyn [Zhang et al., 2024] first maps each row to a continuous latent vector with a variational autoencoder over the numerical and categorical features and trains a score-based diffusion model [Karras et al., 2022] in that latent space. TabDiff [Shi et al., 2025] defines one continuous-time diffusion over the numerical and categorical features, Gaussian for numerical and masking for categorical ones, with learnable, feature-specific noise schedules. Both TabSyn and TabDiff are trained for thousands of epochs and are, at the time of writing, among the strongest generators of tabular data [Shi et al., 2025, Scassola et al., 2026]. 

Diffusion models as classifiers A diffusion model can also be used as a classifier [Li et al., 2023]. By Bayes’ rule, 

p(y | s) ∝ p(x | y) p(y) and thus the most probable class for an input x is the one that maximises p(x | y) p(y). A diffusion model does not give this likelihood exactly, but its denoising loss equation 9 is an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood [Kingma et al., 2021] and can be used in its place. Li et al. [2023] condition an image diffusion model 

pθ (x | y) on each class in turn, compute the denoising loss of the image for every class with the same noise draws, and predict the class with the lowest loss. Because their image datasets have balanced classes, they take p(y) to be uniform. 

2.3 Tabular foundation models 

Tabular foundation models classify a new dataset without being trained on it. Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) [Müller et al., 2022] are transformers that are pre-trained once on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a prior over data-generating processes. When faced with a new prediction task the training dataset D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 is passed to the network as context together with the test rows, and a single forward pass approximates the posterior predictive distribution p(y | x, D). This is also called in-context learning [Brown et al., 2020]: there is no training on D and 4Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data the cost moves to inference, where every prediction is a forward pass over the whole context, with attention costs that grow quadratically in the number of context rows. TabPFN [Hollmann et al., 2023] alternates attention over the rows and over the columns of a table and, in its second version, matches or outperforms tuned gradient-boosted trees on small and medium-sized tables [Hollmann et al., 2025]. TabICL [Qu et al., 2025] first embeds each row into a single vector, using attention within each column and then within each row, and only then lets the test rows attend to the labelled training rows. Because this in-context stage operates on one token per row rather than one per cell, TabICL scales to larger tables than TabPFN. TabFM [Kong et al., 2026] is a recent model of the same kind that embeds after a number of TabPFN-style alternating attention layers first, making it more expensive but more performant. All these foundation models are trained as either classifiers and regressors, depending on the type of y in the predictive distribution p(y | x, D).

Tabular foundation models as generators Because a numerical or categorical feature x can itself be treated as the prediction target conditioned on the other features, a regression and a classification model together can define every conditional of the table. Hollmann et al. [2025] show that this can be used to turn TabPFN into a generative model of whole rows by the chain rule,

2.4 Probabilistic circuits 

A probabilistic circuit (PC) [Choi et al., 2020] represents a distribution as a computational graph of sum units (weighted mixtures with non-negative weights), product units (factorisations) and input units (simple univariate distributions such as Gaussians or categoricals); sum-product networks are a well-known instance [Poon and Domingos, 2011]. If every sum unit mixes inputs with the same scope ( smoothness ) and every product unit multiplies inputs with disjoint scopes ( decomposability ), the circuit is normalised by construction, and any marginal can be computed exactly in a single forward pass by setting the input units of the marginalised variables to one. Exact sampling is ancestral, without MCMC. PCs are trained by maximum likelihood, with expectation-maximisation or gradient descent. Their structure can be learned from data, for instance from a Chow–Liu tree [Chow and Liu, 1968] over the variables, which gives the hidden Chow–Liu tree (HCLT) [Liu and Van den Broeck, 2021], and tensorised implementations scale them to many units per variable [Peharz et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2023, Loconte et al., 2025]. Scassola et al. [2026] recently showed that such a tree-structured PC (TabPC), trained by maximum likelihood, matches or outperforms the diffusion models of Section 2.2 on standard tabular generation benchmarks at a fraction of their cost. 

## 3 Included Methods 

This section lists every joint tabular modeling method considered in our study, how each one defines its classifier and generator, and how they were made applicable to mixed-type tables. We also list a number of reference baselines for both generation and classification. Table 1 at the end of the section summarises all candidates and serves as a reference for the rest of the paper. 

3.1 Joint models and choice of candidates Joint models We call a method joint if one fit to the training table yields both a classifier p(y | x) and a generator of rows (x, y ). A pair fits a classifier and a generator separately (Section 3.2). 

Four model families We take our candidates from the four families of Section 2. These families differ in how they provide both the classifier and generator. Probabilistic circuits and joint energy-based models model p(x, y ) itself. Tabular foundation models are classifiers and regressors, and become generators through the chain rule in equation 10. Tabular diffusion models are generators, and become classifiers through Bayes’ rule. For each family, Sections 3.1.1 to 3.1.4 describe the candidate methods for this study and what adaptations we apply to yield a joint model of mixed-type tables. 

Choice of candidates We do not attempt to cover every tabular classifier and generator. The study is centred on the recent deep-learning families of Section 2, and per family we chose candidate methods that are: (i) at the time of writing 5Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data among the strongest published representatives of their family on tabular data, (ii) have public code, and (iii) have a single hyperparameter that controls the bulk of their compute budget, such that we can evaluate compute-performance trade off of each method at different settings. The energy-based family has three candidates because they share one backbone and differ only in their training objective, which allows for a controlled comparison of EBM training objectives as a nice side result of this study (Section F). We exclude GAN- and VAE-based generators such as CTGAN [Xu et al., 2019], because these are consistently outperformed by the diffusion models we include [Zhang et al., 2024, Shi et al., 2025]. We also exclude generators based on large language models [Borisov et al., 2023], whose compute lies far outside the budgets that we deem the ’resource constrained setting’. 

3.1.1 Energy-based models 

We include three EBM-based methods that use a similar architecture: a feed-forward network fθ with a learned embedding per categorical column, so that it is defined on mixed-type inputs x = ( xnum , xcat ). Samples are drawn from these EBMs during training and for downstream generation using the same Langevin-within-Gibbs scheme: SGLD updates for the numerical features and exact Gibbs resampling of each categorical feature from its conditional at the cost of one forward pass per category . This combines the Langevin-within-Gibbs sampler of Kelly et al. [2021], where the discrete variable is the label, with the Langevin and Gibbs updates of the contrastive-divergence baseline of Schröder et al. [2024]. All MCMC chains are initialized from the same prior: independent standard normal numerical features and uniformly drawn category levels. 3.1.3 Tabular foundation models 

Tabular foundation models are in-context classifiers and regressors (Section 2.3), which we turn into generators using the chain rule from equation 10. For every column xj in turn, the rows being generated are passed as queries to the classifier (categorical columns) or the regressor (numerical columns), with the context restricted to the columns generated so far, and the column’s value is sampled from the predictive distribution q xj | x<j , D:,<j 

. The label y is generated last by the same classifier that is used for prediction, so generated labels follow the model’s own p(y | x, D). We construct the classifier and the regressor as two separately pre-trained networks instead of two output heads of one backbone, following Hollmann et al. [2025]. Note that we still count them as one joint model, since conditioning both networks on the same context table is a single fit that produces both a generator and a classifier. In terms of computational cost, fitting is free since the foundation model is pre-trained and assumed to be publicly available. Classification costs one in-context forward pass, and generation one forward pass per column, each batched over query rows (Section A). Categorical features are ordinal-encoded as inputs and drawn from the classifier’s distribution over their categories during generation in the chain rule setup from equation 10. 

TabPFN [Hollmann et al., 2025] The appendix of Hollmann et al. [2025] proposes the generative construction in equation 10 but provides no evaluation beyond an illustration. We implement it ourselves, with three adaptations: we use one column order instead of an average over orders, we implement the first column x1 in generation as a draw from its empirical distribution in the context instead of predicted from a dummy noise column, and we use quantile-normalised numerical features. In preliminary experiments our implementation was at least as good as the official implementation 1,which we observed to mishandle low-cardinality numerical columns. To our knowledge, this is the first quantitative evaluation of TabPFN as a generator. The budget hyperparameter is the size of the context. 

TabICL [Qu et al., 2025] We implement the chain-rule based generator for TabICL using the official code 2, which we use as is, with the label appended as the last column and the context capped at 2,000 rows. The classifier uses the full training split as context. The budget hyperparameter is the number of ensemble members, for both the generator and the classifier.

3.1.4 Probabilistic circuits 

A PC is a tractable model of p(x, y ) (see Section 2.4), so it is a joint model without any adaptation: the label is just another categorical variable of the circuit and the circuit is fitted by maximum likelihood on the joint table. Numerical features enter through Gaussian input units, and categorical features through categorical input units. Classification computes the exact conditional p(y=c | x) = p(x, c )/ P 

> c′

p(x, c ′), at the cost of one forward pass per class. Generation draws (x, y ) by exact ancestral sampling, which costs one categorical draw per variable and row and needs neither MCMC nor a diffusion process. We follow TabPC [Scassola et al., 2026]: a Chow-Liu tree is learned over all variables of the joint table, rooted at its centre and compiled into a smooth and decomposable circuit in our own JAX implementation, which is trained with TabPC’s optimisation recipe. We depart from TabPC in two implementation choices: (i) the we use the hidden Chow-Liu tree layout of Liu and Van den Broeck [2021] instead of TabPC’s CP layers, and (ii) we use the preprocessing that is shared by all candidates in this study (Section 4.1) instead of that in the original paper. The budget hyperparameter is the number of units per variable, KPC .

3.2 Single-task references and the two-model baseline Single-task references We add a number of sole classification and generation baselines to the study to add context to the computation-performance trade-offs of the joint models. We include a simple Multilayer Perceptron ( MLP ) as reference classifier, which is the JEM backbone trained with the cross-entropy term alone. We also include XGBoost 

[Chen and Guestrin, 2016] with native categorical splits and early stopping. Finally, we add TabFM [Kong et al., 2026]: an in-context classifier with ordinal-encoded categorical features as the state-of-the-art for tabular classification at the time of this project. For generation, we add two references that bound the generation score rather than compete for it, following the guidelines in Scassola et al. [2026]: a fully factorised sampler (FF) , which draws every column, the label included, independently from its training marginal, and real data , a sample of training rows of the size of the test split. FF reproduces every marginal but no dependency between columns, and real data is the best any generator can score on our metrics; together they provide the reasonable lower and upper bound for generative metrics. 

The two-model baseline. A joint model is only worth it if it is at least as good as the obvious alternative: a classifier and a generator fitted separately. We build this baseline by pairing every classifier with every generator in our study (except a method with itself, also at another budget). The computational cost of a pair is defined as the training and prediction cost of its classifier plus the training and sampling cost of its generator. So for example, we evaluate the combined cost and performance of an MLP for classification and TabDiff at a certain hyperparameter setting for generation. For more details see Section 4.1.

4.1 Experimental setup Benchmarks We use two benchmark suites (details in Section D). UCI-mixed contains five UCI classification datasets: 

Adult, Default, Diabetes and Online Shoppers have numerical and categorical features, and MAGIC is purely numerical. The OpenML-CC18 benchmark [Bischl et al., 2021] is used through 24 classification datasets with at most 2,000 rows, which is 6× less than the smallest dataset in UCI-mixed. We additionally split the suite into an 18-dataset purely numerical subset and a 6-dataset mixed-type subset (Section D). Note that UCI-mixed can be seen as the larger table benchmark, while CC18 is the small table benchmark in this study. Every method is run with five seeds; each seed re-draws a stratified 64/16/20 train/validation/test split. 

Preprocessing All methods share the same preprocessing of the data. Numerical features are mapped to an approxi-mately standard normal scale through a Gaussian quantile transformation fitted on the training split and categorical features are represented by integer codes. How each method then treats the categorical codes is described per candidate in Section 3. 

Hyperparameters Each candidate is swept over a grid of its ’budget hyperparameter’ (see Table 1). All other hyperparameters are fixed across datasets at the authors’ published or package defaults for the published methods and at one shared setting for the energy-based models. Table 2 reports each method at its best budget (Section 4.3). The budget grids, the default budgets, all other hyperparameter settings and implementation details can be found in Section E. 

Metrics Predictive performance is measured on the held-out test split with accuracy, one-vs-rest AUROC, the multiclass Brier score [Brier, 1950], the conditional negative log-likelihood (NLL) and the expected calibration error (ECE Guo et al., 2017). For generative performance metrics we follow Scassola et al. [2026]. They show that the metrics most commonly used to rank tabular generators, TREND (pairwise correlation similarity) and C2ST with a logistic-regression classifier, are near-saturated for state-of-the-art diffusion models and equally close to perfect for the FF baseline that samples every column independently from the marginal. SHAPE, the third common metric, only compares univariate marginals. As replacements they propose a weighted normalised mutual-information similarity (wNMIS), which measures pairwise dependence between generated variables of any type, and C2ST with an XGBoost classifier, which detects discrepancies in the full joint distribution. Therefore, we let each method generate a synthetic table of both x and y of the size of the test split and report wNMIS, C2ST-XGB and also the machine-learning efficacy 9Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data (MLE), which is the test AUROC of an XGBoost classifier trained on the synthetic table and evaluated on real data [Xu et al., 2019]. For a joint model MLE is interesting because it is the only one of the three metrics that measures whether the generated labels actually depend on the generated features. A sampler that produced perfect rows with random labels would score close to the ceiling on wNMIS and C2ST-XGB but at chance on MLE. We do not use SHAPE, TREND or C2ST-LR because of the objections raised by [Scassola et al., 2026]. 

Aggregate scores In order to decide whether one joint method is better than another on either prediction or generation, a single score per task is needed. We therefore formulate a single aggregate prediction and aggregate generation score. The metrics that evaluate either prediction or generation listed above cannot simply be averaged, since they differ in domain, scale and trivial upper and lower bounds. For example, a naive majority-class classifier reaches an accuracy of 0.73 on UCI-mixed and a generator with independent columns obtains a wNMIS of 0.80 but a C2ST-XGB of 0.02 

(Table 5), so that C2ST-XGB would decide an unnormalised average. Therefore, we rescale every metric between a trivial floor (score 0) and an ideal ceiling (score 1). For prediction, the floor is the class prior and the ceiling a perfect classifier; for generation, the floor is a fully factorised sampler and the ceiling a sample of real training rows (as suggested by Scassola et al. [2026]). This gives two aggregate scores: • Prediction score (Pred.): the mean of (acc − acc 0)/(1 − acc 0), (AUROC − 0.5) /0.5 and 1 − Brier /Brier 0,where subscript 0 denotes the class prior , which predicts the empirical class frequencies for every row; a perfect classifier (accuracy and AUROC 1, Brier score 0) scores 1 on each term. The Brier score covers calibration because unlike the NLL, it is bounded so no single over-confident dataset dominates and unlike ECE, it is a proper scoring rule. • Generation score (Gen.): the mean of (m − mFF )/(mreal − mFF ) over m ∈ { wNMIS , C2ST-XGB , MLE },where the floor ’FF’ is a fully factorised sampler that draws every column independently from its empirical marginal distribution [Scassola et al., 2026], and the ceiling is a sample of real training rows of the size of the test split. We compute both scores separately for every dataset and seed, using the floor and ceiling of that same split. We then average them over the five seeds and take the interquartile mean (IQM; Agarwal et al., 2021) of the resulting per-dataset scores over the datasets of a suite. Additionally we obtain 95% intervals using a bootstrap over datasets (Table 6) and, for every single model against a pair, the probability P that the pair has the higher overall score, defined below (Section 4.4). Note that the metrics for the references (class prior, FF sampler, real data) are given in Section B Our conclusions about which joint modeling method is perceived to perform better on either generation or prediction than another method is based on these two scores. We also add an overall score , the geometric mean of the prediction and generation scores, to rank joint modeling methods overall. 

Cost accounting We define computational cost of a method as the total compute needed to fit it on a new dataset, predict the test split labels and generate a synthetic table the size of the test split. We report this cost in FLOPs, which are independent of hardware, and in wall-clock time such that we also capture memory-bound and sequential work (e.g the difference between JEM-SGLD and JEM-ED). We count all floating-point arithmetic of fitting, prediction and sampling: matrix products are two FLOPs per multiply-accumulate and all other operations are one FLOP per element. We count them for every run with our own counter, which records every operation executed in JAX and PyTorch. XGBoost runs on CPU, so its cost comes from a model fitted to the floating-point instructions it executes. Section A gives the details. Wall-clock times are measured on one NVIDIA GH200 per run. Because we recognize the errors that come with counting flops, our claims rest only on cost differences of at least about 2×.

Ranking is their weak point: on UCI-mixed TabDiff has the lowest AUROC of all joint models ( 0.788 ), below even its accuracy ( 0.813 ), and TabSyn’s AUROC is also slightly below its accuracy, whereas for every other model the AUROC is above the accuracy. How performance reacts to longer training depends on the size of the tables. On CC18, training TabDiff longer improves both generation and prediction: from 500 to 8,000 epochs its prediction score rises from 0.15 to 0.45 and its generation score from 0.10 to 0.69 . On UCI-mixed however the performance on the tasks diverges: the generation score keeps rising up to 8,000 epochs (from 0.32 to 0.90 ), while the prediction score falls from the start (from 0.34 to 0.25 ). TabDiff’s best overall budget (2,000 epochs) is therefore neither its best classifier nor its best generator. TabSyn shows neither effect, because its early stopping ends the training of its latent diffusion model before about 2,500 epochs, so beyond its best budget of 1,250 epochs its scores change little and without a trend. So once the tables are large enough, a diffusion model is a joint model whose two halves want different amounts of training: the longer it fits the data distribution, the less its ELBO separates the classes, and a single budget cannot serve both tasks. Classification is also the expensive half of these models: reading out a class takes an ELBO per class, estimated from 250 diffusion-time draws per row, which costs six to fourteen times the FLOPs of generating the synthetic table.

4.6 Limitations of the evaluation 

We point out the current limitations of the empirical study. Scope: The empirical study is not complete in terms of methods that are compared. Many more methods from literature or different architectures for the methods present (e.g the specific type of PC used) could be added. However, we believe we chose a representative subset of methods from 4 different families of joint modeling methods that are close to state-of-the-art on generation or prediction. Moreover, some of the joint modeling methods we present here have not been proposed or studied before, which is why we regard this study a first step towards a more complete empirical evaluation of joint modeling methods instead of a complete benchmark. Aggregation: Scores are interquartile means over datasets with bootstrap intervals over datasets. With five datasets in UCI-mixed and six in CC18-mixed these intervals are wide, and several ’joint-vs-pair’ verdicts have bootstrap probabilities between 0.4 and 0.7, so differences of a few hundredths of a score should not be over-interpreted. Normalising per dataset is also unstable when the real data barely beats the floor on a metric (for instance wNMIS on tic-tac-toe, where real data and the fully factorised sampler differ by 0.006 ), which can push a dataset’s score far outside [0 , 1] . Scores: label dependence is measured only through MLE, which reflects whether a classifier trained on the synthetic data transfers to real data, not directly whether the generated labels follow the real conditional distribution. A class-conditional two-sample test would measure that more directly. Hyperparameters: no method was tuned per dataset (Section 4.1) but mostly uses default hyperparameters. This favours methods whose defaults were tuned on similar data. Tasks not covered: out-of-distribution detection and adversarial robustness, which motivate JEMs (see [Grathwohl et al., 2019]), are not part of this study and the joint models were only evaluated on classification datasets, not regression. Cost: The way we measure FLOPs is not exact due to computational constraints and because it is nontrivial to validate the FLOP counters in general and relatively between methods. FLOPs are only a proxy for compute (Section A). They miss memory-bound work, and they do not show the effect of parallelization. For example, every SGLD step in JEM-SGLD is counted, but these steps run sequentially. JEM-ED may use the same amount of counted flops on its negative particles, but run this compute in parallel, which can speed up execution. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We asked what the best and most efficient way is to obtain one model that both classifies and generates tabular data, and whether one model is ever worth it compared with two specialists. We compared joint models from four families: energy-based models, probabilistic circuits, tabular foundation models and tabular diffusion models. Some of these joint modeling methods were based on existing adjustments and techniques, though have never been introduced or evaluated before this work. We evaluated all joint modeling methods’ performance on 29 classification datasets along a budget hyperparameter that we chose, scoring prediction and generation per dataset between a trivial floor and real data. 

Which joint model at which budget On smaller training datasets from CC18, the smallest probabilistic circuit is the best joint model on lower FLOP budgets and from there on TabICL, a tabular foundation model that generates column by column with its own predictive distributions, is the best based on overall score. On the larger UCI-mixed tables, we see the same pattern. Looking at prediction and generation separately, TabICL is the best classifier among the joint models on both small and large tables, while XGBoost is the cheapest strong classifier overall. For generation 17 Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data the answer depends on the size of the training dataset: the foundation models are the best generators on small tables, whereas on large tables the probabilistic circuit is the most efficient generator and a diffusion model the best one when compute is plentiful. 

Joint models versus pairs A joint model is rarely preferable to two separate models. It beats every pair only on small tables at medium budgets, where TabICL is both the best generator and classifier. In all other cases a pair showed itself to be at least as good. The best pair often keeps the generator of a joint model and adds a cheap separate classifier such as XGBoost, which improves prediction at little extra cost. A single model remains preferable mainly where its other benefits matter: one set of weights and a classifier that is consistent with its generator. 

How the families differ The tabular foundation models are the strongest joint classifiers and the strongest generators on small training tables. On large tables their limited context holds them back as generators. The diffusion models are among the best generators on large tables but weak classifiers, and there their classifier and generator need different amounts of training. The probabilistic circuit is a cheap and strong generator, provided its capacity matches the size of the data. The energy-based models are competitive classifiers whose generative term seems to act as a regulariser, but are weak generators of numerical features in terms of especially C2ST-XGB. Within this family, energy discrepancy reaches the quality of SGLD training at up to two orders of magnitude fewer training FLOPs.

## A Cost accounting details 

We report the cost of every method in FLOPs and in wall-clock time. We count the FLOPs of every run with our own counter which we will describe below. 

A.1 Counting FLOPs 

We count all floating-point arithmetic that a run executes, in the forward and in the backward pass. Matrix products and attention count two FLOPs per multiply-accumulate. Every other floating-point operation counts one FLOP per output element: additions, multiplications, activations, exponentials and logarithms, normalisations and optimiser updates. Reductions, such as a sum over a dimension, count one FLOP per input element. We do not count data movement (copies, gathers, reshapes, concatenations and type casts), random-number generation, comparisons, integer arithmetic, or preprocessing outside the framework, such as the quantile transformation in scikit-learn. Every run has three phases, fitting, prediction and sampling, which we count separately. We note that elementwise operations make up less than 2% 

of the FLOPs of every method, except the small PCs, whose sum layers are surrounded by exponentials and logarithms (about 9% at 64 units). 

JAX The energy-based models, the MLP and the PC are written in JAX, and all their computation runs inside compiled ( jit ) functions. During a run, our counter wraps every compiled function. The first time a function is called with given input shapes, the counter traces it to JAX’s intermediate representation (the jaxpr). The jaxpr contains the forward and the backward pass exactly as they are handed to the compiler, and the counter counts every operation in it at its full shape. It then adds this count each time the function is called. Loops are handled at two levels. A Python loop that calls a compiled function, such as the training loop over updates or the sampling loop over steps, is counted through the number of calls. A loop inside a compiled function ( scan ) is counted as its body times its number of iterations, and a conditional as its more expensive branch. Vectorised code, such as an operation mapped over a batch with vmap ,appears in the jaxpr as one operation on the batched arrays, so it is counted at its full batched shape. Matrix products are the dot_general and convolution operations of the jaxpr, at two FLOPs per multiply-accumulate. Elementwise operations are counted from the jaxpr’s arithmetic primitives on floating-point arrays, at one FLOP per output element: addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, negation, absolute value, maximum, minimum, squares, reciprocals, powers, rounding, and the transcendental functions (exponential, logarithm, log1p , tanh , the logistic function, square roots, the error function and others). Reductions (sums, maxima, minima, products, cumulative sums and argmax )count one FLOP per input element, and scatter-adds one per added element. Comparisons, selections, sign functions, sorting and indexing are not counted. 

PyTorch The foundation models and the diffusion models run in PyTorch. Here the counter is a dispatch mode that sees every elementary (ATen) operation that actually executes, forward and backward, at its real shape. Because it records operations as they run, Python loops (over epochs, sampling steps or generated columns) are counted automatically, and a batched operation is counted with all of its elements: a batched matrix product counts the multiply-accumulates of every matrix in the batch. Fused kernels are split into their parts. Fused attention counts as its two matrix products, and a fused elementwise kernel, such as softmax or layer normalisation, counts as the elementary operations it consists of, so that both frameworks count the same arithmetic. Elementwise operations are recognised by PyTorch’s own tags. Every operation tagged as pointwise (additions, multiplications, activations such as ReLU, GELU and SiLU, exponentials, logarithms, square roots and the like) counts one FLOP per floating-point output element. Every operation tagged as a reduction (sums, means, maxima and the like) counts one FLOP per input element. Fused kernels count a fixed number of FLOPs per input element, equal to the number of elementary operations they consist of: for example 5 for softmax, 4 for logsumexp , 8 for layer normalisation (14 in its backward pass) and 12 per parameter for the fused 21 Joint Classification and Generation on Tabular Data Adam update. Operations on integer tensors are not counted. Every operation of the reported runs falls into one of these groups. None of the models uses custom GPU kernels or torch.compile , which the counter would not see. 

CPU XGBoost runs as compiled C++ code on the CPU, outside both frameworks. We counted the floating-point instructions it executes with the opcode-mix tool of DynamoRIO, which counts every instruction as it is executed, so loops are included. Additions, subtractions, multiplications, divisions, minima, maxima and square roots count once per element. A vectorised (SIMD) instruction counts once per lane, and a fused multiply-add counts twice. Comparisons are not counted. On a CPU there is no separate class of elementwise operations: every arithmetic instruction counts, whether it is part of a gradient computation, a histogram sum or a split gain. Functions such as the exponential and the logarithm in XGBoost’s loss are computed in software from these instructions, and are counted through them. We counted XGBoost 3.2.0 in this way on 37 synthetic tables ( 500 –8,000 rows, 1–40 features, 2–8 classes, 0–50% 

categorical features) with the benchmark’s settings. From these counts we fitted a cost model, which gives the cost of every XGBoost run. One fit costs

E.4 Tabular foundation models 

All foundation models are used with their publicly available pretrained weights. 

TabPFN We use the official implementation of TabPFN, the tabpfn package (version 2.0.3) 3, with its pretrained TabPFN v2 weights. The package provides a classifier ( TabPFNClassifier ) and a regressor (TabPFNRegressor ). These are two separate models, each pretrained for its own task with its own weights. The classifier half of the joint model uses only the classifier; the generator uses both. Both halves are conditioned on a context of training rows, drawn uniformly at random without replacement from the training split, separately for each seed. The context size is the budget hyperparameter (64 to 4,096 rows, Table 10). If the training split is smaller, the whole split is used, which is the case for all 24 CC18 datasets at 2,048 and 4,096 rows, for 18 of them at 1,024 rows and for 8 at 512 rows; the UCI training splits are larger than every budget. The points at 2,048 and 4,096 rows were run later, on one A100 (Section A). The classifier and the generator use the same context size, but draw their rows independently. The generator’s context contains the label as an additional column. The classifier is a single forward pass of the pretrained classifier with one ensemble member, on inputs standardised with the mean and standard deviation of the context. It bypasses the package’s own preprocessing and ensembling, and its class probabilities are the softmax of the logits. This differentiable forward pass is the one on which TabPFGen builds [Ma et al., 2024], and its logits also define the energy in the sampler ablation (Section C). It is cheaper than the package’s default prediction, which averages several ensemble members over its own preprocessing, and may be less accurate. The generator is TabPFN’s chain-rule construction (equation 10; Hollmann et al., 2025), in our implementation (Section 3.1.3). Unlike the classifier, it calls TabPFNClassifier and TabPFNRegressor through the package’s standard interface, with their own preprocessing and 8 ensemble members each. It generates as many rows as the test split has, one column at a time, in schema order with the label last: • The first column is drawn from its empirical distribution in the context. A column that is constant in the context is copied. • For every later column j, a model is fitted on the context, with columns 1, . . . , j − 1 as inputs and column j

as target: the regressor for numerical columns and the classifier for categorical columns, including the label. It then predicts column j for the rows generated so far, and one value per row is drawn from its predictive distribution at temperature 1. For the regressor, this is TabPFN’s discretised distribution over target values. • TabPFN v2 accepts at most 10 classes. Categorical columns with more levels are sampled hierarchically: first one of at most 10 balanced groups of levels, then a level within that group. 

TabICL The classifier is TabICLClassifier with the whole training split as its context and the number of ensem-ble members given by the budget hyperparameter. Categorical columns are passed as categorical columns of a data frame, so that TabICL applies its own categorical preprocessing. The generator is the package’s TabICLUnsupervised 

with the same number of ensemble members, fitted on a random subsample of at most 2,000 rows of the joint table (features and label). It generates the columns in schema order with the label last, at temperature 1. 

TabFM TabFM is a single-task classifier in this study [Kong et al., 2026]. It runs with the PyTorch backend in single precision (float32 instead of the package’s bfloat16 default), with the training split as its context and the number of ensemble members given by the budget hyperparameter. Its default of 32 members is the headline budget.

The draws lie on a shared grid with a half-step offset and are the same for every class. For TabDiff, which keeps the label column clean and therefore scores log p(x | y), the training class prior is added. For TabSyn the label is inside the VAE latent, so the bound is joint and no prior is added. 

E.6 Probabilistic circuit 

The PC follows TabPC’s HCLT recipe [Scassola et al., 2026]. Its Chow–Liu tree is learned on the training split from pairwise mutual information, with numerical columns discretised into 16 quantile bins for this estimate only, and is rooted at its centre. Numerical columns have Gaussian leaves, with the standard deviation floored at 0.05 ,and categorical columns, label included, have categorical leaves. Training uses RAdam with learning rate 0.1 and batch size 512 for at most 200 epochs, with early stopping on the validation log-likelihood (patience 10) and the learning rate multiplied by 0.85 when the validation log-likelihood plateaus. The classifier is the exact conditional 

p(y | x) = p(x, y )/p (x), and samples are drawn by ancestral sampling. 

E.7 XGBoost 

XGBoost uses its native categorical splits and the package defaults otherwise (histogram trees, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.3). The budget hyperparameter is the maximum number of boosting rounds, and training stops early when the validation log-loss has not improved for 20 rounds. 

E.8 Reference rows 

FF, the real-data row and the class prior (Section B) have no hyperparameters.
