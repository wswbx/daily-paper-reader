Keywords: RAG, User Simulation, Multi-Hop Questions, Evaluation Abstract: Quality assessment is essential for determining whether changes to a Retrieval-Augmented Generation system improve or maintain its performance. However, existing evaluation approaches often rely on single-turn or single-hop interactions and therefore do not capture the complexity of multi-hop, multi-turn conversations. We introduce MH-RAG-DIVE, a dynamic interactive user simulation framework designed to evaluate RAG systems in more complex multi-hop and multi-turn settings. The framework extends RAG-DIVE with a Context Discoverer, which identifies documents that can be combined to generate multi-hop questions within an ongoing conversation. To address the high computational cost of dynamic user simulation, we further introduce a Conversation Cacher, which reuses turns from previous evaluation runs while preserving the dynamic nature of the evaluation. We evaluate the framework across six distinct RAG configurations and compare its results with the original RAG-DIVE evaluation and the static, human-created CLAPNQ dataset. Our experiments show consistent performance trends across the evaluation approaches, while the more complex multi-hop and multi-turn setting exposes additional challenges for RAG systems. Furthermore, the Conversation Cacher substantially reduces token consumption, demonstrating its potential to make dynamic evaluation more cost-efficient. Finally, we apply MH-RAG-DIVE to an industrial RAG system with domain-specific data, demonstrating its applicability to real-world evaluation scenarios. Overall, our results show that the proposed approach provides a more complex and cost-efficient framework for evaluating RAG systems. All code and experimental data are publicly available in our GitHub repository (https://github.com/Daniel-Innsbruck/MH-RAG-DIVE).

# 1 Introduction 

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) was devel-oped to address the limitations of Large Language Models (LLMs) in answering questions that require knowledge beyond their training data (Lewis et al., 2021). The RAG pipeline, including document index-ing, retrieval, and the integration of external knowl-edge into the generation process, offers numerous op-portunities for customization (Kukreja et al., 2024; Lyu et al., 2025; Brehme et al., 2025b). Furthermore, these configu-rations require a standardized and reliable evaluation methodology to enable meaningful comparisons (Gao et al., 2024). To obtain meaningful evaluations of RAG sys-tems, the experimental setup should reproduce the characteristics of real-world information-seeking, both in the formulation of search queries and in human interactions with the system (Gao et al., 2024; Brehme et al., 2025a). These interactions are typically simulated using a question–answer (QA) dataset, which can either be manually created by hu-man experts or automatically generated by an LLM (Schimanski et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). In arXiv:2611.30335v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 practice, however, realistic information-seeking be-haviour is difficult to capture because current eval-uation methods often employ simplified settings, in-cluding single-turn interactions (Es et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025; Tang and Yang, 2024) and questions that require information from only a single document (Chen et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Brehme et al., 2026a). In contrast, real-world applications often re-quire multi-hop reasoning, where answers must be synthesized from information distributed across mul-tiple documents rather than retrieved from a single source (Lee et al., 2025; Tang and Yang, 2024). Fur-thermore, users frequently engage in multi-turn con-versations, in which the context established through previous interactions influences subsequent queries and responses (Brehme et al., 2026a; Zheng et al., 2023). Existing approaches, such as MultiHop-RAG (Tang and Yang, 2024), which automatically gener-ates multi-hop QA datasets, and RAG-DIVE (Brehme et al., 2026a), which dynamically evaluates RAG sys-tems in a multi-turn setting, already address these requirements individually. However, they do not combine these capabilities. Specifically, existing ap-proaches lack a framework for automatically evaluat-ing RAG systems in a multi-hop, multi-turn setting. To bridge this gap, we introduce MH-RAG-DIVE, which builds upon the RAG-DIVE framework and extends it by incorporating multi-hop queries. This is achieved through the introduction of the Con-text Discoverer, which identifies connected document chunks that are subsequently used to generate multi-hop questions. Additionally, we optimize the com-putational cost of dynamic interaction generation by introducing a caching algorithm that reduces unnec-essary regeneration of questions and only creates new interactions when required. 

# 2 Related Work 

A comprehensive evaluation of a RAG system relies on an appropriate dataset that provides the informa-tion against which the system is evaluated (Brehme et al., 2026a). Early approaches to RAG evalua-tion predominantly used single-hop QA datasets, in-cluding SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016). Single-hop queries can be answered using information retrieved from a single document or passage, requiring only one retrieval and reasoning step. However, as RAG systems are increasingly de-ployed in knowledge-intensive domains, their evalu-ation must also account for multi-hop queries (Tang and Yang, 2024). In contrast to single-hop queries, multi-hop queries require the model to retrieve and in-tegrate information from multiple documents or pas-sages before generating the correct answer. Individual retrieved contexts may appear irrelevant when con-sidered in isolation, yet become essential when com-bined to support the required reasoning process (Park et al., 2025). Consequently, evaluating RAG systems on multi-hop datasets provides a more realistic assess-ment of their retrieval capabilities and reasoning per-formance in complex, real-world applications (Tang and Yang, 2024; Brehme et al., 2026b). Multi-hop RAG evaluation has traditionally been supported by specialised benchmarks, including Hot-potQA and MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2018). HotpotQA established a benchmark setting in which answering a question requires the integra-tion of evidence from multiple supporting documents, while distractor documents are included to assess the robustness of the retrieval process. MuSiQue builds upon this concept by composing multi-hop questions from simpler single-hop questions, thereby providing greater control over the structure of the underlying reasoning chains. Although such benchmarks have substantially contributed to the evaluation of multi-hop reasoning, their static nature introduces several challenges for contemporary RAG evaluation. In par-ticular, publicly available datasets may be susceptible to data contamination resulting from their inclusion in pre-training corpora, potentially enabling LLMs to answer questions based on memorised knowledge rather than retrieved evidence (Singh et al., 2024). Moreover, fixed datasets provide limited opportuni-ties to adapt evaluation scenarios to particular applica-tion domains or changing system requirements (Gan et al., 2025). To address these limitations, recent re-search has explored more flexible dataset construction methods, including domain-specific and synthetically generated evaluation data (Gan et al., 2025; Saad-Falcon et al., 2024; Tang and Yang, 2024). Neverthe-less, these methods typically generate evaluation in-stances independently of one another and therefore do not capture the evolving state of a conversational in-teraction, in which earlier retrieval outcomes and gen-erated responses can affect subsequent dialogue states (Brehme et al., 2026a). Despite the availability of numerous metrics for evaluating RAG systems, multi-hop capabilities are still frequently measured through system performance on multi-hop question answering benchmarks (Tang and Yang, 2024; Brehme and Jatowt, 2026). Such evaluations typically focus on the quality of the final answer using general answer-level metrics, leaving the intermediate reasoning and retrieval steps largely unassessed. Recent work has therefore begun to ad-dress the evaluation of these intermediate processes. For example, (Nguyen et al., 2024) introduce a frame-work that directly evaluates Chain-of-Thought rea-soning in multi-hop question answering and show that a correct final answer does not necessarily imply a valid underlying reasoning process. From a comple-mentary retrieval perspective, (Brehme et al., 2026b) examine the assessment of retrieved information in multi-hop scenarios. They highlight that conventional retrieval metrics evaluate passages individually, even though the relevance of retrieved information may de-pend on the combination of multiple passages. To account for this, they propose a context-aware evalu-ation approach that assesses the retrieved context as a whole and reports higher agreement with human judgements than passage-level evaluation methods. Whereas multi-hop evaluation has largely exam-ined complex reasoning within individual queries, practical RAG applications commonly operate through ongoing conversational exchanges. This has motivated a shift towards multi-turn evaluation, in which the system is required not only to produce accurate responses but also to incorporate infor-mation from preceding interactions and maintain conversational context over time (Brehme et al., 2026a). Recent conversational benchmarks conse-quently include a range of interaction patterns, such as context-dependent follow-up questions, requests for clarification, and user corrections (Sirdeshmukh et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the majority of existing multi-turn datasets remain static in their design (Cheng et al., 2024; Katsis et al., 2025). Their prede-fined conversational trajectories prevent subsequent queries from being influenced by the system’s actual responses or preceding errors. As a result, such benchmarks provide only limited insight into the adaptive behaviour that RAG systems need to exhibit in realistic conversational settings (Brehme et al., 2026a). RAG-DIVE addresses the shortcomings of static evaluation by replacing predefined test scenarios with dynamic LLM-to-LLM interactions (Brehme et al., 2026a). Its use of adaptive user personas enables a more realistic assessment of multi-turn RAG be-haviour. However, the framework is designed around interactions with a single, isolated document (Brehme et al., 2026a). Consequently, it does not support the systematic evaluation of multi-hop reasoning across multiple documents within an evolving conversation. This limitation leaves room for evaluation frame-works that combine dynamic conversational interac-tions with the need to bridge information across mul-tiple documents. Although RAG-DIVE’s LLM-driven dialogue generation allows conversational RAG systems to be evaluated under flexible and realistic interaction sce-narios, generating these simulated dialogues intro-duces additional LLM inference overhead and con-sequently increases computational and financial costs (Brehme et al., 2026a). Cost efficiency is therefore an important factor in making LLM-based evaluation approaches scalable. One strategy for addressing the computational demands of repeated LLM inference is inference caching, which stores previously generated outputs for subsequent reuse (Liu et al., 2026). Con-ventional caching mechanisms generally require an exact match between a new request and a previously cached request. Semantic caching relaxes this re-quirement by identifying requests with similar mean-ings and reusing the corresponding previously gener-ated outputs (Liu et al., 2026). 

# 3 MH-RAG-DIVE 

To evaluate a RAG system in a multi-hop, multi-turn setting, we developed MH-RAG-DIVE. The frame-work extends the previously proposed RAG-DIVE approach (Brehme et al., 2026a). MH-RAG-DIVE extends this architecture with two additional components and is composed of five core components (cf. Figure 1): (1) the Context Discoverer (CD), which identifies multiple relevant context chunks used to construct multi-hop questions requiring the integration of information from mul-tiple sources; (2) the Conversation Generator (CG), which generates synthetic conversations; (3) the Con-versation Validator (CV), which validates the gener-ated conversations to ensure their quality and consis-tency; (4) the Conversation Cacher (CC), which re-duces computational costs by caching previously gen-erated conversations and regenerating only affected conversation turns instead of recreating entire conver-sations from scratch; and (5) the Conversation Eval-uator (CE), which evaluates the responses generated by the RAG system.

This database is used to discover relevant context chunks for multi-hop question generation and to sup-port context expansion throughout the course of a conversation.

The overlap between consecutive chunks helps maintain semantic continuity across chunk boundaries. Each resulting chunk di is sup-plemented with descriptive metadata, such as docu-ment headers and creation dates, thereby preserving relevant contextual information from its source docu-ment. In addition, structural metadata is attached to the chunks to facilitate both multi-hop context selec-tion and context management across multiple conver-sational turns. Specifically, each chunk stores the identifier of its parent document, P(di), which enables cross-document filtering for true multi-hop context sam-pling. Furthermore, each chunk is assigned its se-quential position within the parent document, Idx (di),together with the total number of chunks in that docu-ment, N(di). These structural attributes enable deter-ministic context expansion throughout a conversation while preserving the original document order. Finally, all chunks are embedded and stored in 

Veval , ensuring that the preprocessing pipeline re-mains independent of the downstream retrieval and generation architecture. 

Two-Stage Multi-Hop Context Discovery To es-tablish the multi-hop context for the initial query, a two-stage context discovery process is performed. In the first stage, an arbitrary starting chunk, dA, is sampled from Veval , and a candidate pool is con-structed by retrieving its top-k nearest neighbours. This retrieval is performed using a filtered semantic k-nearest neighbours search that enforces the constraint 

P(d j)̸ = P(dA), ensuring that all candidate chunks originate from different source documents and there-fore enable cross-document reasoning. The second stage selects a second chunk, dB, with the objective of completing the initial multi-hop con-text. To identify a suitable candidate, chunks are sampled from the retrieved neighbourhood and as-sessed by an LLM for their thematic relationship to 

dA. For each candidate, the LLM produces a poten-tially empty set of bridging topics, Tbridge , as well as a binary confidence score indicating whether the iden-tified semantic relationship is adequate for generat-ing a multi-hop question. Candidates receiving a con-fidence score of 0 are rejected, after which another candidate is sampled. This process continues until a suitable pair is found or all k neighbours have been considered. If none of the candidates satisfies the re-quired criteria, dA is discarded and the discovery pro-cess is restarted using a newly sampled initial chunk. Once a valid pair has been identified, the resulting source set, S1 : = {dA, dB}, together with the corre-sponding bridging topics, Tbridge , is forwarded to the CG to generate the initial multi-hop query 1 . 

Deterministic Context Expansion During the con-versation, the CD incrementally expands the active context by adding adjacent chunks from the same source documents. This expansion is controlled by two optional trigger parameters, α and β (1 < α, β ≤

n, where n is the total number of dialogue turns), which specify the turns at which the contexts of dA

and dB are expanded, respectively. Consequently, the context can be expanded either simultaneously (α = β) or at different stages of the conversation. Be-fore these trigger turns, the active context remains un-changed ( St = S1). Context expansion follows a configurable traver-sal direction, dir ∈ { above , below }, which determines whether the preceding or succeeding chunk in the parent document is retrieved. If the selected direc-tion reaches a document boundary, the traversal direc-tion is automatically reversed. The retrieved adjacent chunk is then added to the active context, i.e., St =

St−1 ∪ { dad j }, allowing the CG to produce follow-up questions based on an expanded context while pre-serving the multi-hop nature of the dialogue. 

## 3.2 Conversation Generator 

The CG is responsible for generating the conversa-tion. As in the original RAG-DIVE approach, it per-forms the same core tasks described by RAG-DIVE (Brehme et al., 2026a). To process the new multi-source input provided by the CD, the CG is prompted, following (Tang and Yang, 2024), to formulate a question that requires integrating information from both context chunks based on the established seman-tic links. The CG’s output schema is extended to include a final Bridging Topic , which is synthesised from the candidate set Tbridge and the generated Query Type . For this purpose, we used three types defined by (Tang and Yang, 2024): inference, which refers to cases where information from one document must be retrieved and used as an intermediate step before the final search query can be formulated to identify the document containing the information required for the final answer; comparison, where two entities are compared; and temporal, where two items are re-lated temporally. In the first turn, the question is re-quired to involve multi-hop reasoning. In subsequent turns, multi-hop reasoning is optional. Therefore, only multi-hop questions in the first turn are classified as multi-hop questions, as subsequent turns do not introduce new documents but only extend the exist-ing chunks. Consequently, we cannot reliably deter-mine whether these follow-up questions genuinely re-quire multi-hop reasoning. The CG produces a com-plete output bundle, denoted by G1, containing the 

RAG input , standalone question , query type , ground-truth answer , binary Multi-Hop Flag , and synthesised 

bridging topic . This bundle is then passed from the CG to the CV for subsequent processing 2 . 

## 3.3 Conversation Validator 

The CV validates the output of the CG. As in the orig-inal RAG-DIVE framework, it follows the validation procedure proposed by RAG-DIVE 3 4 (Brehme et al., 2026a). In addition to the baseline pass/fail cri-teria, the CV verifies two further aspects for the ini-tial turn: Query Type Alignment , which determines whether the generated question accurately reflects the declared query type, and Multi-Hop Integrity , which ensures that both context chunks are required to an-swer the question. Queries that can be answered using information from a single chunk are rejected. 

## 3.4 Conversation Cacher 

To avoid regenerating every conversation turn, we in-troduce the CC. This component caches each gener-ated turn and determines whether regeneration is re-quired. The cache is organized as a tree structure. Initially, the source set S1 and the CG’s initial output bundle 

G1 are stored as the root node. In subsequent runs, the framework retrieves these cached elements to in-stantly initialize the dialogue and establish a shared starting point. Since RAG systems can produce vari-able responses, the cache tree grows dynamically by creating new branches for divergent RAG outputs. The CV and semantic similarity are used to navigate the cache and determine whether existing conversa-tion paths can be reused or whether new branches must be generated. At each turn t, a node corresponds to a specific di-alogue state and maintains three primary components: the target system response yt , the cached output bun-dle Gt+1, and references to its child nodes. For termi-nal states, the latter is an empty set. The hyperparam-eters controlling the dialogue progression, α, β, and 

dir , are stored together with the conversation nodes to maintain a consistent tree structure across different runs. Persisting these parameters ensures that the dy-namic context expansion remains compatible with the cached dialogue trajectories. In contrast, the active source set St+1 is not stored as part of the cached state. Since context expansion is deterministically governed by the predefined triggers and remains synchronised with the turn index t.Once the target RAG system delivers its current response yt 5a , the CC computes the semantic simi-larity of yt to all cached response keys ˜ Yt at the current node. The conversation continues from the cached re-sponse with the highest semantic similarity 6a . The cached bundle ˜Gt+1 is then validated by the CV against the current conversation history Ht 3 . If the CV accepts the cached bundle, the corresponding turn is reused 4a . If no suitable cached branch is avail-able, the current turn is regenerated 4b , and the re-sulting sequence is added as a new branch to the cur-rent node. The conversation is subsequently gener-ated and cached through the final turn n, allowing the dialogue tree to expand incrementally as new system interactions are encountered. 

## 3.5 Conversation Evaluator 

The CE evaluates the RAG system’s responses us-ing the same evaluation criteria as the original RAG-DIVE framework 5b 6b (Brehme et al., 2026a). Specifically, it employs the four single-turn met-rics—context recall, context precision, faithfulness, and correctness—as well as the two multi-turn met-rics, forgetfulness and context retention. To provide a more fine-grained analysis, MH-RAG-DIVE ex-tends this evaluation by filtering the single-turn met-rics according to the Multi-Hop Flag , thereby report-ing performance separately for single-hop and multi-hop queries. This produces both an overall evaluation score and a detailed overview of the RAG system’s performance across different retrieval and reasoning settings. 

# 4 Experimental Setup 

To validate MH-RAG-DIVE, we evaluate six differ-ent RAG setups on a common knowledge dataset, CLAPNQ (Rosenthal et al., 2024), and compare their performance against both the static CLAPNQ dataset and the original RAG-DIVE results. Additionally, we evaluate MH-RAG-DIVE in an industrial setting us-ing a RAG system developed by Kufgem GmbH, a software company providing IT solutions for munici-palities. 

MH-RAG-DIVE For the knowledge base, we used the Wikipedia articles provided in the CLAPNQ dataset to construct Veval . We chunked the Wikipedia articles using a RecursiveCharacter-TextSplitter (LangChain, 2025) with a chunk size of 3500 characters and an overlap of 525 characters, and used gemini-embedding-001 as the embedding model (Google, 2026). This configuration served as the base 

Veval for all experiments. To run MH-RAG-DIVE, we used different LLMs for the individual compo-nents of the pipeline. For the CD, we used gemini-3.1-flash-lite (Google Cloud, 2026a). For the CG, we used gemini-3.1-pro-preview for the first turn (Google Cloud, 2026b), and gemini-3.1-flash-lite in all subse-quent turns (Google Cloud, 2026a). For the CV, we used gpt-5.6-luna (OpenAI, 2026), while gpt-5-nano 

was used as for the CE (OpenAI, 2025). 

RAG Setups To run MH-RAG-DIVE, we config-ured six different RAG setups. We defined one base RAG system and varied a single parameter for each of the other configurations to investigate its effect on the evaluation results. For the Vtarget , we used the CLAPNQ dataset and chunked the documents with a chunk size of 500 char-acters and no overlap. This chunking configuration was kept fixed across all RAG setups. For the embedding model, we used three dif-ferent configurations. The base setup used gemini-embedding-001 (Google, 2026). For comparison, we additionally used BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al., 2023) and sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (Reimers and Gurevych, 2020). We also varied the number of retrieved chunks. The base configuration retrieved five chunks per query, while additional configurations retrieved either one or eight chunks. Finally, we included an Agentic RAG setup that allows the system to perform multiple retrieval steps instead of relying on a single retrieval step, as in the base configuration. Across all setups, we used gemma-4-31b-it as the underlying LLM (Google DeepMind, 2026). The RAG systems were implemented using LangChain (LangChain, 2025). We conducted the experiments using 100 conver-sations with five turns each, resulting in a total of 500 QA pairs. Additionally, we performed a consistency check by running MH-RAG-DIVE ten times on the base RAG setup to assess the consistency and repro-ducibility of the results. 

Human Validation To assess the quality of the gen-erated questions, we sampled 100 turns and manually evaluated each turn and its corresponding conversa-tion using the criteria proposed by (Lee et al., 2024). The evaluation was conducted independently by two individuals. At the turn level, we assessed correct-ness, answerability, and plausibility, while at the dia-logue level, we evaluated diversity and coherence. To additionally assess whether the multi-hop questions correctly capture the intended multi-hop structure, we introduced a new metric, multi-hop integrity. This metric measures whether all retrieved chunks are nec-essary to answer the question, thereby verifying that the question genuinely requires the intended multi-hop reasoning process. 

Industrial Use Case To assess whether our ap-proach generalizes to a domain-specific setting, we evaluated MH-RAG-DIVE on the RAG system of Kufgem GmbH, which is based on their k5 |Next plat-form (Kufgem GmbH, 2026). The k5 |Next platform provides digital administration solutions for munici-palities. For this evaluation, we used the platform’s documentation as the document base and constructed 

Veval using the same parameters as in our other exper-iments. We applied MH-RAG-DIVE to their RAG sys-tem and evaluated its performance using the synthetic dataset provided by them. We then compared the re-sults obtained with MH-RAG-DIVE against the per-formance measured using the existing synthetic eval-uation dataset and the RAG-DIVE baseline. To facilitate reproducibility, all code and exper-imental data are publicly available in our GitHub repository 1.

# 5 Validation of MH-RAG-DIVE 

In this section, we evaluate the performance of MH-RAG-DIVE by comparing it with two baseline ap-proaches, validating the results through human evalu-ation, and demonstrating its applicability in an indus-trial setting. 

## 5.1 Question Quality 

First, we validated the quality of the generated ques-tions. The human evaluation showed that the ques-tion quality exceeded 90% across all evaluated met-rics, except for plausibility, where one reviewer rated only 89% of the turns as plausible. 93% of the ques-tions were assessed as correct, meaning that both the ground-truth answer and the standalone question were generated correctly. Furthermore, 93% of the ques-tions were answerable using the provided chunks.

The multi-hop integrity metric showed that 95% of the multi-hop questions in the first turn were gen-uine multi-hop questions that required combining in-formation from both chunks to derive the answer, thereby providing the intended challenge for the RAG system. At the conversation level, 90% of the conver-sations were assessed as coherent and diverse. Over-all, these results demonstrate the high quality of both the generated questions and the resulting conversa-tions. 

## 5.2 Conversation Cacher 

To validate the usefulness of the CC, we evaluated the acceptance rate of the CV for an existing turn. This acceptance rate is used as the cache hit rate. In the first turn, we observed a hit rate of 100%, since the initial turn is reused for every new conversation, followed by 71.4% in the second turn, 50% in the third turn, 35% in the fourth turn, and 23.7% in the fifth turn. These results demonstrate how frequently a generated turn can be reused. To evaluate the impact on token consumption, we compared the token usage of MH-RAG-DIVE with and without caching and also compared it with the original RAG-DIVE framework. Table 1 shows that the CC reduces the number of CG tokens by 45%. At the same time, it increases the number of CV to-kens by approximately 11%, resulting in an overall reduction of around 28% in token consumption. Ad-ditionally, RAG-DIVE uses more than twice as many tokens, with a total of 233,330 tokens, indicating that MH-RAG-DIVE is less computationally expensive in terms of token usage. Both approaches require the same number of to-kens for the CE, which amounts to 480,000 tokens.

To assess the sensitivity of our approach, we evaluated MH-RAG-DIVE across six different se-tups and compared its performance with the original RAG-DIVE and the human-generated questions from CLAPNQ (cf. Figure 2). For the single-turn evaluation, we considered three question-generation settings. Overall, the RAG system achieved the best performance on the CLAPNQ questions across all metrics, except for Context Recall in the one-chunk setup, where all ap-proaches achieved comparable results. Comparing MH-RAG-DIVE with RAG-DIVE, the latter gener-ally achieved higher Faithfulness and Context Preci-sion. The two approaches performed comparably in Faithfulness for the Agentic and eight-chunk setups. For Correctness, both approaches achieved similar re-sults in the Base, BGE-small, and Agentic setups, while RAG-DIVE performed better in the remaining setups. Context Recall remained comparable between MH-RAG-DIVE and RAG-DIVE across all evaluated setups. Looking at the performance trends, all three ap-proaches exhibit broadly similar patterns across the evaluated setups. Correctness is highest for the Agen-tic setup, followed by the eight-chunk configura-tion, while the one-chunk configuration consistently achieves the lowest correctness. For MH-RAG-DIVE and CLAPNQ, the one-chunk setup is followed by the All-MiniLM and then the BGE-small configurations. In contrast, for RAG-DIVE, BGE-small achieved bet-ter performance than All-MiniLM. Faithfulness follows a similar trend. For Context Precision, however, the two embedding-based config-urations generally perform worse than the one-chunk setup. For both CLAPNQ and RAG-DIVE, increas-ing the context to eight chunks does not improve Con-text Precision compared to the one-chunk configu-ration. In contrast, MH-RAG-DIVE benefits from the larger context, achieving higher Context Precision with eight chunks than with one chunk. The trends for Context Recall largely resemble those observed for Correctness, with one notable difference: the eight-chunk configuration achieves higher Context Recall than the Agentic setup. Overall, MH-RAG-DIVE exhibits larger perfor-mance differences across the evaluated setups than RAG-DIVE. For example, Correctness in the one-chunk setup is approximately 15 percentage points lower for MH-RAG-DIVE than for RAG-DIVE, whereas both approaches achieve comparable perfor-mance with eight chunks. In contrast, the CLAPNQ results remain relatively consistent across setups, with little variation in most metrics, except for Context Re-call, which shows more pronounced differences. The multi-turn metrics are available only for MH-RAG-DIVE and RAG-DIVE, as CLAPNQ consists exclusively of single-turn interactions. For Con-text Retention, RAG-DIVE achieves equal or better performance than MH-RAG-DIVE across all evalu-ated setups. In contrast, MH-RAG-DIVE performs equally well or better than RAG-DIVE on Forget-fulness. The overall performance trends are simi-lar across the two approaches, with the main differ-ences again observed between the two embedding-based configurations, consistent with the single-turn results. 

5.4.1 MultiHop Questions 

In addition to the overall scores, MH-RAG-DIVE provides a separate score for multi-hop questions by labeling the initial turn as a multi-hop question. This enables a more fine-grained analysis of RAG perfor-mance specifically on multi-hop questions (cf. Fig-ure 2). The results indicate that the RAG systems generally struggle more with multi-hop questions, as reflected by their lower scores. This effect is par-ticularly pronounced for the one-chunk configuration and the setup using the BGE-small embedding model. For the remaining setups, the differences are gener-ally smaller, with multi-hop questions yielding mostly lower or comparable performance. The only notable exception is Context Precision, where the multi-hop configurations achieve higher scores in the Agentic and eight-chunk setups.

# 6 Discussion 

Our experiments demonstrate the applicability of our proposed evaluation approach in both an artificial setup based on Wikipedia data and a domain-specific setting using technical documentation. In both set-tings, it provides a more challenging evaluation of RAG systems, reflected in lower scores compared to the RAG-DIVE baseline and simple single-turn QA pairs. This suggests that the multi-hop formulation can expose weaknesses that may remain hidden when evaluating RAG systems using simpler, single-turn questions. Despite the increased difficulty, the consistency checks show that MH-RAG-DIVE produces stable re-sults across repeated runs, indicating that it can pro-vide a reliable evaluation. This consistency is sup-ported by the caching and validation mechanisms of the CV. Furthermore, comparisons across the three evaluated approaches demonstrate consistent perfor-mance patterns across different experimental setups. In general, RAG systems struggle more with MH-RAG-DIVE because the multi-hop questions require the retrieval of multiple relevant pieces of informa-tion. This effect is particularly visible in the one-and eight-chunk configurations. In the eight-chunk setup, however, the performance difference compared to the RAG-DIVE evaluation is less pronounced, as the multi-hop questions require several chunks to be retrieved to obtain the correct answer. In contrast, the other evaluation settings contain no multi-hop questions, meaning that a single correctly retrieved chunk can often be sufficient to answer the ques-tion. Consequently, the difference between the one-and eight-chunk configurations is more pronounced for MH-RAG-DIVE. Overall, RAG-DIVE exhibits smaller performance differences between configura-tions than MH-RAG-DIVE. We attribute this to the lower difficulty of the questions used by RAG-DIVE: simpler questions can often be answered successfully even with less effective retrieval configurations. In contrast, the more challenging multi-hop questions in MH-RAG-DIVE amplify differences between re-trieval setups, making the strengths and weaknesses of each configuration more apparent. Additionally, the eight-chunk configuration achieves higher Context Recall than the Agentic setup. With eight chunks, a larger number of poten-tially relevant chunks is retrieved deterministically, whereas the Agentic setup relies on the agent to determine how many chunks to retrieve. If the agent retrieves fewer chunks, relevant information may be missed, resulting in lower recall. An observation concerns the choice of embedding model. While all-MiniLM performed worst among the evaluated embedding configurations for MH-RAG-DIVE and CLAPNQ, the BGE-small model achieved better performance for RAG-DIVE. This difference in performance trends warrants further in-vestigation to understand why the effectiveness of the embedding models varies across the evaluation ap-proaches. In addition to improving question quality and in-creasing the difficulty of the evaluation, we introduce the CC, which reduces token usage by reusing pre-viously generated questions. This reduction in to-ken consumption can lower computational and energy costs. Our experiments demonstrate that the CC suc-cessfully reuses previously generated questions and quantify the resulting savings compared to the orig-inal approach. Despite the reduction in token usage achieved by the Conversation Cacher, the majority of the tokens are still consumed by the CE, which cur-rently relies on the RAGAS framework. Future work could therefore investigate ways to reduce the com-putational and token costs of this component. Addi-tionally, evaluating the CE using self-hosted models could further reduce reliance on external APIs and as-sociated costs, while also providing insights into the performance and feasibility of locally deployed eval-uation models. One potential limitation of our approach is re-triever bias arising from the architecture of the CD, which retrieves chunks based on semantic similar-ity. If the same retriever is used by the evaluated RAG system, this could favor systems that employ similar retrieval mechanisms and potentially lead to artificially similar performance. To investigate this possibility, we evaluated three different RAG config-urations using different embedding models and ex-amined whether they exhibited similar performance patterns. The observed trends were similar to those of the human-generated CLAPNQ questions, whereas RAG-DIVE showed different trends, with BGE-small performing worst rather than All-MiniLM. These dif-ferences suggest that further investigation is required to determine whether retriever bias affects the evalu-ation. Therefore, the potential influence of retriever bias should be considered when interpreting the re-sults and determining which RAG configuration per-forms best. Future work will investigate this effect in greater detail. A second potential limitation is LLM circularity, as an LLM is used to validate questions generated by an LLM. This setup could introduce biases if the eval-uator favors outputs produced by models with similar characteristics. To mitigate this risk, we used different LLM architectures for question generation and valida-tion and additionally conducted a human evaluation to verify the quality of the generated conversations. Nevertheless, users of the approach should remain aware of the potential limitations of LLM-as-a-judge evaluation and avoid overfitting RAG systems to the evaluation model. Additional evaluation is therefore required before drawing definitive conclusions about the best-performing RAG configuration. In practice, this could include human-in-the-loop evaluation or feedback from real users. 

# 7 Conclusion 

This paper introduces an extension of the RAG-DIVE evaluation approach by incorporating the CD to gen-erate multi-hop questions, enabling a more complex and comprehensive evaluation. Additionally, it in-troduces the CC to reduce token usage and, conse-quently, the cost of the evaluation process. Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach compared with the RAG-DIVE baseline and show that the CC can substantially reduce token consumption. Overall, this extension provides a more complex and cost-efficient evaluation and demonstrates its applicability in an industrial use case involving domain-specific data.
