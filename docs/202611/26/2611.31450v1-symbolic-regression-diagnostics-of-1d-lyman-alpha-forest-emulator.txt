Abstract We use symbolic regression to diagnose the parameter dependence of a black-box multi-fidelity emulator of the Lyman-α forest 1D flux power spectrum. Symbolic regression is appealing in its ability to reduce complex parameter spaces, thereby reducing compute time and offering potential interpretability in parameter inter-dependencies. Starting from a pretrained Gaussian-process (GP) emulator that connects low- and high-resolution PRIYA simulations, we search for explicit equations describing the dependence of the power spectrum on resolution and each cosmological and astrophys-ical parameter. We find that symbolic regression can recover equations that reproduce the emulator values well, but accurate predictions do not guarantee accurate parameter derivatives, making such equations unreliable for downstream applications such as Fisher forecasts. We therefore introduce a derivative-faithfulness diagnostic that directly compares the parameter gradient of each symbolic equation to the GP, and explore a derivative-matching (Sobolev) loss to improve this agreement. At 

z = 3 .6, the Sobolev objective yields derivative-faithful equations for 9 of the 11 PRIYA parameters constraining the Lyman-α forest, with the approach only ineffective on h and ϵAGN , whose 1D power spectrum imprints are weak or degenerate. Redefining the training objective with the Sobolev loss is a more robust and computationally efficient approach to gradient-matching repair than increasing model complexity. Our methods present potential mitigation strategies for failure modes of PySR, enabling downstream Fisher forecasts in some of these cases. Further, we document limitations potentially relevant to future work bridging symbolic regression with emulators. With this, our results demon-strate symbolic regression as an emulator diagnostic tool, with particular utility for low-dimensional or weakly coupled parameter dependencies. 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Cosmological surveys have successfully enabled preci-sion measurements of fundamental physics using large-scale structure (Sailer et al. 2021). The future of cos-mology, such as the further understanding of dark en-ergy and dark matter, relies on understanding non-linear structure formation at small scales, some examples of which, specifically in Lyman-α structure formation, are Rogers and Peiris (2021), and Ma et al. (2025). One of the classic small-scale probes of cosmological struc-

ture (Croft et al. 1998) is the Lyman-α absorption in quasars tracing neutral hydrogen distributed around the Universe (Hernquist et al. 1996). The Lyman-α forest traces low-density gas, probing complementary scales of gravitational collapse to those probed in galaxy surveys. It is also a unique window into structure formation at redshifts z = 2 − 5, reaching scales as small as ∼ 1 Mpc. However, as both of those advantages highlight, the utility of the Lyman-α forest stems from being able to model this with a cosmological simulation, such as in Bird et al. (2019). For example, resolving gas on any scales smaller than 10 Mpc needs N-body hydrodynamic arXiv:2611.31450v1 [astro-ph.CO] 23 Nov 2026 2simulations as these scales are not accurately modeled by cosmological perturbation theory. To fully leverage the Lyman-α forest, one needs a suite of expensive simula-tions that span a wide range of cosmological and thermal parameters. Additionally, the Lyman-α forest is sensi-tive to smaller scales which require a higher resolution of simulations at higher redshifts (Bolton et al. (2014)). Emulators are tools that make uniformly accurate esti-mates over a broad range of parameters by the use of simulations (Bird et al. 2019). As a result, multi-fidelity emulation is particularly useful for Lyman-α studies. Numerous Lyman-α simulation efforts aim to address the need of a wide parameter range and often address this problem with using emulator-based approaches. The Lyssa suite (Walther et al. 2025) has 18 high-resolution simulations, and has a high resolution at mean density. This is a single fidelity emulator, taking a different ap-proach to modeling larger parameter sets. Several neural network based emulators utilize Bayesian inference for their models or in the downstream applications of the model. For example, Jin et al. (2024) uses multilayer perceptrons to build a neural network model of the re-lationship between a variety of thermal parameters to constrain the cosmic reionization. Cabayol-Garcia et al. 

(2023) uses a mixture density network, a type of NN that can model complex probability distributions, to model the P1D as a function of the slope and amplitude of the linear matter power spectrum rather than as a holis-tic function of cosmological parameters. Both Palanque-Delabrouille et al. (2013, 2015) approximate changes in the power spectrum using a Taylor expansion about the best-fit values of the cosmological parameters. In comparison to the emulators previously described, the PRIYA (Bird et al. 2023) emulator covers parameter space with an overall higher sample density across model parameter space. PRIYA is unique in providing large-volume Lyman-alpha forest simulations with full galaxy formation physics and a realistic population of damped Lyman-alpha absorbers. PRIYA also has already been applied to analyze eBOSS and KODIAQ-SQUAD data (Fernandez et al. 2024; Ho et al. 2025). The main advantage of a multi-fidelity emulator is to decrease computational cost by incorporating sim-ulations with varying resolutions. Many lower resolu-tion simulations across a wide model parameter space are combined with fewer high resolution simulations that sample a smaller subset of model parameters (Ho et al. 

(2022)). With multi-fidelity methods, one can probe a wider parameter volume of the Lyman-α forest with-out having to perform a substantial amount of large simulations which are computationally expensive. For example, a single high-resolution PRIYA simulation re-quires ∼ 90 000 node-hours on a supercomputer, whereas a low-resolution simulation requires ∼ 7 000 node-hours (Bird et al. (2023)). The current best cosmological multi-fidelity emulator (Yang et al. (2026)) can reduce this cost by up to 94%. Overall, multi-fidelity methods are highly effective at exploiting the resolution-volume trade-off in existing simulation suites, where different particle reso-lution and box sizes are used to assess numerical conver-gence (Ho et al. 2023). However, there is a broader limitation of emulator-based inference: once constraints are derived from a given emulator, these constraints are often tied to that particular black-box model. This coupling makes it dif-ficult to debug and understand what has actually been learned and also limits portability. If another group uses a different emulator, it is not straightforward to test an additional parameter direction unless that degree of free-dom is already built into their own simulation suite. One possible way to ease this limitation is to use sym-bolic regression to extract approximate analytical re-sponse relations from emulator outputs. These expres-sions would not replace the full emulator, but could pro-vide interpretable parameter-sensitivity templates that are easier to transfer across different emulator frame-works. Kammerer et al. (2025) shows how parameter level equations can allow for a more complete interpre-tation of an emulator through the individual relations. Symbolic regression is a method of filling the data space with modeling expressions and simultaneously minimizing prediction error and model complexity (Cranmer (2023)). Symbolic regression has been applied to modeling the hypergeometric functions for the Λ CDM 

comoving distance and linear growth factor (Bartlett and Pandey (2026)). Bartlett and Pandey (2026) also showed that one could integrate symbolic methods into a Dark Energy Survey like analysis while maintaining the accu-racy of the numerical methods such as cosmoSIS (Zuntz 

et al. 2015), jax-cosmo (Campagne et al. 2023), and 

CosmoPower-jax (Piras and Spurio Mancini 2023). Sim-ilarly, Bartlett et al. (2024) shows how a symbolic re-gression can be applied to the linear matter power spec-trum with sub 1% accuracy. These analytic fits were able to achieve similar accuracy to black-box emulator methods in less time. In this work, we utilize PySR 1, a specific symbolic regression package for Python Cranmer (2023). PySR has been used in numerous applications across astrophysics, such as to model galaxy-halo con-nections (Delgado et al. (2022)) or to develop paramet-ric relationships for black holes (Davis and Jin (2023)). In our application, we apply symbolic regression to the multi-fidelity emulator of the PRIYA simulation suite. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the training data and our symbolic regression methodol-ogy. Section 3 presents the recovered equations and the tests we put them through. Section 4 reports how accu-rate they are, and how faithful their parameter deriva-tives are. Section 5 covers the limitations of the method and its computational cost, and we conclude in Section 6. Three appendices give the production PySR configura-tion (Appendix A), the full per-parameter equation table (Appendix B), and the derivative-faithfulness approach (Appendix C).

2.2. Symbolic Regression 

Our main tool for describing the relationship between the parameters and P1D with respect to the fidelity was PySR. PySR is a Python package that uses Symbolic Re-gression on data sets with a number of binary and unary operations. These operations are customizable by the user in that we are able to choose which operations the model tries to fit to the data. This choice matters when the discovered equations are used for downstream cosmo-logical inference. Operations that produce numerical in-stabilities or divergent gradients (such as unbounded di-vision or unrestricted exponentials) can make the result-ing equations unreliable for downstream analysis (e.g., Fisher forecast), even when they fit the training data well. In our application to the PRIYA suite, we apply the model separately to every parameter within the data set. Treating each parameter this way allows us to stress-test how reliable PySR is at extracting equations from the emulator before we trust it for a multi-dimensional fit. In addition, PySR works best on low-dimensional prob-lems as discussed in Cranmer (2023). Conversely, we used both high- and low-fidelity parameter values simul-taneously in the model, but included a resolution variable 

r in the fitting taking the value r = 0 .4 for the LF sim-ulations and r = 0 .8 for the HF ones.. This allows us to use both fidelities simultaneously and provides a clearer interpretation for the multi-fidelity emulator. In what follows, we describe how the per-parameter equations are combined into a multi-parameter predic-tion. 

2.2.1. Normalization 

For each per-parameter PySR fit we vary a single pa-rameter θi across its prior while the others stay at the fiducial point θfid , a reference point with each param-eter set to a central value within its prior range; the specific values used in this work are in our companion code repository 4 and include τ0 = 1 .09, nP = 0 .983, 

AP = 1 .46 × 10 −9, h = 0 .688, and the corresponding central values for the IGM thermal parameters. The θfid 

is chosen to match Fernandez et al. (2024). We fit the log-arithm of the flux power and normalize it per k-bin over that parameter’s sweep. > 2.2.3. Hyperparameter Choices

Our PySR hyperparameters were tuned empirically. Two of these are the number of iterations and the com-plexity limit we set in PySR. The number of iterations, 

niterations , sets how many search iterations the algo-rithm runs at a fixed complexity limit. The complex-ity limit, maxsize , is the largest complexity a candidate may reach, counted as the number of nodes in its ex-pression tree, and limits the size of expressions PySR can search. Increasing niterations means PySR runs more search iterations without changing the complexity limit, while increasing maxsize allows PySR to consider larger expressions. The full production configuration is in Appendix A. We use more iterations but a lower complex-ity limit than the PySR defaults: production runs 

niterations = 200 and maxsize = 20, against PySR defaults of 100 and 30. We deliberately choose a lower complexity limit, as each fit spans only a single 1D in-put parameter. Section 4.2 tests how far our conclusions depend on it by refitting at maxsize ∈ { 20 , 30 , 35 , 40 }.Section 5.2 summarizes the computational cost. The following terminology helps clarify how we select equations in our symbolic regression pipeline: 

Pareto front : A PySR run returns a sequence of equa-tions with different complexities, rather than a single best equation. Simpler equations generally have higher loss, while more complex equations can give better fits. PySR keeps an equation at a given complexity only if it has a lower loss than all simpler equations, so some com-plexity levels may be skipped. The resulting sequence is called the Pareto front . For example, the τ0 fit of Sec-tion 3.1 contains 18 candidates, ranging from a one-node equation with training loss 31 .7 to a twenty-node equa-tion with loss 0 .0024, with complexities 4 and 12 absent because no better equation was found there. Choosing an equation therefore means choosing how much additional complexity we are willing to trade for better accuracy. The rest of this subsection describes how we make that choice. 

Selection rules : We also override PySR’s own choice of equation from the Pareto front. The first rule is that a fit is accepted only if its Pareto front contains at least one valid equation: one that uses the parameter input, has no constant with an absolute value greater than 100, and has a nonzero gradient with respect to the input parame-ter. This is deliberate because sometimes the lowest-loss equation does not use the parameter at all .If no valid equation is found, we re-run PySR with a new random seed. From an accepted front, we then select either the lowest-loss equation or the knee equation. The knee is the simplest valid equation whose loss is within 10% of the minimum loss among valid equations. The accuracy and prediction results use the lowest-loss equation, while we use the knee equation to report the equations in the tables and to classify each fit as faithful 

or unfaithful . We call a fit faithful if its gradient loss is below the threshold of 0 .25, and unfaithful otherwise. Appendix A states both rules and compares the knee against PySR’s own default selection rule. In this work, we have one validity filter, two ways of selecting an equation from the Pareto front, and one GP fallback rule. They are defined as follows: 

• Validity filter : An equation is considered valid if 6it uses the parameter input, contains no constant with an absolute value greater than 100, and has a nonzero gradient that can be evaluated using the finite differences in a Fisher forecast. We apply this filter when selecting the knee equation, but not when selecting the lowest-loss equation. For ex-ample, the lowest-loss αq equation in Table 2 con-tains constants 1601 .11 and −1598 .32, and there-fore does not pass this filter. 

• The lowest-loss equation : The equation with the lowest training loss on an accepted Pareto front, which is the equation stored by the PySR refit. It does not necessarily pass the validity filter. 

• The knee equation : Among the valid candidates, we select the simplest equation whose training loss is within 10% of the minimum training loss among valid candidates. We use this equation to report the symbolic expressions in the tables and evalu-ate its gradient loss to determine whether the fit is faithful or unfaithful. 

• GP fallback rule : For the multi-parameter additive model, we also require the selected equation to pass the gradient-loss threshold of Eq. 9. We test valid equations from lowest to highest loss and use the first one that passes. If none passes, we use the original GP emulator prediction for that parameter instead. The lowest-loss and knee selections give different equa-tions for eight of the eleven parameters at z = 3 .6. How-ever, under the Sobolev loss, both selections give the same classification of faithful and unfaithful fits. Nine parameters pass the gradient-loss threshold: AP , αq , nS ,

τ0, dτ 0, Ω M h2, zHei , zHef , and zHi . The remaining two, 

h and ϵAGN , are unfaithful under both selections. We run PySR in multithreaded mode using four par-allel CPU cores to reduce the runtime. As a result, the genetic search is not fully deterministically reproducible between runs, even with the same random seed (see Sec-tion 5.2).  

> 2.2.4. Operator Choices

The choice of operators in the PySR search space di-rectly affects whether the discovered equations can be used for the Fisher forecast, which requires well-defined derivatives with respect to the model parameters. We use {+, −, ×, aq } as binary operators, where aq( x, y ) = 

x/ p1 + y2 is the analytic quotient, and {exp , log , x 2}

as unary operators. We list the full production config-uration in Appendix A. Our earlier experimental runs included raw division x/y , sin, cos, √x, x−1, and expo-nentiation xy , all of which we have since dropped. We explain why we excluded these operators below, as this may also be useful for other PySR applications. Trigonometric terms can introduce rapidly oscillating derivatives that make the resulting equations unusable for Fisher forecasts. In runs that included them, the dis-covered equations’ parameter slopes ∂ log PF /∂θ differed from those of the GP by more than an order of magnitude (E∂ ≫ 1), because the fitted gradients were dominated by oscillations rather than the smooth parameter depen-dence of the original GP emulator. The exponentiation operator xy allows the exponent to depend on the input variables. In our earlier runs, such expressions could fit the training data well but showed large deviations near the boundaries of the pa-rameter prior (A good fit locally around the fiducial point does not necessarily mean the equation remains accurate across the full prior range). We dropped √x and x−1 for related reasons. The derivative of √x is 1 /(2 √x), which diverges as x → 0+; the derivative of 1 /x is −1/x 2, which diverges as x → 0. Raw division x/y can also introduce a singularity when the denominator approaches zero. Instead of removing division entirely, we replace it with the analytic quotient aq( x, y ) = x/ p1 + y2. This operator behaves approxi-mately as x/ |y| when |y| ≫ 1, but its denominator never reaches zero for real inputs. This allows PySR to retain a division-like operation without introducing a pole. The exp operator does not introduce a singularity at finite inputs. Its derivative is exp( x), which remains bounded over a finite range of x. Similarly, the squaring operator x2 has a derivative 2 x, which is also bounded over a finite range.  

> 2.2.5. Loss Function

PySR’s default mean-squared-error (MSE) loss mea-sures how well the predicted values match the training data, but does not directly constrain the derivatives with respect to the parameter θ. When most of the variance in the training data comes from ( k, resolution), the genetic search can find equations that fit the dominant varia-tion well without using the parameter input θ at all. We saw this happen for 6 of the 11 per-parameter fits at the smaller niterations = 20. To address this, we re-run PySR with a new random seed if none of the equations on the Pareto front passes the validity filter described in Section 2.2.3. This helps recover equations that depend on the input parameter. However, including the parameter in an equation does not guarantee that its derivative is accurate. For param-eters where re-running PySR is not sufficient, we instead add a derivative-matching (Sobolev) term to the training loss (Eq. 10, described in Section 3.4), which penalizes the mismatch between the equation’s parameter gradi-ents and the training emulator’s (GP’s) gradients, in ad-dition to the mismatch in predicted values. With this Sobolev loss, nine of the eleven parameters have faithful equations (passing 0.25 gradient loss threshold), while only h and ϵAGN remain above the gradient-loss thresh-old of 0 .25 (Section 3.4, Table 3).

3.4. Derivative loss 

We compare the symbolic emulator to the Gaussian-process (GP) emulator not only on the value of the power spectrum but also on its slope , the parameter deriva-tive ∂P F /∂θ that a downstream emulator analysis, e.g., Fisher forecast, would consume. The reason is that an equation can reproduce PF to a percent and still bias that derivative if its slope is wrong (similar to “Fisher’s Mi-rage” mentioned in Wilson and Bean (2025)). The resolution here with the Sobolev term is a bit ad-hoc as we are scoring the equa-tions against the gradient error. We briefly summarize the accuracy of the per-parameter one-dimensional equations and their multi-parameter combination. We first discuss how well PySR can recover the 1D pa-rameter variations. In general, at LF, the mean prediction errors are roughly below 1%. At HF, the worst prediction errors could reach 10% for the most challenging parameters. The best-performing parameter dimensions at HF are the mean-flux slope dτ 0 and the mean-flux amplitude τ0.This makes sense because the dependence on τ0 is almost linear in each redshift bin. The large variation in P1D also makes it easier for the PySR to find a good fit. In contrast, the worst performing parameters are nP ,

h, and zHi . In Bird et al. (2023), the PRIYA simula-tions show that the 1D flux power spectrum is not very sensitive to h and zHi . h represents the change of km/s 

to M pc/h conversion, while largely affected by the cos-mic variance of the simulation box at low k, and thus the effect is not smooth. zHi is the redshift of hydrogen reionization, which is higher redshift ( zHi > 5) than the nominal redshift of the P1D ( z = 2 .2 − 4.6) of either eBOSS or KODIAQ-SQUAD, and thus the P1D is not very sensitive to this parameter. nP is the slope of the primordial power spectrum, which is the primary cos-mological parameter that P1D is constraining. The fact that it’s hard to use PySR to find a good fit for nP is suggesting this nP dimension is more complex than the 10 other parameters. It is also possible the nP dimension is influenced by PySR struggling with fitting slope changes at both fidelities and therefore causing an effect at low and high k values each. This makes sense because P1D is as a function of k scale, so intrinsically the low-k end has more cosmic variance and high-k end has more resolu-tion effects, while a good nP fit requires a good fit across the entire k range. We will also mention the difficulty of making reasonable gradients for nP in Section 4.2. We briefly discuss the multi-parameter combination from extrapolation of the additive-Taylor method using our 1D PySR equations. We use the method in Al-gorithm 1 to combine the 1D equations into a multi-parameter prediction, and predict against the unseen Sobol sequence of 256 points per combination. The goal is to show how much information we capture or lose when we only vary in per-dimension manner. Fig-ure 2 (with Figure 3 for three varied parameters) sum-marizes the additive-Taylor combination method com-parison against the GP over 256 Sobol draws per combi-nation. We specifically tested against the combinations of cosmology, mean flux, and IGM thermal parameters. Most of the tested combinations are below 1% mean er-ror, which is unsurprising because the Taylor-additive method is expected to perform fine around the fiducial parameter point and the mean error is dominated by the Sobol points that are close to the fiducial point. Derivative error 

Value accuracy, such as the one shown in Figure 4, alone does not fully validate that the PySR equations can give reasonable parameter constraints. For example, 

ϵAGN has the fourth-smallest high-fidelity mean error in Table 2 at 0 .38%, but its slope/gradient error under the Sobolev objective is 0 .771, well above the 0 .25 threshold for a derivative-faithful equation. Figure 5 summarizes the per-parameter faithfulness verification across five PySR randomness seeds. A gradi-ent error threshold, 0 .25, is designed to visually indicate whether a candidate equation is faithful enough to be-have as a good approximation of the target emulator. Interestingly, relying solely on value loss (red dots) can-not reproduce the same level of faithfulness at the gra-dient level, which implies at these dimensions, although the emulator predictions are good but the gradients are not well captured enough to be used for downstream in-ference analysis. One obvious plausibility is that even though one can have a good MSE value loss, it might just happen by chance that the value variation is too small to be captured by the PySR equation. Their gra-dients might be arbitrarily larger or smaller, making any downstream inference analysis biased. Figure 5 shows that the Sobolev loss (green squares) is able to recover the gradient error threshold for most parameters, includ-ing nP , by including the gradient into the loss function when searching for the symbolic equations. To study how the search budget affects PySR equa-tions’ faithfulness towards gradient errors, we show the PySR’s loss versus model complexity in Figure 6. In Fig-ure 6, one panel per parameter, every candidate equation placed by complexity and value error and coloured by its gradient error (Eq. 9), with a bold black ring on equa-tions that clear the 0 .25 threshold. 2025). Similarly, h has a minimal effect on the P1D for the range considered (Bird et al. 2023). Increasing the complexity budget recovers most of the missing gradient directions. The recovery is not complete, and the largest fits are also more difficult to search reliably. We therefore view the joint fit as a useful demonstration that symbolic regression can begin to recover coupled parameter dependence, but that doing so faithfully requires substantially more complexity than the one-dimensional fits. For the present analysis, the additive-Taylor construction remains the more controlled approach. 

5.2. Computational cost and debugging 

Each PySR fit performs a genetic search over equation space and is intrinsically more expensive than a simple polynomial fit. A production fit runs at niterations = 200, populations = 48 and maxsize = 20 in multi-threaded mode on four cores with 8 GB of memory. Un-der the Sobolev loss (gradient-matching) a fit takes a median of 13 .1 min (9 .3–21 .1 min over the 87 fits at that budget). As a comparison, a plain value loss (without gradient-match) at the identical budget the median is 4.2 min. So the derivative matching costs about a factor of three in wall time. Cost grows with the complexity cap: raising maxsize from 20 to 30 and then to 40 moves the mean fit from 13 .6 to 18 .3 and 23 .8 min under the Sobolev loss, and from 4 .3 to 6 .5 and 9 .1 min under the plain value loss. 6. CONCLUSION 

In this work, we have presented symbolic regression models for the cosmological and astrophysical depen-dence of the one-dimensional Lyman-α power spectrum. We trained our symbolic regression models on emulator spectra based on the multi-fidelity (across low- and high-resolution) PRIYA simulation suite. We used the multi-fidelity approach for PySR to learn equations that model 13 the dependence of the power spectra on individual cos-mological and astrophysical parameters. We evaluated performance in both high- and low-fidelity regimes. By building a framework of equations unique to each pa-rameter, we are able to accurately model the PRIYA simulations in a multi-fidelity setting. We have also shown that value accuracy is not suffi-cient for an emulator distilled into symbolic form to be used in a downstream analysis such as a Fisher forecast. An equation can reproduce the P1D flux power spectrum to the percent level and still misrepresent its parame-ter derivative. We introduced a derivative-faithfulness diagnostic, E∂ , that scores each equation’s parameter slope against the GP emulator. We also introduce a derivative-matching (Sobolev) training loss that repairs an unfaithful slope at the same search budget. Applied to the eleven PRIYA parameters at z = 3 .6, the Sobolev objective yields a derivative-faithful equation for nine of them, including the primordial amplitude and tilt, which the plain value loss leaves slope-biased. Only the Hub-ble parameter h and the AGN feedback strength ϵAGN ,whose P1D imprints are weak or degenerate, resist. We find that this gradient-matching repair comes from redefining the training objective rather than from a larger complexity search: value-loss is fragile to derivative-faithfulness in both the complexity budget and the ran-dom seed, while the Sobolev loss reaches faithfulness reliably at the smallest budget. The pitfalls we doc-ument here in this work would be useful for similar symbolic-emulator work in the future. Beyond accuracy, a key advantage of this approach lies in the potential interpretability of the learned equations. The result-ing symbolic expressions make explicit how cosmologi-cal, astrophysical, and resolution parameters contribute to the power spectrum. In systematically varying single-dimensional relations, we can quantify the learned de-pendency of the emulator output on any given param-eter, mitigating a “black-box” treatment of the emula-tor. For example, the learned equation for h contains h

only inside a single logarithm and is dominated by its dependence on resolution and wavenumber, so the slope it returns misses the emulator’s altogether. In contrast, 

dτ 0 enters its own equation linearly at leading order. Our method provides a pathway towards assessing whether emulator corrections are meaningful in a phys-ical sense rather than merely statistical. In particular, the learned dependence on simulation resolution suggests that symbolic regression can offer a new way to analyze how numerical effects propagate through Lyman-α forest 1D analysis. Overall, this work shows that symbolic regression is a useful tool to complement cosmological emulators. By combining statistical emulators with analytical expres-sions, we are able to extract the underlying parameter dependence of a multi-fidelity model, begin interpreta-tion of what is learned in a black-box emulator, and in-form future computational efforts on high-need areas of parameter space.

C. DERIVATIVE ERROR THRESHOLD AND THE SOBOLEV LOSS 

Below we summarize the technical details how we calculate the derivative error. For each parameter θi, we compare the derivatives of the PySR equation with respect to θi, keeping all other parameters at their fiducial values (one parameter at a time). While the Sobolev loss compares these derivatives across the training samples, the derivative error E∂ (Eq. 9) evaluates their agreement only at θi = θi, fid , across the k-bins. We use the same central finite-difference stencil for both PySR and GP. The score is evaluated only where the GP slope is non-negligible, with 

|∂θ log P GP | ≥ 10 −3 of the peak response for that parameter. This avoids taking a relative error against a slope that is effectively zero. In practice, the cut removes almost nothing. All reported scores retain all 48 k-bins, except for ϵAGN 

at z = 2 .6, where 47 are retained. We calculate the derivative error for each k-bin and take the median across the retained bins as E∂ . This measures how well the equation reproduces the GP parameter gradient for a typical k-bin, without being strongly affected by a few bins with large errors. We classify an equation as faithful if E∂ < 0.25. Under the Sobolev loss, this threshold clearly separates the equations at z = 3 .6: across five random seeds, the nine faithful parameters have errors no larger than 0 .246, while the other two, h and ϵAGN , have errors above 0 .59. The improvement is also clear at z = 4 .2, but less so at z = 2 .6, where the spectral tilt nP has an error of 0 .255, just above the threshold. The classification is also more sensitive to the threshold under the value-only loss. We therefore report the measured E∂ values alongside the classifications for transparency. We implement the Sobolev loss in PySR by comparing both the predicted values and parameter gradients of the symbolic equations with those of the GP emulator at each resolution.
