# Seldon TFM family: Seldon-Mini, Seldon and Seldon+ 

Neuralk-AI Research Team November 2026 

Abstract 

We introduce Seldon, a family of tabular foundation models. Our classifier Seldon+ ranks first among 15 models on TabBench, our benchmark of 124 classification datasets, and on its variant with missing values. In our own runs of public benchmarks, it also ranks first on BeyondArena IID classification and TabBench-Bio, and second on TabArena classification. The open-weight models Seldon-Mini (27M parameters) and Seldon (213M parameters) both lie on the TabArena Pareto front of Elo against inference time: no entry is both more accurate and faster. On TabArena, Seldon ranks fourth and Seldon-Mini seventh among 109 leaderboard entries, and on the IID track of BeyondArena, Seldon ranks second among 42 entries. Seldon-Mini and Seldon handle both classification and regression, use an architecture similar to those of Kumo-Tabular, TabFM and TabPFN-3.5, and are trained on synthetic tables only.

# 1 Introduction 

Gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) and CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) have long been the default model for tabular data, but each new table requires its own training and hyperparameter tuning. A tabular foundation model (TFM) needs neither. It is pretrained once, typically on synthetic tables, and then makes predictions on a new table without being trained on it. It reads the labelled rows of the table as context and predicts the labels of the remaining rows in a single forward pass, with no gradient step and no hyperparameter search. Because much of the data behind decisions in finance, healthcare and industry is tabular, a model that is accurate on new tables without training or tuning could remove the per-task cycle of model selection and tuning, and 1arXiv:2611.32257v1 [cs.LG] 24 Nov 2026 bring strong predictive models to teams without machine-learning expertise. TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025) introduced this approach, and TabICL (Qu et al., 2025, 2026a) scaled it to larger tables. TFMs now lead public leaderboards such as TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), ahead of tuned tree ensembles, with recent models including TabPFN-3.5 (Jäger et al., 2026), LimiX-2 (Zhang et al., 2026), TabFM (Kong et al., 2026) and Kumo-Tabular (Qu et al., 2026b). Yet these models differ widely in inference cost: among the ten best entries of TabArena, the median time to predict 1,000 rows ranges from under 0.1 to over 8 seconds (Figure 2). This report presents Seldon, a family of three TFMs that succeeds an earlier Seldon model (Atienza et al., 2026). Two of them, Seldon-Mini (27M parameters) and Seldon (213M parameters), are released with open weights, each as a classifier and a regressor. Like Kumo-Tabular, TabFM and TabPFN-3.5, they interleave column and row blocks, with a cell embedding close to that of TabFM, and they are trained from scratch on synthetic tables drawn from our own prior only. The third, Seldon+, is a classifier served through the Neuralk API, for which we report results only. We first evaluate the models on public leaderboards. On TabArena, Seldon ranks fourth among 109 entries, and Seldon-Mini and Seldon both lie on the Pareto front of Elo against inference time, overall and on classification. On the IID track of BeyondArena (Purucker et al., 2026), Seldon ranks second among 42 entries. We also report the published leaderboard of the biomedical benchmark TabBench-Bio (Kreuer et al., 2026). In our own runs, Seldon+ ranks first on BeyondArena IID classification and TabBench-Bio, and second on TabArena classification. We then evaluate on our own benchmarks. TabBench gathers 124 classification datasets, with two variants focused on industrial use cases (TabBench-Indus) and on missing values (TabBench-Missing). On TabBench, Seldon+ and Seldon rank first and second among 15 models, and Seldon+ also ranks first on TabBench-Missing. Breaking TFMs is a new suite of 10 synthetic probes, each isolating one failure mode of TFMs against a control table. Figure 1 summarises the rank of each Seldon model on every benchmark, and Figure 2 places Seldon-Mini and Seldon against inference time on TabArena. Section 2 frames TFMs as prior-fitted networks and reviews their architectures, Section 3 describes the Seldon models, and Section 4 reports the results. Our contributions are as follows. 

• Two open-weight TFMs, Seldon-Mini (27M parameters) and Seldon (213M parameters), trained only on synthetic tables, that compete with the strongest tabular models. On TabArena, Seldon ranks fourth among 109 entries, and third on classification. Seldon-Mini ranks seventh with eight times fewer parameters. Both lie on the Pareto front of Elo against inference time: no entry is both more accurate and faster. 

• Results that hold beyond TabArena. Seldon ranks second among 42 entries on the IID track of BeyondArena. On TabBench, our suite of 124 classification datasets, the API model Seldon+ ranks first and Seldon second among 15 models. Seldon+ also ranks first on BeyondArena IID classification and TabBench-Bio in our own runs. On TabArena, BeyondArena and TabBench alike, Seldon wins more than half of its comparisons against at least four of six leading TFMs. 

• Breaking TFMs, a suite of 10 synthetic probes that each isolate one failure mode of TFMs against a control table, with known Bayes-optimal accuracy. Seldon-Mini ranks first overall, and Seldon and Seldon-Mini are two of the only three models that solve the parity of eight bits. 

# 2 Background 

Seldon-Mini and Seldon are trained only on synthetic tables, and their architecture builds on TabICL and on more recent TFMs that interleave column and row blocks. Section 2.1 recalls why training on synthetic tables transfers to real ones, Section 2.2 reviews the priors and training data of recent TFMs, and Section 2.3 reviews their architectures.

2.2 Priors and Training Data 

The prior determines which tables a PFN learns to handle. Recent TFMs differ in how they build it, and some add real tables to their training data. 

Structural causal models. The prior of TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025) samples a structural causal model (SCM): a random directed acyclic graph in which each node is a noisy random function of its parents. The features and the target are read from a subset of the nodes, and the target is discretised into classes for classification. Varying the graph, the functions and the noise yields tables with diverse feature interactions and dependence structures. 

Mixtures of priors. TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) complements this prior with SCMs whose functions are tree ensembles, which produce the axis-aligned, piecewise-constant relations that gradient-boosted trees model well. Mitra (Zhang et al., 2025) studies mixtures of synthetic priors more systematically, and finds that the diversity and the distinctiveness of the priors in a mixture both matter for the performance of the resulting model on real tables. 

Real tables. Other TFMs use real data. TabDPT (Ma et al., 2025) is pretrained on a collection of real tables with self-supervised objectives, and Real-TabPFN (Garg et al., 2025) continues the pretraining of TabPFN v2 on curated real-world tables. Real data brings distributions that a synthetic prior may miss, but it limits the number of distinct training tables and must be kept disjoint from the evaluation benchmarks. 

Training on larger tables. The cost of training grows with the number of rows of the tables, so several TFMs are trained on small tables first. TabICL, for instance, trains in successive stages of increasing table size. 2.3 Tabular Foundation Model Architectures 

TFM architectures differ mainly in how they attend over the rows and the features of a table, and in how their cost grows with the size of the table. 

Cell-level attention. TabPFN v2 (Hollmann et al., 2025) represents each cell as a token and alternates attention across the features of a row and across the rows of a feature. Every cell thus interacts with the other cells of its row and of its column, but the cost grows quadratically with the number of rows, for every feature. 

Column, row and ICL stages. TabICL (Qu et al., 2025) reduces this cost by splitting the model into three stages. A column stage embeds each cell with a set transformer over the column, with inducing points (Lee et al., 2019). A row stage then aggregates the cells of a row into a few readout tokens. Finally, an in-context learning (ICL) stage attends from each test row to the context rows, over one vector per row instead of one per cell. 

Interleaved column and row blocks. In TabICL, the column and row stages each run once. Kumo-Tabular (Qu et al., 2026b), TabFM (Kong et al., 2026) and TabPFN-3.5 (Jäger et al., 2026) instead interleave them layer by layer: each layer applies a column block and a row block. TabFM also embeds each cell with Fourier features of a group of features. Seldon-Mini and Seldon use interleaved column and row blocks, with a cell embedding close to that of TabFM (Section 3).

3.1 Architecture 

Seldon-Mini has the size of Kumo-Tabular-Small, and Seldon the size of Kumo-Tabular, the large configuration (Qu et al., 2026b). The prediction for a test row therefore does not depend on the other test rows. In Seldon, the test rows attend with two key-value heads, shared by the 16 query heads as in grouped-query attention, while the context rows use all 16 heads. In Seldon-Mini, all rows use all 8 heads. 

Blocks and head. All blocks are pre-norm transformer blocks with RMSNorm, query-key normalisation and a feed-forward layer of twice the width. The classification head returns logits for up to 10 classes. Above 10 classes, an error-correcting output code turns the task into max (⌈ 𝐶 /9⌉, 4⌈log 10 𝐶 ⌉) 10-way tasks, for 𝐶 classes. Each task keeps 9 classes apart and merges the others into the tenth, and every class is kept apart in at least one task. The code is drawn to maximise the smallest Hamming distance between classes. The model runs once per task, and the score of a class averages its log probabilities over the tasks that keep it apart. The regression head returns 999 quantiles of the standardised target. The point prediction is their mean. 

3.2 Prior and Training 

Seldon-Mini and Seldon are trained on synthetic tables only. The tables are drawn from a prior over structural causal models (SCMs), in the line of the priors of TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023) and TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026a). The classifier and the regressor are trained separately, on classification and regression tables drawn from this prior. 

Prior. Each training table is generated by a random SCM. A random directed acyclic graph is drawn over latent nodes, and the value of each node is computed from those of its parents,

3.3 Inference 

Predictions average an ensemble of forward passes: 8 for the Seldon classifier and 4 for the Seldon-Mini classifier and both regressors. Each member applies one of four numerical transforms (identity, Yeo-Johnson, robust scaling or a rank-Gaussian transform), a sign flip of half of the numerical features and a feature permutation. For classification, each member also shifts the class labels cyclically. Half of the members shuffle the codes of the categorical features. Classification averages the logits of the members. Regression sorts the quantiles of each member and combines them with a trimmed mean. 

3.4 Usage 

Seldon-Mini and Seldon are available as SeldonClassifier and SeldonRegressor in the neuralk-oss Python package, which can be installed from PyPI ( pypi.org/project/neuralk-oss ). Both follow the scikit-learn interface: fit takes the labelled rows that serve as context, and predict returns predictions for new rows, with the ensemble described above. The weights are hosted on Hugging Face, in gated repositories, under two licences. Seldon-Mini uses the Seldon Model License Agreement, which allows internal business use, whereas Seldon uses the Seldon Research and Evaluation License, which is limited to non-commercial research and evaluation. Seldon+ is not released as weights and is accessed through the Neuralk API.

Metrics. TabArena and BeyondArena score binary classification with ROC AUC, multiclass classification with log loss and regression with RMSE. TabBench scores every dataset with ROC AUC, and TabBench-Bio scores classification with macro-F1 and regression with RMSE. TabArena, BeyondArena, TabBench and TabBench-Bio rank models with a Bradley–Terry Elo fitted on per-split comparisons (Bradley and Terry, 1952; Erickson et al., 2025). The 95% confidence intervals come from a bootstrap over datasets, with 200 resamples on TabArena and BeyondArena and 100 on TabBench. The anchor is the default random forest at 1000 on TabArena, and the default XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) at 1000 on BeyondArena. TabBench has no random forest on its board, so its Elo is centred instead: its 12 external models average 1000. Its Elo scale is therefore not comparable with the other two. On TabBench, the external models are first rated among themselves, and each Seldon model is then rated against this fixed field. The Seldon models are therefore not compared with each other in the Elo fit, and we compare them directly with win rates. The win rate of model A against model B is the fraction of comparisons that A wins, with ties counted as one half: per split on TabArena and BeyondArena, with each dataset weighted equally, and per dataset on TabBench. A model that fails on a dataset receives the score of a reference model: the default random forest on TabArena and BeyondArena, CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) on TabBench. 

4.2 Public Benchmarks 

4.2.1 TabArena 

TabArena has 51 datasets, 38 for classification and 13 for regression.

4.3.2 Breaking TFMs 

Breaking TFMs has 10 probes, each testing one failure mode at 2 to 4 levels. Each level is one synthetic classification table of 2,000 rows, and the lowest level of a probe is a control without the failure mode. The tables are drawn from known class probabilities, so the accuracy of the Bayes-optimal classifier is known. Appendix B defines every probe and level. Our evaluation service scores each model by accuracy, with 5-fold cross-validation. We divide the accuracy of a model on each table by the Bayes-optimal accuracy of that table. The relative loss of a model on a probe is the drop of this ratio from the control to the hardest level, in percent. Figure 8 gives the relative losses, with a negative loss shown as 0. The overall rank of a model is its mean rank over the nine probes every model is scored on. Seldon-Mini ranks first overall and Seldon fourth. Seldon+ and the tree-based models are not evaluated on this suite. 

Seldon, Seldon-Mini and Xiaomi-TabLDM solve the parity of 8 bits. The target is the parity of 8 binary features, with 5% of the labels flipped. Seldon, Seldon-Mini and Xiaomi-TabLDM reach the Bayes-optimal accuracy of 95.4%, and TabFM reaches 93.8%. Kumo-Tabular reaches 67.4%, and the other models stay between 50.8% and 63.3%. 

Seldon has the highest accuracy on the high-frequency target. The target is the sign of sin (50 𝑥 ), with 5% of the labels flipped. Seldon reaches 91.7% and Seldon-Mini 90.6%, against 94.5% for the Bayes-optimal classifier. TabICLv2 and Xiaomi-TabLDM fall to 50.0% and 59.8%. 

Every model loses accuracy on many classes and on a high-cardinality categorical. With 200 classes, the relative loss of every model is between 11.4% and 18.9%. Kumo-Tabular-Medium and Kumo-Tabular are the most accurate, with 67.5% and 67.3%, and Seldon reaches 65.4%. With a categorical feature of 1000 levels, the four best models are Seldon-Mini, Kumo-Tabular-Medium, Kumo-Tabular and Seldon, between 74.2% and 74.5%, against 81.0% for the Bayes-optimal classifier. With 500 noise columns, the relative loss is 4.3% for Seldon and 0.5% for Seldon-Mini. On the target leak, the target hidden in a parity, the missing-not-at-random column, the shuffled category codes and the offset of 10 6, the relative loss of every model is below 1%.

# 5 Conclusion 

This report introduced Seldon, a family of three tabular foundation models. Seldon-Mini and Seldon are trained on synthetic tables only and predict on a new table in a single forward pass, with no training and no tuning. On the public benchmarks, Seldon ranks fourth among 109 entries on TabArena and second among 42 on the IID track of BeyondArena, and Seldon-Mini and Seldon both lie on the TabArena Pareto front of Elo against inference time. On our own benchmarks, Seldon+ and Seldon rank first and second on TabBench, and Breaking TFMs shows that Seldon and Seldon-Mini solve the parity of eight bits, which most of the other foundation models we evaluate do not. A small model can remain competitive: with 27M parameters, Seldon-Mini lies on that front alongside Seldon, eight times larger. 

The Seldon Plan. A single table of independent rows is only the beginning. The structured data on which decisions are made is richer: rows arrive over time, tables are linked to one another, cells hold text and images next to numbers, and the questions asked of the data are often causal, about the effect of an action rather than the value of a label. Synthetic priors are a natural path towards a broader array of capabilities, since structural causal models already describe time, relations and interventions. # A TabBench 

TabBench is not public. This appendix gives what is needed to rebuild it: the datasets of each suite, how a table is read, the splits, the metric and the scoring rules. Tables 3 to 5 list the datasets. 

A.1 Suites 

TabBench has three suites. All three are classification suites, and every dataset is an OpenML dataset, named by its OpenML ID. 

• TabBench holds 124 datasets, chosen to cover as many kinds of real tabular data as possible (Table 3). Each dataset has one domain label, used only to group results. The datasets have 500 to 150,000 rows, with a median of 3,996, and 2 to 1,776 features, with a median of 16.5. Of the 124 tasks, 86 are binary and 38 multiclass, with up to 100 classes. 

• TabBench-Indus holds 54 datasets, each an industrial use case built from a public dataset (Table 4). A dataset enters the suite when it is relevant to a business, when its prediction can inform a decision, and when a tabular model is the right tool for it. Duplicates are excluded, and so are datasets on which gradient-boosted trees already score close to the maximum. Each dataset has one use-case label. Of the 54 datasets, 52 are also in TabBench; the other two are KDDCup09_churn (1112) and Credit-Risk-Dataset (43454). 

• TabBench-Missing holds the 21 TabBench datasets with at least one missing cell (Table 5). The share of missing cells ranges from 0.01% to 63%. The datasets are grouped in three bands: under 1% of cells missing (10 datasets), 1% to 10% (8) and over 10% (3). TabBench-Missing needs no run of its own: its scores are the TabBench scores of these 21 datasets. 

A.2 Data 

Each table is the full OpenML dataset, as returned by openml.datasets.get_dataset(id).get_data() . No row is removed or subsampled, and no feature is removed. The target is the default target attribute of the OpenML dataset, and loan_status for Credit-Risk-Dataset (43454), which has none; every other column is a feature. Our evaluation service reads a parquet snapshot of each table, taken once, so that every run sees the same rows. The service hands each model the raw features of the training rows. It replaces ±∞ with a missing value and drops the columns that are missing on every training row. Missing values are kept. A model that does not encode categorical or datetime features itself receives a default encoding, fitted on the training rows only: constant and duplicate columns are dropped, a datetime becomes seconds since the epoch, plus its year, month, day and weekday when these vary over the training rows, and every other non-numeric column becomes integer codes in sorted order of its levels (the declared order for a category column), with a level unseen in training coded as missing. The Seldon models receive the raw features and apply their own preprocessing. 

A.3 Protocol 

Splits. Each dataset is evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation, stratified on the target and shuffled with seed 0. The folds are those of scikit-learn’s StratifiedKFold , with n_splits=5 , shuffle=True and random_state=0 ,applied to the rows in their OpenML order. A model is fitted on four folds and predicts class probabilities on the fifth, once per fold. 13 Metric. Each fold is scored with ROC AUC. For a binary task, it is the ROC AUC of the second class in sorted label order. For a multiclass task, it is the mean of the one-vs-rest ROC AUCs over the classes present in the test fold. The score of a model on a dataset is the mean over the five folds. 

Failures. A model fails on a dataset when any of its five folds raises an error, runs out of memory, or exceeds the time budget of 9,000 seconds per dataset, that is 1,800 seconds per fold. A failed dataset receives the score of the default CatBoost on that dataset. 

Ranking. Models are ranked with the Elo and the win rates of Section 4.1. The external models are first rated among themselves, with 100 bootstrap resamples over datasets for the confidence intervals. Each Seldon model is then rated against this fixed field.

16 B Breaking TFMs 

Breaking TFMs is a suite of 31 synthetic binary and multiclass classification tables. They form 10 probes, each testing one failure mode at 2 to 4 levels. The lowest level of each probe is its control, the same kind of table without the failure mode. Table 6 lists the probes. 

B.1 Tables 

Each table has 2,000 rows, and the class probabilities 𝑝 (𝑦 | 𝑥 ) of each row are known in closed form. A label is drawn from them, except in two cases. In the target leak, target in parity and missing not at random probes, the label is drawn from the base probability, the leak or the missingness is drawn given the label, and 𝑝 (𝑦 | 𝑥 ) is the posterior. In the many-classes probe, the classes are balanced and the features are drawn given the class. The tables are fixed: each is drawn once, with a random generator seeded with 0.
