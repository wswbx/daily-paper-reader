Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for generative recommendation, where recommended items are generated directly from users’ interaction histories. To further improve recommendation quality, reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to align these models with recommendation objectives. However, existing RL methods rely on scalar rewards to implicitly guide exploration. These rewards do not specify which aspects of a candidate conflict with the user’s preferences or how the next candidate should differ. To address this gap, we propose VerRec, a verbalized feedback-guided RL framework for generative recommendation. Specifically, a user simulator generates personalized verbalized feedback based on the user’s historical preferences and the candidate item profile. The policy uses this feedback to produce refined recommendations. Original and refined candidates form an expanded group and are jointly optimized with group relative policy optimization. The user simulator is only required during training and is removed at inference time. Experiments on three Amazon review datasets with two generative recommendation backbones demonstrate that VerRec improves recommendation accuracy and ranking quality. Further analyses show that verbalized guidance encourages exploration of more diverse candidates and helps the policy discover more rewarding recommendations during training. 

Keywords: Large Language Models, Generative Recommendation, User Simulator, Reinforcement Learning

## 1 Introduction 

Generative recommendation (GR) reformulates personalized rec-ommendation as autoregressive generation of the next item condi-tioned on a user’s interaction history [12, 26, 37, 52]. A common training pipeline combines supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with sub-sequent reinforcement learning (RL) [10, 34, 53, 54]. Although SFT trains the recommendation policy by fitting to the interactions, its supervision is largely restricted to observed behavior, causing poor generalization and diversity [9, 21, 38, 53, 54]. RL then complements SFT by sampling items from the current policy and optimizing the policy with recommendation-oriented rewards [23, 34, 54]. Explor-ing interactions that are absent from the training data, RL extends supervision beyond observed behavior and thereby improves the generalization ability, making it an important step in GR train-ing [20, 23, 32, 44, 50]. RL for GR follows an iterative process. Conditioned on a user’s interaction history, the policy samples candidate items and receives scalar rewards based on recommendation objectives. It then up-dates its parameters to increase the likelihood of candidates with higher rewards. Prior work has applied this learning process to improve recommendation accuracy or user engagement through item generation [20, 32, 53, 54], and to optimize reasoning trajec-tories together with item predictions based on recommendation outcomes [23, 34, 44]. In essence, these methods rely on scalar re-ward to judge the policy’s behaviors and update the policy to favor those that can lead to higher rewards. User history

However, the reliance on scalar rewards will lead to the implicit exploration issue : a low reward can only indicate an unsuccessful recommendation but fail to reveal the underlying reason and opti-mization direction. As an example shown in Figure 1, we consider a user whose interaction history contains fantasy and adventure books. Policy A recommends finance books that miss the user’s interests, whereas Policy B recommends books the user has already read and offers insufficient diversity. Both recommendations may receive the same low reward, but require different corrections: the former should move toward the user’s preferred genres, while the latter should avoid previously consumed books within those genres. The reward alone does not indicate which correction is needed, leaving the policy to discover better candidates through repeated trial and error. arXiv:2611.22410v1 [cs.IR] 17 Nov 2026 Chen et al. 

Based on the above insights, we aim to investigate explicit guid-ance in RL, which can identify the cause of failure and direct explo-ration toward suitable candidates. However, incorporating explicit guidance into RL for GR presents two key challenges: 

• Guidance acquisition. The guidance should identify why a candidate matches or conflicts with a user’s preferences and in-dicate how it can be improved. Simulated verbalized feedback (VF) offers a potential source of such guidance by expressing preference assessments and refinement suggestions in natural language [4, 17, 48]. The challenge is to obtain reliable, personal-ized feedback based on the user’s historical preferences and the recommended candidate, so that it provides concrete directions for refinement. 

• Guidance utilization. The obtained feedback should inform policy updates so that the policy can internalize the suggested improvements. However, verbalized feedback is discrete and can-not directly guide policy updates through backpropagation. The challenge is to incorporate the information in textual guidance into rewards, enabling the policy to learn from useful suggestions and improve subsequent recommendations. Building on these insights, we propose VerRec, a reinforcement learning framework with verbalized feedback for generative rec-ommendation. At its core, VerRec consists of two tightly coupled components. 1) Personalized Feedback Simulation : For each original candidate generated by the policy, an LLM-based user simulator generates personalized VF based on the user’s historical preferences and the candidate. This VF explains how the candidate matches or conflicts with the user’s preferences. 2) Joint Optimization : For each original candidate, the policy combines the candidate, its cor-responding VF, and the user’s history to construct a refinement prompt and generate a refined candidate. The original and refined candidates are then combined into an expanded group and jointly optimized with GRPO using scalar rewards. This process allows VF to guide exploration while the reward function penalizes unsuc-cessful original candidates. To evaluate the effectiveness of VerRec, we conduct comprehen-sive experiments on three real-world datasets. The results demon-strate that VerRec effectively uses verbalized feedback to guide exploration and improve recommendation performance. Further analyses show that verbalized guidance encourages exploration of more diverse candidates and enables the policy to attain higher rewards as training progresses. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows: 

• We formulate personalized verbalized feedback as an explicit ex-ploration signal that guides the policy in reinforcement learning for generative recommendation. 

• We propose VerRec, a novel RL framework that integrates per-sonalized feedback simulation, feedback-conditioned candidate refinement, and joint GRPO optimization over original and re-fined candidates. 

• We evaluate VerRec on three Amazon review datasets with two generative recommendation backbones. The results show im-provements on recommendation performance and reveal how verbalized feedback affects policy exploration and optimization. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Generative Recommendation 

Generative recommendation has emerged as a paradigm for directly generating items from user interaction histories. Early work ex-plores recommendation through text generation, using pretrained language models to process user and item information and generate recommendation outputs [2, 13, 46]. Subsequent work introduces compact semantic IDs (SIDs) to represent items and formulates next-item prediction as SID generation [20, 26, 37, 54]. Recent work further improves generative recommendation through multi-task supervised fine-tuning that aligns language semantics with col-laborative signals [15, 42, 52]. Beyond supervised learning, recent studies apply reinforcement learning to align generative recom-mendation with recommendation objectives, either by directly op-timizing item generation [9, 20, 32, 50] or by jointly optimizing reasoning and item prediction [4, 15, 23, 44]. These approaches use scalar rewards to evaluate recommendation outcomes and guide policy updates. Our work introduces personalized verbalized feed-back to explicitly guide candidate exploration. 

## 2.2 Reinforcement Learning with Verbalized Feedback 

Verbalized (natural language) feedback provides actionable guid-ance beyond scalar rewards by identifying errors and suggesting improvements. Existing work has primarily explored such feed-back in general-purpose tasks. One line of work uses such feedback at inference time, revising outputs through iterative self-critique or retaining verbal reflections to guide subsequent attempts with-out updating model parameters [24, 30]. External tools can further ground this process by providing feedback for output verification and correction [6, 14]. Another line incorporates such feedback into model training, either by optimizing critique generators to improve a fixed model’s revisions [1, 25] or by constructing syn-thetic preference data for offline optimization while constraining changes in unrelated contexts [31]. Recent studies combine textual critiques with scalar rewards in online RL, learning from initial responses and their refinements to guide exploration and using successful refinements as off-policy guidance [17, 48]. Our work investigates explicit guidance in RL for generative recommendation and develops a recommendation-specific framework for guidance acquisition and utilization. 

## 2.3 User Simulator 

User simulators provide controllable environments for training and evaluating recommender systems without costly online in-teractions. Traditional platforms model user choices or responses through handcrafted or learned behavioral models [18, 29, 51]. Re-cent LLM-based simulators enrich this process with profiles, mem-ory, and reasoning. One line of work directly initializes generative user agents with interaction histories and user profiles, relying on the in-context understanding ability of LLMs to simulate user pref-erences and behaviors [5, 22, 36, 45, 47]. Another line fine-tunes LLM-based simulators on user feedback or behavioral trajectories, enabling them to better understand user preferences and align sim-ulated behaviors with real users [8, 39, 49]. In guidance ac-quisition (Section 4.2), a user simulator provides personalized ver-balized feedback for sampled candidates. In guidance utilization

## 5 Experiments 

In this section, we present the experimental results and correspond-ing analysis to answer the following research questions (RQs). 

• RQ1: How does the recommendation performance of VerRec compare to other baselines? 

• RQ2: How do different feedback strategies compare in simulation quality and downstream recommendation performance? 

• RQ3: How does verbalized feedback affect candidate exploration during RL training? 

• RQ4: How do the key components of VerRec affect its overall performance? 

## 5.1 Experimental Setup 

5.1.1 Datasets and Evaluation. Following prior works [2, 3, 20], we conduct experiments on three categories of the Amazon review dataset 1: Cell Phones and Accessories (Cell Phones), Baby, and Book. Each dataset contains users’ interaction histories and rich textual metadata associated with the items. We apply 5-core filtering to ensure that every user and item has at least five interactions. Each user’s interactions are chronologically ordered and segmented using a sliding window with a maximum sequence length of 10. To simulate realistic recommendation scenarios, we adopt a temporal split and partition the resulting sequences into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 8:1:1, reserving the most recent interactions for validation and testing. Detailed statistics for each category are reported in Table 1. For evaluation metrics, we adopt the full-item ranking setting, where each model is required to rank all candidate items rather than a sampled subset. We evaluate the effectiveness of top-𝐾 rec-ommendations using Hit Ratio (H@ 𝐾 ) and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (N@ 𝐾 ), with 𝐾 ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 }.

10 −5 for RL. Both stages use AdamW with a batch size of 128. For the key RL hyperparameters, we set 𝛾 = 0.1 and 𝛽 = 0.001. We adopt Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 [41] both to summarize user and item preferences from historical reviews and to serve as the user simulator that generates verbalized feedback. In the performance comparison with existing recommenders, traditional recommenders are trained with binary cross-entropy and the Adam optimizer; learning rates are chosen from {10 −2, 10 −3, 10 −4 }, and the weight-decay term is scanned over {10 −2, 10 −3, 10 −4, 10 −5, 10 −6 }.For LLM-based baselines, we carefully tune the hyperparameters of the baselines following the suggestions in the original papers.

5.3.2 Effect of Feedback Quality on Recommendation Performance. 

To examine how different feedback strategies affect recommenda-tion learning, we compare the following settings on MiniOneRec-SFT and LETTER. “Bin.” provides binary feedback derived from user interaction records, either “I like it” or “I do not like it”. “Shuf.” replaces each user’s feedback with that of a randomly selected user for the same candidate, disrupting personalized preference align-ment. For “MGF.” (MGFRec) and “A4R.” (Agent4Rec), we replace the user simulator in our framework with their respective simulators to generate feedback, while “Ours” uses our proposed simulator. Figure 6 compares the downstream recommendation perfor-mance of different feedback settings on both backbones. “Bin.” yields performance similar to “w/o VF”, suggesting that binary feedback provides limited guidance for policy optimization. Notably, “Shuf.” and “MGF.” perform even worse than the setting without verbal-ized feedback, indicating that mismatched feedback can misguide candidate refinement and hinder policy learning. This further high-lights that the effectiveness of verbalized feedback depends on its alignment with user preferences, rather than merely introducing textual guidance. The relative ordering of our simulator, “A4R.”, and “MGF.” is consistent between the simulation evaluation and the downstream comparison. Better simulation scores are accompanied by stronger recommendation performance, suggesting that more accurate and informative verbalized feedback can provide more effective learning signals for recommendation models.

## 5.5 Impact of Key Components (RQ4) 

5.5.1 Ablation Study. To assess the contribution of each compo-nent, we conduct ablation studies of VerRec on Cell Phones using MiniOneRec-SFT and LETTER. The “w/o RL” variant denotes the original backbone before RL training. The “w/o VF” variant opti-mizes 2 𝐺 candidates per input on the corresponding backbone with-out verbalized feedback. The “w/o Jopt” variant excludes original candidates from optimization and trains only on refined candidates. As shown in Figure 9, VerRec achieves the best performance on both metrics across the two backbones. Removing verbalized feedback reduces performance, suggesting that explicit guidance about preference mismatches and revision directions benefits learn-ing beyond scalar rewards alone. Removing joint optimization also reduces performance, although training on refined candidates still improves over the original backbones. This indicates the benefit of jointly learning from original and refined candidates. Together, these results support the contributions of verbalized feedback and joint rollout optimization to recommendation performance.

## 6 Conclusion and Future Work 

In this work, we propose VerRec, a novel reinforcement learning framework with verbalized feedback for generative recommenda-tion. VerRec augments scalar rewards with personalized feedback generated by an LLM-based user simulator. Experiments on three Amazon review datasets with two backbones demonstrate that VerRec improves recommendation performance. Further analyses More Than a Score: Reinforcement Learning with Verbalized Feedback for Generative Recommendation 

highlight the complementary roles of personalized verbalized feed-back and joint optimization in guiding exploration toward more diverse candidates with higher rewards. In future work, we will develop more personalized and accurate user simulators to improve feedback quality. Moreover, incorporat-ing real-world users’ verbalized feedback into this framework is a promising direction for guiding candidate refinement and aligning the policy with users’ evolving preferences. Finally, we will also investigate more efficient feedback generation (e.g., asynchronous generation) to reduce RL training overhead.
