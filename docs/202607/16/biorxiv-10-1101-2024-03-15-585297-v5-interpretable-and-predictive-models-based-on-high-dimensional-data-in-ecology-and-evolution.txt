## Abstract 1

The proliferation of high-dimensional data in ecology and evolutionary biology raises the promise 2

of statistical and machine learning models that are highly predictive and interpretable. However, 3

high-dimensional data are commonly burdened with an inherent trade-off: in-sample prediction of 4

outcomes will improve as additional variables are included in the model, but this may come at the 5

cost of poor predictive accuracy and limited generalizability for future or unsampled observations 6

(out-of-sample prediction). To confront this problem of overfitting, sparse models can focus on key 7

variables by correctly placing low weight on unimportant variables. We competed nine methods to 8

quantify their performance in variable selection and prediction using simulated data with different 9

sample sizes, numbers of variables, and strengths of effects. Overfitting was typical for many 10 

methods and simulation scenarios. Despite this, in-sample and out-of-sample prediction converged 11 

on the true predictive target for simulations with more observations, larger causal effects, and fewer 12 

variables. Accurate variable selection to support process-based understanding will be unattainable 13 

for many realistic sampling schemes in ecology and evolution. We use our analyses to characterize 14 

data attributes for which statistical learning is possible, and illustrate how some sparse methods 15 

can achieve predictive accuracy while mitigating and learning the extent of overfitting. 16 

Keywords: prediction, reducible error, simulation, sparse modeling, statistical learning, variable 17 

selection. 18 

## 1 Introduction 19 

Research in ecology and evolution has seen dramatic growth in data due to technological advances 20 

for automation and high-throughput sampling (e.g., water or air sampling, Porter et al. 2009; 21 

satellite imagery, Ustin & Middleton 2021, Cavender-Bares et al. 2022; DNA sequencing, Halldors-22 

son et al. 2022, Rubinacci et al. 2023; and GPS telemetry, Wilmers et al. 2015, Gigliotti et al. 23 

2022). While large data sets (i.e. many records (N), many predictor variables (P), or both) have 24 

the potential to greatly improve our understanding of complex systems across the continuum of 25 

modeling purposes (e.g. data exploration, inference, prediction; Dormann et al. 2012, Tredennick 26 

et al. 2021), they also pose considerable challenges for data analysis and incorporation into formal 27 

process models. For example, a cross-cutting objective in ecology and evolutionary biology involves 28 

learning the causes of species abundances and distributions, including making predictions about 29 

the responses of wild and cultivated organisms to climate change (Faske et al. 2023, Forister et al. 30 

2023, Grames & Forister 2024, Halsch et al. 2024, Laughlin & McGill 2024, Li et al. 2024). These 31 

predictions are commonly based on contemporary observations of organisms and many dimensions 32 

of abiotic and biotic environments, and intrinsic attributes (e.g., the genome) that could be causally 33 

associated with their species distributions (e.g., Faske et al. 2023, Grames & Forister 2024, Li et al. 34 

2024). Despite remarkably large sampling effort, many studies have more covariates (measures of 35 

climate or the genome) to make their predictions than they do observations. This poses a profound 36 

challenge for predicting organismal responses to climate beyond the settings in which they were 37 

studied. While some subdisciplines would suggest a priori limiting the number of potential pre-38 

dictor parameters to substantially fewer than available samples (i.e., degrees-of-freedom spending; 39 

Giudice et al. 2012, Harrell 2015), or the use of model averaging (Dormann et al. 2018), here we 40 

investigate methods that allow models to be confronted with all available data. 41 

Limited generalizability is common in parameter-rich models and results from overfitting, or 42 

the tendency for flexible models to fit too closely to the observed data (see Glossary of key terms). 43

Overfitting occurs when idiosyncratic variation in the observed data is taken as causal associations 44 

rather than spurious associations that can arise in finite samples (Hastie et al. 2015). Thus, the 45 

availability of big data with potentially many more covariates ( P ; i.e., high-dimensional) than 46 

observations ( N ) may counter-intuitively lead to models with poor predictive performance outside 47 

the scope of the sample (“the curse of dimensionality”, Altman & Krzywinski 2018). Indeed, 48 

ecological studies have experienced a general decline in predictive power over the past century 49 

despite concomitant increases in statistical complexity (Low-D´ ecarie et al. 2014). More broadly, we 50 

face the problem of how to realistically and intelligently constrain the flexibility of our models to 51 

capture potential general patterns while learning about genuine context-dependent effects (Weiss 52 

2008). Given that there are perhaps as many ways to analyze a given dataset as there are biologists 53 

to analyze it (Gould et al. 2025), and that this can affect the magnitude of effect sizes, if not the 54 

direction, it is imperative that we grow our understanding of how the models we fit can be expected 55 

to perform when applied to covariate-rich data sets. 56 

We have made great strides in ecology and evolution in constructing highly predictive models 57 

to address the proliferation of large data sets. Machine learning complements standard methods 58 

for statistical modeling that make a priori choices of which variables to include based on concep-59 

tual, process-based understanding. More generally, advances in machine learning have prompted 60 

a reevaluation of how we value models and what it means for a model to ‘understand’ something 61 

about the world (Breiman 2001b, Mitchell & Krakauer 2023). Statistical models can be used to 62 

learn which variables are associated with a response (i.e., variable or feature selection), to gener-63 

ate accurate predictions about the sampled population (i.e., in-sample prediction), and to make 64 

generalizations about populations, localities, or time-points for which we have no prior informa-65 

tion about response variables (i.e., out-of-sample prediction). We value models that can reveal 66 

potentially causal variables that are associated with the generative processes leading to variation 67 

in the response, while also avoiding shortcut learning that can garner accurate predictions from 68 

tangentially related variables (Geirhos et al. 2020; see Fourcade et al. 2018 for an example with 69 

species distribution modeling). 70 

Ecologists and evolutionary biologists would benefit from a direct comparison and evaluation of 71 

the prospects of different statistical learning methods (Porwal & Raftery 2022), and from greater 72 

clarity about critical issues in model evaluation, including overfitting, the extent to which process 73 

variance is recovered in model predictions, and the explanatory value of important variables. Com-74 

putational methods for sparse modeling might be particularly valuable approaches: these methods 75 

assume that most variables have no causal relationship with the response and therefore only gen-76 

erate estimates for a subset of variables (Hastie et al. 2015). The hope is that selected variables 77 

correspond to the key process variables that are causally linked to variation in the response, which 78 

should limit overfitting and improve predictive performance when generalizing to unsampled or fu-79 

ture observations. This can be contrasted to explicit causal modeling using directed acyclic graphs 80 

(DAGs; Laubach et al. 2021), or variable selection using information criteria to maximize in-sample 81 

prediction and parsimony (see Addicott et al. 2022, Arif & MacNeil 2022, for more extensive discus-82 

sion of prediction at the cost of causal understanding). It is an open question to what extent sparse 83 

models can maximize predictive performance and yield interpretable model outputs, particularly 84 

for high-dimensional data where the number of covariates ( P ) is much greater than the number of 85 

samples ( N ). 86 

We compared the relative performance of several modeling methods by applying them to the 87 

same data sets with known, simulated causal relationships, of the type commonly encountered 88 

in ecology and evolutionary biology. Our 36 core simulation scenarios (100 simulated replicates 89 

each) differed in the number of observations ( N = 50, 150, or 500), the number of covariates ( P90

## 2 Materials and Methods 109 

2.1 Description of simulated data 110 

When selecting the attributes of our simulations, we chose a range of variation in N and P that 111 

resemble current ecological and evolutionary datasets (e.g., months of micrometeorological sensor 112 

measurements, genome-wide estimates of genetic polymorphisms), though we acknowledge that 113 

data dimensionality is continually expanding for most fields. The simulations included 36 scenarios 114 

that considered three main factors in a fully crossed design: the number of observations ( N =115 

50, 150, or 500), the number of variables or features ( P = 100, 1,000, 10,000, or 100,000), and 116 

the effect size of the ten causal variables ( βcausal = 0.1, 0.3, or 0.8; Table S1). To evaluate the 117 

potential benefits of even larger N, we simulated two additional scenarios in which N was 1,000 or 118 

10,000, P was 1,000, and βcausal was 0.3. To thoroughly incorporate and evaluate variable outcomes 119 

among simulations, we obtained 100 replicate data sets for all scenarios. Each replicate data set 120 

consisted of N observations for training (i.e., variable selection and in-sample prediction) and an 121 

additional 500 observations for testing out-of-sample prediction. We did not explore how variation 122 

in the size of the out-of-sample test data influenced model performance, but this would be worthy 123 

of exploration in future studies. 124 

For each replicate, we first created an observation ( N + 500) × variable ( P ) matrix X consisting 125 

of P /50 clusters of correlated variables (50 per cluster). Each cluster of variables was generated 126 

by taking N + 500 draws from a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector μ = 0 and 127 

covariance matrix Σ. We generated covariance matrices using a spherical parameterization (Pin-128 

heiro & Bates 1996), which transforms a P (P +1)/2-dimension vector of unconstrained parameters 129 

θ into a positive semi-definite covariance matrix Σ. The goal of this approach was to create clusters 130 

of variables with a range of correlation strengths, from strongly negatively to strongly positively 131 

correlated, a situation that is common in biological relationships and that presents a challenge for 132 

many modeling approaches (but see Dormann et al. 2013, for solutions to limit colinearity in anal-133 

yses). We found that drawing values of θ from a uniform distribution between -1 and 1 produced 134

Next, we sampled a P -dimension vector of coefficients β representing the causal effects of the 138

linear model using only the 10 causal variables. 144 

We made several decisions in simulating data that could influence our results and interpretation. 145 

For example, causal parameters in the simulated data sets were specified as simple linear effects, 146 

as opposed to non-linear or threshold effects that could be more or less difficult to identify for 147 

some methods. However, while linear approximations of non-linear processes introduce bias, they 148 

can often outperform more flexible non-linear or non-parametric approaches that introduce more 149 

variance, particularly for high-dimensional data (i.e., “the bias-variance trade-off”; James et al. 150 

2021). Furthermore, we intentionally avoided the complexities of causal inference in the presence 151 

of confounding variables and interactions. Instead, we studied a simplified system in which sparse 152 

effects could estimate causal effects; this can be thought of as a best case scenario for the purpose of 153 

statistical learning. Finally, we have explored a fairly simple range of data attributes that might be 154 

encountered in the life sciences, and acknowledge that the consideration of other axes of variation 155 

(e.g. thresholds, degree of colinearity, non-continuous distributions) will undoubtedly lead to new 156 

insights about how we can use modeling approaches to better understand the world. 157 

2.2 Analyses 158 

Each simulated data set was analyzed using nine different methods. Eight of these are penalized 159 

regression methods using standard likelihood (LASSO, Tibshirani 1996; Ridge, Hoerl & Kennard 160 

1970; Elastic Net, Zou & Hastie 2005) or Bayesian estimation (Bayesian LASSO [BLASSO], Park 161 

& Casella 2008; Horseshoe, Carvalho et al. 2010; Spike-and-slab, Ishwaran & Rao 2005; Bayesian 162 

sparse linear mixed model [BSLMM], Zhou et al. 2013; sum of single effects [SuSiE], Wang et al. 163 

2020a). The final method, Random Forest (Breiman 2001a), served as a benchmark to compare 164 

other methods to and is a commonly used, highly flexible machine learning approach based on an 165 

ensemble of decision trees. All analyses were conducted in R v4.2.2 (R Core Team 2023). Each 166 

data set was provided to the methods using the Nextflow v22.10.4.5836 workflow description 167 

language (Di Tommaso et al. 2017) to distribute the work and aggregate the output in a computing 168 

cluster using SLURM (Yoo et al. 2003). We used implementations of Elastic Net, LASSO, and Ridge 169 

in the glmnet v4.1-6 package (Friedman et al. 2010), of BLASSO, Horseshoe, and alternatives 170 

of LASSO and Ridge in the monomvn v1.9-17 package (Gramacy 2023), of Spike-and-slab in the 171 

spikeslab v1.1.6 package (Ishwaran et al. 2010), of SuSiE in the susieR v0.12.27 package 172 

(Wang et al. 2020a), of Random Forest in the randomForest v4.7-1.1 package (Liaw & Wiener 173 

2002), and of BSLMM in the software gemma v0.98.6 (Zhou et al. 2013). We used ‘off-the-shelf’, 174 

default settings for all analyses (as in Porwal & Raftery 2022). This means that we did not perform 175 

hyperparameter optimization, which could potentially lead to biases when comparing methods, as 176 

the importance of optimization varies across models (Bischl et al. 2023). BLASSO and Horseshoe 177 

were not performed for the large N scenarios ( N = 1,000 or 10,000) due to extremely long run times. 178

between estimated and actual parameter values ( β) for all analyses except Random Forest, which 188 

reports importance measures instead of estimates. Variable selection was first assessed for methods 189 

that can return true zeros for parameter estimates (BSLMM, Elastic Net, LASSO, Spike-and-slab) 190

used to train the model, whereas out-of-sample prediction was based on a separate set of 500 202 

observations. Finally, we recorded the runtime required to fit each model to each data set. 203 

## 3 Results 204 

A perfect model would: 1) identify only the ten truly causal variables and accurately estimate 205 

their effect sizes; 2) accurately attribute the variation in the response that arises directly from 206 

the causal variables (i.e., reducible error); and 3) disregard variation in the response arising from 207 

other unmeasured or stochastic processes (i.e., irreducible error; James et al. 2021). Across all 208 

simulations, the magnitude of reducible error was overwhelmingly associated with the effect size 209 

of causal variables (R 2 between the known, additive effects of simulated causal variables and the 210 

simulated response variable was ≈ 0.10, 0.47, and 0.86 with βcausal = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.8, respectively; 211 

Fig. S1). Reducible error was more variable among replicates when N or P were low. 212 

The different methods varied greatly in their performance for variable selection and prediction. 213 

For one example data set ( N = 150, P = 10,000, βcausal = 0.8; see Fig. 1), LASSO monomvn had the 214 

greatest success at delineating between causal and non-causal variables (true positive rate [TPR] 215 

= 0.9; true negative rate [TNR] = 0.997). While Random Forest correctly identified all ten causal 216 

variables (TPR = 1), it implicated a large proportion of non-causal variables as being associated 217 

with the response (TNR = 0.118). In contrast, BSLMM was successful at excluding non-causal 218 

variables (TNR = 0.965), but could only identify six of the causal variables (TPR = 0.6). For 219 

prediction, the true reducible error for the example data set was R 2 = 0.832, which served as the 220 

target for both in-sample and out-of-sample prediction (based on 500 observations not used to 221 

train the model). For LASSO monomvn , in-sample prediction was very close to the reducible error 222 

(R 2 = 0.826), which translated to the highest success for out-of-sample prediction (R 2 = 0.754). 223

trade-off between identifying causal variables and excluding non-causal variables (Fig. 4). Variable 241 

selection was also assessed based on posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) for four Bayesian meth-242 

ods (BLASSO, BSLMM, Horseshoe, SuSiE) using the example data set from Fig. 1. The use of a 243 

small PIP threshold of 0.05 (i.e., only variables with PIP ≥ 0.05 are scored as positives) improved 244 

variable selection for BSLMM and SuSiE, whereas larger thresholds were needed to recover more 245 

limited gains for BLASSO and Horseshoe (Fig. S3). Parameter estimation was remarkably con-246 

sistent across different analyses, and was instead most strongly influenced by data dimensionality: 247 

estimation was worse with greater βcausal and lower N and P (Fig. 2D). This pattern arose because 248 

most methods are worse at estimating variables with β 6 = 0 than those with β = 0, resulting in 249 

larger root mean square error (RMSE) when the proportion of causal to non-causal variables was 250 

relatively large (i.e., when P was small) or when the effect size of causal variables was very different 251 

from zero (i.e., when βcausal was large). The ratio of causal to non-causal variables will be par-252 

ticularly influential on parameter estimation for methods using penalization (e.g. Ridge, LASSO, 253 

Elastic Net, BLASSO), as the penalty will depend on the sample size, with analyses of smaller 254 

sample sizes having more parameters shrunk to 0. Analysis of the 3,600 data sets completed in 255 

2.49 CPU-years, with BLASSO and Horseshoe contributing 46.6% and 46.7% of the total run-time, 256 

respectively (Fig. 2E). 257 

## 4 Discussion 258 

High-throughput and automated data acquisition promises to yield valuable information about 259 

processes that generate variation. This promise is diminished in the common situation in ecology 260 

and evolutionary biology when sampling is of few individuals ( N ) and many potential covariates 261 

(P ; e.g., genomic polymorphisms at 10 6 sites, months of micrometeorological sensor measurements 262 

at 10Hz). Our simulations highlight that the most consistent way to obtain highly predictive and 263 

explanatory models is to maximize the number of independent observations. We recognize that to 264 

tell ecologists and evolutionary biologists ‘just collect more data’ is not always a helpful statement. 265 

However, when we extended our simulations to sample sizes of 1,000 or 10,000 observations, in-266 

sample and out-of-sample R 2 converged to the maximum reducible error, and variable selection 267 

improved for most analyses (Fig. 5). This positive relationship between model performance and 268

based on small sample sizes, especially when P is large or when effect sizes are small relative to 273 

background levels of stochastic variation (Fig. 2). This means that for many typical analyses in 274 

ecology and evolutionary biology, variable selection will suffer from low precision and sensitivity, 275 

and prediction models will be overfit and have poor generalizability. In cases where sparse methods 276 

struggle with a low signal-to-noise ratio, other methods will also struggle (“the bet-on-sparsity 277 

principle”; Hastie et al. 2009), though model averaging frameworks could benefit prediction when 278 

model covariance is low and the ratio of bias to variance is small (Dormann et al. 2018). We do not 279 

want to imply that statistics should not be done when sample sizes are small. Indeed, in some cases, 280 

such as when species are rare or endangered, small samples are better than nothing. However, we 281 

counsel following the precautionary principle when applying models from small training sets to out 282 

of sample data, as prediction is expected to be poor. 283 

It is perhaps n¨ aive to use statistical learning for prediction without large training sets, particu-284 

larly when causal effect sizes are small relative to variance from extraneous sources. The temptation 285 

to do so might stem from working with big data ( N × P ), but not appreciating that all statistical 286 

approaches are expected to yield relatively poor out-of-sample prediction when N is small (e.g., 287 

< 500) and effect sizes are modest. Some of the most remarkable models in society, such as those 288 

for large language modeling (Zhao et al. 2023), natural voice recognition (Xiong et al. 2016), image 289 

segmentation (Kirillov et al. 2023), and board game algorithms (Silver et al. 2018), are typically 290 

trained on enormous sample sizes. For example, Tabak et al. (2019) trained a convolutional neural 291 

network with more than 3 million images to achieve more than 80% out-of-sample accuracy when 292 

detecting ungulates from camera trap imagery. We do believe there is a place for sparse meth-293 

ods in the life sciences when many observations ( N ) can be obtained (Fig. 5). Our simulations 294 

provide context for evaluating different dimensions of model quality and the comparison of model 295 

approaches suggests which methods will be most useful and when. 296 

For most predictive contexts, the primary objective is to account for the reducible error in the 297 

data, as this is the variation in the response associated with generative processes (James et al. 2021). 298 

We were able to directly quantify the reducible error in our simulated data sets and easily identify 299 

cases of overfitting in which in-sample R 2 > reducible error R 2 (Figs. 2A & 3). With empirical data, 300 

the true reducible error and prediction errors arising from model variance and bias will be unknown 301 

(James et al. 2021). While these can be estimated via cross validation or bootstrapping (Fieberg & 302 

Johnson 2015, Harrell 2015), overfitting may be evident when in-sample R 2 exceeds out-of-sample 303 

R2. It is worth emphasizing that in our simulations and analyses, we minimized the potential for 304 

model bias and underfitting by simulating data from simple additive generative processes that are 305 

mirrored in the statistical learning methods we used. In other words, we simulated the best case 306 

scenarios for explaining reducible error, and we still typically fell short. 307 

To minimize errors in prediction, we can strive for large sample sizes of representative data for 308 

model training (i.e., homogeneous with the out-of-sample, test data). Additionally, on average, 309 

in-sample prediction accuracy cannot be less than out-of-sample prediction accuracy, and both 310 

will converge on the true reducible error with increasing βcausal and N and decreasing P (Fig. 311 

3). Recovery of similar in-sample and out-of-sample R 2 is consistent with minimal overfitting, but 312 

could arise from model bias (e.g., choosing too simple of a model that has large bias but small 313 

variance; Lever et al. 2016, James et al. 2021). Similar out-of-sample R 2 from multiple, genuinely 314 

different analysis methods would be consistent with having minimized prediction error given the 315

safeguarding against misleading model results, reduced R2 values from cross-validated models could 327 

result in studies being less likely to be published or going into a lower profile journal (i.e., the “file 328 

drawer problem”; Csada et al. 1996, Low-D´ ecarie et al. 2014), suggesting the need for a shift in 329 

how researchers evaluate prediction results in the context of cross-validation. 330 

The conditions that allow for strong prediction, namely when βcausal and N are large and P331 

is small, are the same in which variable selection is possible (Figs. 2C & 3), though reliable out-332 

of-sample prediction did not necessarily depend on perfect variable selection (i.e., including all 333 

causal and excluding all non-causal variables; Fig. S2). A variable selection trade-off emerged for 334 

the data sets in which variable selection was most difficult (e.g., βcausal = 0.1), as evidenced by a 335 

negative relationship between true positive and true negative rates (Fig. 4). This result has broad 336 

implications because effect sizes are expected to be small and diffuse for many biological systems 337 

(e.g., in genetics, Boyle et al. 2017). Moreover, the consequences of different variable selection 338 

errors will have disparate repercussions in exploratory versus diagnostic settings, so researchers 339 

will need to weigh the costs and benefits of either identifying all causal variables at the expense 340 

of including some false positives (e.g., when developing candidate variables for further study) or 341 

missing some causal variables to ensure the absence of any false positives (e.g., when identifying 342 

biomarkers for disease detection). In other words, best statistical practices, including when using 343 

sparse models, rely on identifying whether an analysis is hypothesis generating or hypothesis testing 344 

prior to implementation (Tredennick et al. 2021). For the Bayesian methods that generate posterior 345 

inclusion probabilities (PIPs), the threshold for deciding whether or not to include a variable may 346 

vary across disciplines and fields. In evolutionary genetics, researchers may choose to only consider 347 

genetic loci that have a PIP > 0.1 (Lucas et al. 2018, McFarlane & Pemberton 2021), and this 348 

simple choice would have substantially improved variable selection for BSLMM and SuSiE, but not 349 

BLASSO or Horseshoe, for one example scenario (Fig. S3). Overall, accurate variable selection 350 

requires large numbers of observations (Fig. 5), perhaps even more so than prediction, as has been 351 

found previously in trait mapping and phenotypic prediction (Wray et al. 2013, Gompert et al. 352 

2017). 353 

One striking feature of our results was the absence of a single method that excelled at all 354 

modeling purposes, consistent with the “no free lunch theorem” for supervised learning (Wolpert 355 

1996, Wolpert & Macready 1997). Trade-offs in model building have long been recognized (Levins 356 

1966, James et al. 2021, Tredennick et al. 2021) and serve as an important reminder for researchers 357 

to wield methods that align with their research objectives. Consequently, it can be useful to 358 

simulate data and measure the correlation (and other measures of the relationship) of the response 359 

variable with process parameters ( βcausal ) under relevant sample sizes, so as to gauge information 360 

about the expected reducible error. It may be the case that researchers will need to employ 361 

multiple, complementary statistical learning methods for questions involving both prediction and 362

## 5 Glossary 376 

• Bias-variance tradeoff: model fitting can be highly flexible and responsive (the variance 377 

part) or insensitive (the bias part) to data that are used for statistical learning. These two 378 

attributes are inversely related to one another and exhibit a trade-off (James et al. 2021). 379 

• Causal relationship: one process or measure affects another, directing or influencing a 380 

response. This is in contrast to associations between measures that arise from sources other 381 

than causality (spurious or confounded associations, etc.). 382 

• Covariates ( P ): measures of interest that can be used as potential explanatory variables 383 

for a response variable (e.g., many climate variables as covariates in predicting survivorship). 384 

• Cross-validation: a scheme by which fractions of the data are iteratively left out during 385 

the training (fitting) stage of model-building. The left out data are used as a gauge to avoid 386 

having the model fit the training data excessively well, while having a poor fit to left out data 387 

(i.e., guard against over-fitting). 388 

• Generalizability: a goal in science to obtain explanations and models that apply to broader 389 

contexts beyond a narrow scope of an individual sampling unit, study, population, etc. (please 390 

note that other studies instead call this transferability; e.g. Spake et al. 2022). While gener-391 

alizability is an aspiration, whether it is achievable or not is an open question and a scientific 392 

result itself. 393 

• Generative process: the production of a measurable system feature by biological, chemical, 394 

and physical phenomena, which generate or cause the result with some level of determinism. 395 

• High-dimensional data: herein we are drawing attention to data sets with many covariates 396 

(dimensions), potentially providing sufficient information to predict the response variable, but 397 

also raising challenges we describe. 398 

• In-sample or out-of-sample prediction: model fit can be quantified using the same 399 

samples that were used for model-fitting as is done commonly in statistics (in-sample; using 400 

R2, residuals, information criteria like AIC, etc.), or a held-out set of samples (out-of-sample). 401

• Inference: an understanding based on evidence, reasoning, or both; in this context, an 402 

understanding of relationships based on the association between the parameters and the 403 

response variable of interest. 404 

• Irreducible error: excess variability in predictions beyond what can be accounted for by 405 

causal processes (James et al. 2021). 406 

• Machine learning: statistical algorithms that use large sets of data to recognize patterns, 407 

adjust models, and make predictions of additional, out-of-sample data. 408 

• Overfitting: a phenomenon in which a model places too much weight on variables that are 409 

not related to causal processes, thus limiting the model’s potential for out-of-sample prediction 410 

(i.e. poor generalizability; James et al. 2021). 411 

• Precision: the fraction of selected variables that are truly causal. 412 

• Reducible error: variation in the response variable that can be truly attributed to causal 413 

parameters, in contrast to variation that arises due to extraneous causes (see Irreducible 414 

error). 415 

• Root mean squared error (RMSE): a commonly used metric for estimating how far 416 

response parameter estimates deviate from the true value (larger values indicate more error). 417 

• Sample size ( N ): the number of units of observation that are available to estimate nu-418 

meric relationships between the response variable and covariates in support of inference and 419 

prediction. 420 

• Sensitivity: the fraction of causal parameters that are accurately identified by the model 421 

(i.e., the true positive or recall rate). 422 

• Sparse modeling: a statistical framework in which a relatively small proportion of covariates 423 

is assumed to have a strong relationship with the response variable (Hastie et al. 2015). 424 

• Specificity: the number of non-causal parameters that are accurately excluded by the model 425 

(i.e., the true negative rate). 426 

• Statistical learning: understanding data using computational methods (James et al. 2021). 427 

• Variable (or feature) selection: the identification of a subset of predictors that are asso-428 

ciated with variation in the response variable. 429

Nine methods were con-sidered, as well as two different implementations of Ridge and LASSO in the glmnet and monomvn 

packages in R. Metrics for BLASSO and Horseshoe were calculated two ways: model-averaged (ma) estimates are based on all samples from the reversible jump MCMC, whereas non-zero (nz) estimates use only samples in which the variable and associated coefficient were included in the model. (A) In-sample and (B) out-of-sample prediction were evaluated with R 2 between the actual and predicted values of the response. In these panels, the grey and white regions represent the mean reducible and irreducible error, respectively, across all scenarios within a βcausal level. While reducible error represents the expected maximum value for out-of-sample prediction, in-sample prediction can exceed the reducible error when too flexible models are employed (i.e., overfitting). This means that the target for prediction is to recover a model with in-sample and out-of-sample R2 equal to the maximum reducible error. (C) Variable selection was evaluated using F 1, which is the harmonic mean of precision (i.e., the fraction of selected variables that are truly causal) and sensitivity (i.e., true positive rate): 2×P recision ×Sensitivity P recision +Sensitivity . F 1 was only calculated for analyses that can return truly sparse parameter estimates (i.e., β = 0; BSLMM, Elastic Net, LASSO, Spike-and-slab) or importance values (Random Forest). (D) Parameter estimation was evaluated for all methods except Random Forest using the root mean square error (RMSE) between estimated and actual parameter values. (E) Model speed was evaluated based on the natural log of runtime in seconds. Each circle represents the median value from 100 replicate simulations. 18

Figure 4: Variable selection performance varied greatly across scenarios, and was only possible for some methods when there were many observations ( N ), few variables ( P ), and effect sizes (βcausal ) were large. A negative correlation between true positive rate and true negative rate emerged for many simulations, especially when βcausal was small, indicative of a trade-off between identifying causal variables (sensitivity) and excluding non-causal variables (specificity). This trade-off disappears when conditions are more favorable for variable selection: when βcausal and N are large and when P is small. Variable selection was only evaluated for analyses that can return truly sparse parameter estimates (i.e., β = 0; BSLMM, Elastic Net, LASSO, Spike-and-slab) or importance values (Random Forest).
